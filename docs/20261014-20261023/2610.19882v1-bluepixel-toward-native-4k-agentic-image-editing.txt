BluePixel: Toward Native 4K Agentic Image Editing

Abstract
Instruction-driven image editing has reached native 4K, yet how such editors are trained remains
undisclosed, and pretraining one from scratch is beyond most teams. The task itself compounds
the difficulty: a single end-to-end editor cannot make precise parametric adjustments, match
specialized experts, or interpret the vague instructions users actually write. We argue that 4K
editing is a system problem rather than a single-model problem, and present BluePixel, which
couples a native-4K editing model with an agent harness; the complete training recipe is
disclosed in this paper. The model makes 4K training practical: it turns a 1K-pretrained editor
into a native-4K one through progressive VAE adaptation that keeps 4K training as affordable
as 1K, absorbs a long tail of vertical editing skills through continual learning, and refines the
aesthetic–fidelity trade-off with reinforcement learning. Distillation via source-anchored target pro-
jection (SATP) then compresses 50 denoising steps to 4, completing a 4K edit in 8.7 s on a single
Kunlunxin P800 XPU while preserving task success rate and background consistency. The har-
ness, BluePixel Agent, makes editing controllable: it decomposes user instructions into specialist
and general operations, verifies every result with the same reward model that guided training,
and retries within bounded attempts. On VINS-4KEval++ (3,305 native-4K cases), BluePixel
ranks second among seven models, behind only GPT Image 2 in non-edited-region consistency.

Introduction
Diffusion models [15, 41, 45] have brought photorealistic image synthesis and instruction-driven editing [7,
26] to production scale, and output resolution keeps rising: closed-source models went from 1K to 2K and
then to 4K, and editing has now reached native 4K as well. A straightforward idea is to cascade a 1K editor
with a super-resolution or repainting model [31, 56]; the cascade, however, introduces structural artifacts at
the upsampling boundary (ghosting, texture repetition, and loss of fine-grained control) that professional
workflows cannot accept, as shown in [11]. VINS-120K [11] first proposed post-training a 1K-pretrained
editor directly into a native-4K editing model, and introduced an ultra-high-resolution editing dataset to
make this possible. More recently, open-source releases such as FLUX.3 Image [5] and SenseNova-U1.5 [48]
have followed the move to 4K, but their training recipes remain undisclosed. This paper starts from two
questions. How can an efficient native-4K editing model be trained with limited resources? And is editing
fundamentally a single-model problem, or should it be solved by an agent framework?

The second question goes deeper than resolution. The dominant paradigm treats editing as a single end-to-
end model: one network takes an image and an instruction and returns the edited result [7, 38, 50]. This
formulation conflates heterogeneous capabilities into one set of weights, and in practice it falls short in
three ways. It cannot make precise, parametric adjustments: an exact brightness step or a specific color
grade comes back only as an approximation. On task types such as text rendering, teeth retouching, and
hairstyle editing, it trails dedicated expert models. And it offers no principled way to act on the short, vague
instructions that users actually write (“make this photo look more professional”).
We answer both questions with BluePixel, whose complete training recipe is disclosed in this paper; Figure 1
previews its editing capabilities. First, the training framework is efficient: it builds on a 1K-pretrained editor
and never pretrains at 4K. A progressive VAE adaptation recipe completes the jump to native 4K (4K-in,
4K-out, no cascaded super-resolution) in four days on 32 Kunlunxin P800 XPUs, continual learning with
ACE-LoRA [36] and MOPD [29] absorbs a long tail of vertical editing skills, and reinforcement learning
refines the aesthetic–fidelity trade-off. Second, the resulting model is fast: the high-compression VAE keeps
the DiT-stage token count of a native-4K edit at the level of a 1K edit, and distillation via source-anchored
target projection (SATP) then compresses 50 denoising steps to 4, enough for interactive multi-turn editing.
Third, we treat editing as an agent framework rather than a single model: BluePixel Agent separates intent
analysis from editing, composes specialist and general-purpose tools, and uses reward-based feedback to
retry or select among candidate images; persisted image provenance supports multi-turn refinement. A
data pipeline (a VLM-based quality gate and a four-dimensional reward model) holds all three to the same
quality standard.
The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 surveys related work. Section 3 presents
BluePixel, covering both the native 4K training recipe and the agentic editing harness. Section 4 presents
data construction and quality assessment. Section 5 presents experiments, and Section 6 concludes.

Related Work
Instruction-driven image editing.
InstructPix2Pix [7] established instruction-conditioned editing, and sub-
sequent work improved control through test-time optimization [26, 38], larger training data [50], posterior
sampling [53], hybrid prompt inversion [18], and semantic intent understanding [12, 68]. Industry models
such as HiDream-I1 [9] have since carried instruction editing to production scale, and their resolution is
climbing accordingly: Seedream 4.0/5.0 [8, 47], Qwen-Image [51], and ChatGPT-Image [39] already gener-
ate 4K images; recent open-source releases such as FLUX.3 Image [5] and SenseNova-U1.5 [48] have fol-
lowed suit, though their training recipes remain undisclosed. The academic line, meanwhile, still operates
at 1024 × 1024 or below, and all of these editors keep the same shape: a single end-to-end network that takes
an image and an instruction and returns the result. BluePixel keeps this interface and lifts it to native 4K,
with its training recipe fully described in Section 3.1.
Training at native 4K.
Scaling training to native 4K poses two resource problems.
On the data side,
UltraHR-100K [70] and VINS-120K [11] provide large-scale high-resolution editing pairs, and learnable pos-
terior sampling [2] advances high-resolution restoration. On the architecture side, a standard f =8 VAE
turns a 4096 × 4096 frame into a latent sequence that makes training intractable, and the common alterna-
tive, cascading a 1K editor with super-resolution [31, 56], avoids that training cost but introduces structural
artifacts (ghosting, texture repetition, and loss of fine-grained control). BluePixel resolves the architecture
half: it replaces the standard VAE with DCAE-F64 [10] (f =64 vs. f =8), cutting the 4K token count by 16×,
to the level of a 1K edit, so that fine-tuning becomes tractable, and it targets editing rather than generation,
which additionally demands instruction fidelity and non-edited-region consistency.
Agentic image editing.
Agentic systems delegate task steps to tools under a language-model orchestra-
tor [25], a paradigm established in text and code [40, 46, 49, 55, 61] and recently extended to visual creation
and editing [19–21, 30, 32, 42, 43, 54, 57, 63, 66, 72]. BluePixel Agent follows this line but changes what
the loop verifies and what the tools are: turn-level routing and persistent image provenance are coupled to
a structured editing pipeline that delegates text, tone, character effects, and general edits to different tools,
3


and a reward model judges every candidate, driving local retries and final selection rather than serving only
as a post hoc quality report.
3
BluePixel
In this paper, we aim to address two challenges: making native-4K editing practical (efficient training and
fast inference with limited resources), and making editing more controllable than a single model can be
(structured tool use and feedback). BluePixel is a native 4K agentic image editing model built around these
two goals. Its training recipe (Section 3.1) turns a 1K-pretrained editor into a native-4K editor with 4-step
inference, and its agentic editing harness (Section 3.2) combines routing, composable editing tools, reward-
guided retries, and candidate selection.
3.1
Efficient Native 4K Training
Overview.
Native 4K image editing poses two structural challenges. First, token explosion: a standard
f =8 VAE compresses a 4096×4096 frame into a latent sequence so long that training and inference become
prohibitively expensive for diffusion transformers (DiTs). Second, data scarcity: high-quality 4K editing pairs
are rare and costly to produce, so the data volume needed to train a 4K model from scratch is difficult to
assemble. BluePixel addresses both with a three-phase training recipe. The recipe is general: it upgrades any
pretrained low-resolution editor into a native 4K editor and does not assume a particular base model. For
concreteness, we present it on FLUX.2 [klein] [4], the 1K-pretrained editor we build on:
• Phase 1: Preparation. Replace the f =8 VAE with DCAE-F64 [10] (f =64) and adapt the DiT via rep-
resentation alignment plus light fine-tuning to obtain an initial 4K model at low cost (∼200K samples,
32 Kunlunxin P800 XPUs for 4 days).
• Phase 2: Enhancement. Progressively improve the model through (i) progressive quality supervised
fine-tuning (SFT) at 1024×1024 then 4096×4096, (ii) benchmark-driven fine-tuning with data rebalanc-
ing and model merging, (iii) continual learning [36] for vertical editing skills, and (iv) reinforcement
learning with DiffusionNFT [73] for aesthetics and instruction fidelity.
• Phase 3: Acceleration. Compress the 50-step teacher into a 4-step student via source-anchored target
projection (SATP) step distillation for production latency.

3.1.2
Phase 2: Enhancement — Quality and Capability Refinement
Progressive quality SFT.
With the initial 4K model from Phase 1, we now train the model further on high-
quality editing data. Although Phase 1 aligns the representations as closely as possible, the adaptation
process inevitably causes some capability degradation or forgetting in the original model, so the Phase 1
checkpoint provides a good initialization but limited editing quality.

3.1
Efficient Native 4K Training
We adopt a progressive resolution schedule: training first continues at 1024 × 1024, where data is abundant
and training is efficient; then transitions to native 4096 × 4096 for the main fine-tuning phase, which is nec-
essary because the high compression ratio causes a quality ceiling at 1K (e.g. persistently degraded face
rendering) that only native 4K fine-tuning can break through. At each resolution, the input image is resized
to a target area while preserving aspect ratio and aligned to a multiple of the VAE scale factor. This curricu-
lum enables the DiT to gradually recover and exceed its pre-adaptation quality. During the early stage of
training, we keep the data composition across task types approximately balanced to ensure broad coverage.
Once the model approaches convergence, we begin dynamically adjusting the data ratio based on model
performance: after each training epoch, the model is evaluated on an internal benchmark, failure modes
are identified, and the data composition is rebalanced to target them. Finally, we merge checkpoints from
multiple epochs via model merging to obtain a single stable model that is more robust to data distribution
shifts than any individual checkpoint. This merged model is the base for downstream specialization.
Continual learning with ACE-LoRA and MOPD.
Real-world editing involves a long tail of specialized
needs. These needs share two characteristics: task-specific data is scarce and hard to collect, and the needs
themselves are numerous. Training on such data jointly with the main SFT stage risks data dilution, which
makes the specialized skills hard to learn well; learning each need separately with plain LoRA [23] adapta-
tion, in turn, invites task interference that drives the optimization toward inferior solutions and catastrophic
forgetting that erodes the base model’s general capabilities. We therefore adopt continual learning with
ACE-LoRA [36], which applies the gradient-orthogonal principle [35] to image editing, followed by multi-
teacher on-policy distillation (MOPD) [29] to consolidate the acquired knowledge back into the base model;
we refer to this pair as ACE-MOPD, which makes long-sequence learning practical.
Specifically, the vertical skills are defined as many fine-grained templates, each template constituting one
editing task; the templates are organized into groups of seven to nine, and each group is learned with ACE-
LoRA as a two-stage gradient-orthogonal LoRA procedure: stage 1 fine-tunes the model on the group’s
templates, and stage 2 continues training with gradients projected onto the orthogonal complement of the
subspace spanned by earlier groups’ LoRAs, suppressing interference with previously learned groups. The
learned LoRAs of each group then serve as teachers for one round of MOPD, which transfers the group’s
skills back into the base model before training moves on to the next group; the base model therefore accu-
mulates skills group by group while the adapter count stays bounded. We assess final-task performance
on held-out suites rather than assuming that the orthogonality constraint alone prevents forgetting. In
principle, the adaptation–consolidation cycle can run for an unbounded number of rounds, continuously
absorbing new task groups into the base model.
RL for improving aesthetics and instruction fidelity.
We further refine the model with reinforcement learn-
ing in two stages, targeting aesthetics and editing fidelity respectively. Both stages build on Diffusion-
NFT [73], a likelihood-free policy optimization method that operates directly on the flow matching forward
process. In the first stage, we apply DiffusionNFT with PickScore [27], a text-to-image preference reward,
on text-to-image (T2I) tasks: aesthetics improve, but editing capability degrades, because the T2I reward
favors visual appeal without accounting for instruction adherence or edit locality. We therefore continue
training with BluePixReward R(·), our four-dimensional edit quality evaluator (detailed in Section 4.3), on
image-to-image (I2I) editing tasks, which improves editing performance. To improve training efficiency
and effectiveness, we filter out prompts whose sampled group has low reward variance, as these provide
negligible gradient signal for policy improvement.

Agentic Editing Harness
Overview.
The 4K diffusion model from Section 3.1 provides the visual substrate, but it remains a single
model: one network takes an image and an instruction and returns one edited result. As editing tasks grow

Agentic Editing Harness
more compositional (multi-turn refinement, region-disjoint edits, and reference-guided stylization), a single
model reaches a capability ceiling that additional training data alone cannot resolve. BluePixel addresses
this by wrapping the 4K model inside an agentic editing harness (BluePixel Agent) that decomposes an editing
request into a routed sequence of composable skills with closed-loop verification. The design follows two
principles: (i) composability: editing is performed by a registry of specialized, independently replaceable
skills rather than a single monolithic model; and (ii) closed-loop verification: every edit is evaluated by a
quality module before being returned, and failures trigger automatic re-editing instead of silent acceptance.
3.2.1
System Architecture
BluePixel Agent organizes editing around three complementary functions:
1. Request interpretation. A router considers the user’s instruction together with the current image
and conversation context. It determines whether there is enough information to proceed with an edit
or whether the turn instead calls for clarification, suggestions, or assessment of an existing result.
For an actionable edit, an intent analyst formulates the operations to be performed and may gather
supporting information for underspecified references. For example, when a user names a visual style
without describing its defining features, the analyst may use web search to identify relevant visual
attributes before specifying the edit.
2. Skill execution. The harness applies the relevant editing skills to these operations (Table 2). Spe-
cialized tools handle constrained tasks such as color grading and template-guided editing, while the
general-purpose editor handles open-ended changes to image content.
3. Feedback and selection. The resulting images are assessed against the instruction and source image.
This feedback drives further editing when needed and the final candidate selection (Section 3.2.3).
Some edit requests depend on facts beyond the source image, such as attributes of a named character or a
time-sensitive reference. In these cases, the intent analyst can use web search to gather relevant information
and incorporate it into the editing instruction. Figure 3 illustrates this capability with character-specific
visual effects and dialogue, and with a zodiac-themed toy requested by reference to the current year.
These functions separate decisions about what should be edited, how the edit is carried out, and how its
outcome is assessed. The harness also retains conversational constraints and image provenance across turns,
allowing users to refine a selected result or branch from an earlier version. This continuity connects hetero-
geneous editing skills within a service rather than treating each request as an isolated model call.
The advantage of the skill-based approach is illustrated in Figure 4. When a user requests a specific filter look
(a task with strong professional aesthetic orientation), a pure diffusion model tends to generate a plausible
but imprecise approximation. BluePixel Agent, by contrast, can use LUT-based color grading to apply a
calibrated color transform that matches the intended look. This is a direct consequence of the composable
skill design: tasks that have well-defined technical solutions are handled by dedicated tools, while the
primary editing model is reserved for generative edits that truly require it. We next describe the skill-based
editing repertoire and the feedback process used to assess its outputs.
3.2.2
Skill-Based Editing
Production-grade image editing spans a wider range of operations than a single image-to-image transforma-
tion. A request may call for global stylization, localized changes, a calibrated color treatment, or a template-
guided transformation; these operations place different demands on how an image is modified. BluePixel
Agent therefore treats the 4K diffusion editor as one capability within a broader skill repertoire rather than
the sole mechanism for every edit.

tailored to particular editing domains, with task-specific controls that can be used independently when a
request falls within their scope. When an instruction combines such an operation with open-ended content
changes, the harness can coordinate the specialist skill with the primary editor. Auxiliary capabilities interpret

3.2.3
Closed-Loop Editing
Editing in BluePixel Agent is not an open-loop chain that emits a single result and trusts it; it is a closed-loop
process: edit, verify, and re-edit if necessary. An edited image is treated as a candidate whose assessment
informs both subsequent actions and the final result.
Once the requested edit and its constraints have been specified, the image editor executes the relevant skill
and produces a candidate. The quality evaluator receives the source image, candidate, and editing instruction,
and uses BluePixReward, our VLM-based judge (Section 4.3), to assess instruction following, non-edited
region consistency, and visual quality. It returns scores and diagnostic feedback that the harness can use to
decide what to do next.
The evaluation is actionable rather than merely descriptive. If a specialist edit leaves part of the request
unmet, the harness can retry the same skill and, if needed, carry the unmet requirement into a general
edit. For a generative edit, the diagnostic feedback can inform a revised instruction for another attempt.
Recovery attempts are bounded, and the harness retains intermediate candidates rather than assuming that
the latest edit is the best. It selects the final image by assessing the candidates against the complete request,
so feedback governs both whether editing continues and which result is returned.

Data Construction and Quality Assessment
4.1
Overview
Our training data combines publicly available editing datasets, including UnicEdit-10M [62], TextEdit [52],
X2Edit [37], and SpatialEdit [59], with in-house annotated pairs. Because Phase 1 yields a good initializa-
tion, we adopt a quality-over-quantity strategy: rather than scaling data volume, we focus on filtering for
visual quality, aesthetics, information density, and edit accuracy. The pipeline applies two quality gates:
an image quality filter that discards pairs failing visual quality checks, and an edit evaluation reward model
(BluePixReward, detailed in Section 4.3) that scores each surviving triple (source, edited, instruction) on
instruction following, non-edited region consistency, visual quality, and overall. The same quality standard
governs both training-time filtering and evaluation. We construct 1K and 4K datasets, reorganized into eight
task types: Spatial Editing, Text Editing, Portrait Editing, Object Modification, Background & Style Editing,
Object Addition, Object Removal, and Multi-step Editing (Figure 5).

4.2
Image Quality Filtering
VINS-120K [11] establishes a multi-stage filtering paradigm for high-resolution editing data: preliminary file
checks, image quality filtering via hand-crafted perceptual operators (sharpness, exposure, color, and tex-
ture statistics), instruction-following filtering via VLM parsing combined with object detection and segmen-
tation, and aesthetic assessment via dedicated predictors; each stage relies on a manually tuned threshold,
and in VINS-120K only the top 20% of samples survive the full pipeline. We adopt the same joint filtering
principle (if either the source or the edited image fails any quality check, the pair is discarded), but observe
that per-operator threshold tuning is inherently subjective, does not generalize across data distributions,
and requires maintaining a large stack of independent operators with their own failure modes.
To eliminate this subjectivity, we train a single VLM based on Qwen3-VL-8B [44] that takes the image as
input and directly outputs scores for multiple quality metrics along with a reasoned analysis, producing
its own retain-or-discard judgment. The model evaluates five axes that capture complementary aspects of
image quality: aesthetics (composition, lighting, and color harmony), visual clarity (image sharpness and arti-
facts), saturation (color vividness and richness), information density (texture, structural details, and semantic
complexity), and watermark presence. We then filter images based solely on the model’s judgment, replacing
the brittle stack of hand-tuned thresholds with a single, learned quality gate. We pay particular attention to
information density: high-density data, comprising images rich in texture, structure, and semantic content,
can accelerate convergence, as each sample exposes the model to more diverse visual patterns.
4.3
Edit Evaluation Reward Model
Beyond general image quality, we need to verify that each edit pair is correct: the edited image must faith-
fully follow the instruction without introducing artifacts or damaging non-edited regions. To this end, we
train a dedicated edit evaluation reward model, BluePixReward. Expert models are first trained on dif-
ferent editing tasks, and multi-teacher on-policy distillation (MOPD) [29] then distills them into a single
judge built on Qwen3.5-9B; it takes the source image, edited image, and instruction as a triple and outputs
four-dimensional scores2:
• Instruction Following (IF): whether the edit accurately executes the instruction, penalizing wrong edit
type, missing or incomplete edits, extraneous modifications, and person-related errors.
• Non-Edited Region Consistency (NC): requires a reasoning step that first localizes the edit region,

• Visual Quality (VQ): naturalness and fusion quality of the edited region and overall image, penalizing
fusion seams, material/lighting mismatches, inpainting artifacts, person defects, and severe distortion
across the whole image.
• Overall: a composite quality score reported alongside the three component scores. The evaluator also
provides a binary success decision: an output counts as successful when IF ≥9, NC ≥8, and VQ ≥6,
based on its assessment of instruction following, non-edited-region consistency, and visual quality.
This decision is reported as the Success column in Table 8.
The scores are calibrated against human judgment: Pearson correlations with expert Likert ratings on a 150-
pair validation set are r = 0.82 (IF), r = 0.78 (NC), r = 0.76 (VQ), and r = 0.84 (Overall). This reward model
serves as the unified quality signal throughout the pipeline: low-scoring pairs are discarded from training
data; multiple candidates for the same instruction are scored jointly as a group, providing the reward signal
for DiffusionNFT; the same model also serves as the benchmark evaluator for VINS-4KEval++ (Table 8, the
BluePixReward protocol), whose four-dimensional scores serve as the primary evaluation metrics, closing
the quality loop.
4.4
4K Data Construction
Similar to VINS-120K [11], we source 4K pairs from two complementary streams, with the majority com-
ing from video mining. The first uses a dedicated pipeline that mines video footage for frame pairs that
can serve as editing data, selecting frames where the visual difference between two shots constitutes a
plausible edit, and generating corresponding instructions via prompt rewriting. The second applies super-
resolution to a subset of 1K source images. Before upscaling, each candidate is screened by MANIQA [60],
a no-reference image-quality model used here for sharpness screening: the image is cropped into 224 × 224
patches, each patch is scored with a Vision Transformer (ViT), and the patch scores are aggregated; upscaling
a blurry 1K image produces artifact-laden 4K results, so only sufficiently sharp images proceed. All 4K pairs
pass through the same image quality filtering and edit evaluation reward model as their 1K counterparts.

Benchmarks.
We evaluate BluePixel on four benchmarks. ImgEdit-Bench [64] provides broad task cov-

more diverse scenes and instructions, termed VINS-4KEval++, comprising 3,305 evaluation cases. Finally,
we use CIE-Bench [36] to evaluate the continual learning performance (as introduced in Section 3.1.2).

5.1.2
Model Analysis
Beyond the end-to-end comparison, we analyze the two components that distinguish BluePixel from a plain
editing model: the agent harness at inference and continual learning with ACE-LoRA and MOPD in train-
ing.
Is the agent harness effective?
Figure 8 isolates the contribution of the agent harness by contrasting the
backbone editor with BluePixel Agent on the same instructions. In the facial-expression example, the back-
bone changes the expression in the right direction but overshoots, producing a stiff, grinning look rather
than the requested relaxed and serene one; the agent’s verification loop catches the mismatch, retries, and
returns a calm expression that matches the instruction. In the text example, the backbone ignores half of the
request: the word is rendered as garbled letterforms (“COEFAE”) in the wrong color and font, whereas the
agent produces “Decaf” in bold red sans-serif without disturbing the surrounding sign. In the mask-repair
example, the backbone leaves the crack clearly visible across the mask’s forehead, whereas the agent repairs
the fracture and restores a continuous metallic texture. Across all three cases the backbone delivers an ap-
proximate one-shot result, and the agent converges to an output that actually satisfies the instruction, which
is precisely what the verification-and-retry loop is for.

Conclusion

recipe that makes native-4K editing practical on limited resources: progressive VAE adaptation through

consolidation absorbs a long tail of vertical editing skills with limited forgetting, and reinforcement learn-
ing with the purpose-trained BluePixReward refines the aesthetic–fidelity trade-off. Distillation via source-
anchored target projection (SATP) then compresses the 50-step teacher to 4 steps, completing a 4K edit in 8.7 s

The BluePixel Team
BluePixel was developed through the collective efforts of the following team members and collaborators.
Research & Algorithm.
Shanyan Guan led the BluePixel project and designed the overall framework, core
algorithms, data construction, and evaluation pipeline. Shiyu Li developed the BluePixel Agent harness.
Xin Li contributed to data construction, training instruction refinement, and instruction recommendation,
and developed the reward model for editing evaluation. Zhizhou Chen validated the initial training algo-
rithm. Yuehao Liu developed the continual learning algorithm for acquiring multiple editing capabilities.
Yunzhe Xu developed the distillation pipeline. Ruixue Shen developed the data construction pipeline and
explored DPO-based post-training.
Chao Ma (Shanghai Jiao Tong University) and Ying Tai (Nanjing University) contributed as long-term re-
search collaborators. Chao Ma, Ying Tai, Zhongang Qi, Wei Li, and Yanhao Ge provided valuable guidance
and support throughout the project.

Min Jin, Junbo Liu, Qiang Liu, Penggang Shi, Sheng Sun, Xiao Wang, and Yong Zhang devel-
