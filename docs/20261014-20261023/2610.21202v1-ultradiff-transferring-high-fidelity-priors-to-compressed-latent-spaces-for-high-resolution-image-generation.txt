Abstract
High-resolution synthesis with Latent Diffusion Models (LDMs) faces a funda-
mental trade-off between computational cost and generative fidelity. This trade-off
is largely governed by the latent compression factor f (e.g. f8: f=8 and f32:
f=32). While aggressive compression (f32) significantly improves efficiency,
it suffers from severe information loss and ambiguity compared to standard f8
models. We propose UltraDiff, a framework that reformulates this challenge as
joint-latent diffusion learning followed by marginal score distillation. We introduce
MixTeacher, a dual-branch DiT that models the joint f8-f32 distribution, utilizing
shared attention to resolve the ambiguity of the compressed space with high-fidelity
f8 priors. Crucially, we propose vector field distillation in the native f32 space.
Theoretically, this compels the student to learn the conditional expectation of the
teacher’s trajectory, effectively filtering out compression artifacts. Across the eval-
uated 1K–4K settings, UltraDiff approaches f8-level quality while preserving f32
efficiency. With 20 sampling steps on a single A100 GPU, it generates a 2K image
in 6.2 seconds—over 40× faster than our f8 baseline.

Introduction
The demand for ultra-high-definition (UHD) image and video synthesis has surged across streaming,
gaming, and VR applications. While recent Diffusion Transformers (DiTs) [Esser et al., 2024, Chen
et al., 2023, Podell et al., 2023, Black Forest Labs, 2024] excel at high-fidelity generation, self-
attention dominates their compute and scales quadratically with the token count (O(N 2)), making
UHD synthesis prohibitively expensive. To mitigate this, recent works [Team, 2025, Xie et al., 2024]
explore VAEs with more aggressive spatial downsampling, i.e., increasing the latent downsampling
factor from f8 (f=8) to f32 (f=32), reducing the 2D latent token count by 16× compared to a
standard f8 VAE, offering substantial efficiency gains.
However, the efficiency of aggressive downsampling comes at the cost of a substantially harder
diffusion problem [Skorokhodov et al., 2025]. With f32 compression, the diffusion model must
synthesize complex scenes from far fewer spatial tokens, yielding an ambiguous target distribution
for the diffusion model to learn. This makes it difficult for the model to capture fine-grained details
and precise spatial layout, leading to degraded perceptual fidelity and weaker prompt adherence
compared to f8-based models [Black Forest Labs, 2024, Li et al., 2024, Kong et al., 2024].
Given the superior fidelity and detail generation capacity of f8 models, a natural question arises: Can
we transfer the rich generative knowledge of a pre-trained f8 model to guide the training of an
efficient f32 model? To study this, we conduct a controlled comparison between an f32 DiT trained

To address these challenges for efficient UHD synthesis, we propose UltraDiff, a new framework
that learns diffusion in a joint f8–f32 latent space and then distills the resulting score to the f32 latent
space, yielding the final diffusion model in the f32 marginal for efficient sampling. Specifically,
instead of treating the diffusion modeling in f8 and f32 spaces as two separate tasks, we introduce
MixTeacher, a dual-branch DiT that models the joint distribution p(xf8, xf32) where xf8 and xf32
are extracted from the same image. Architecturally, MixTeacher comprises two DiT branches with
separate parameters to accommodate the distinct feature distributions in the f8 and f32 latent spaces,
while sharing self-attention layers within each block to enable dense cross-space interaction. With f8
initialization and layer-wise attention sharing, MixTeacher effectively learns a joint denoising model
over paired f8 and f32 latents, effectively transferring generative knowledge from the pre-trained f8
branch to the f32 branch. Crucially, unlike other implicit feature distillation methods [Leng et al.,
2025, Ren et al., 2025a], it enables direct distilling supervision in the f32 space. Specifically, our
teacher projects its high-fidelity guidance into the student’s f32 space. Theoretically, this forces
the student to learn the marginal vector field, effectively approximating the conditional expectation
of the high-fidelity trajectory. This process “integrates out” the ambiguity inherent to compressed
generation, guiding the student toward structurally coherent manifolds while operating strictly within
the efficient f32 latent space.
Our contributions are summarized as follows:
• We introduce MixTeacher, a unified architecture that learns the joint score function of f8
and f32 latents. Through a stochastic routing strategy and shared attention mechanism, it
captures cross-space correlations while maintaining the marginal generation capabilities of
each branch.
• We develop a marginal score distillation strategy that aligns the vector field directly in
the native f32 space. By learning the conditional expectation of the teacher’s trajectory,
the student model effectively resolves the inherent ambiguity and difficulty of modeling
complex dependencies in the compressed f32 space.
• Our method, UltraDiff, achieves a strong trade-off between quality and efficiency. It pro-
duces detail-rich 4K images with strong semantic alignment shown in Figure 1, approaches
the fidelity of the evaluated f8 systems, and generates a 2K image in 6.2 seconds using 20
sampling steps on a single NVIDIA A100 GPU—40.3× faster than our f8 anchor. UltraDiff
also scales to 4K generation, where the f8 anchor runs out of memory.

Most systems use an 8× spatial compression factor and often reduce the token count further with
2 × 2 patch embedding [Podell et al., 2023, Chen et al., 2023, 2024, Li et al., 2024, Esser et al., 2024,

Black Forest Labs, 2024]. More aggressive compression, such as f = 32, improves efficiency for
high-resolution and long-sequence generation [Xie et al., 2024, HaCohen et al., 2024, Team, 2025].
However, the smaller spatial grid makes structure and semantic alignment harder to learn [Xie et al.,
2024, HaCohen et al., 2024, Pernias et al., 2023]. UltraDiff addresses this problem by transferring
knowledge from a strong f8 model to an f32 model through initialization and cross-latent distillation,
followed by mixed-resolution training.
Recent work has also explored making one autoencoder latent space explicitly compatible with
another during VAE training [Yi et al., 2025, Zhao et al., 2024]. UltraDiff addresses a complementary
setting: our f8 and f32 VAEs are trained independently and then kept fixed, while their relationship is
learned at the diffusion vector-field level through shared attention in MixTeacher. Explicit compatible-
VAE training may further simplify cross-space transfer and is an interesting direction for future
work.
2.2
Diffusion Distillation
Distillation has become a general mechanism for adapting diffusion models to diverse objectives and
deployment constraints. For acceleration, progressive and distribution–matching distillation compress
long sampling trajectories into few- or even one-step generators while maintaining competitive fidelity
[Salimans and Ho, 2022, Yin et al., 2024a,b, Kang et al., 2024]. Related consistency and rectified-
flow formulations further enable fast sampling and have been applied in high-resolution restoration
pipelines [Xu et al., 2025, Sun et al., 2025, Wang et al., 2024b]. Beyond speed, distillation can change
the inference pattern or model architecture. For example, a bidirectional teacher can supervise a
causal student for streaming video generation [Yin et al., 2024c, Huang et al., 2025, Novack et al.,
2024, Shin et al., 2025, Kodaira et al., 2025]. Distillation also supports modality transfer and prior
reuse; score-distillation techniques leverage strong 2D text-to-image priors to supervise 3D synthesis,
with recent work addressing stability and mode collapse [Yu et al., 2023, Ma et al., 2024, Wang et al.,
2024a, 2025]. Another class of methods aligns semantic features from external encoders, such as
DINOv2 or other diffusion networks, with a model’s internal representations to accelerate training
and improve image fidelity [Yu et al., 2024, Leng et al., 2025, Ren et al., 2025a]. We adopt the
spirit of distillation to bridge a latent-scale mismatch: a strong f8 teacher guides an f32 DiT via
initialization and cross-latent supervision, yielding a high-compression student that retains fidelity
while benefiting from the computational savings of far fewer tokens.

Motivation: Feasibility of Cross-Space Transfer. Before detailing our framework, we first em-
pirically validate the hypothesis that the generative representations of f8 (high-fidelity) and f32
(high-efficiency) latent spaces share transferable structural patterns, despite their representation and
dimensional mismatch. We conducted a pilot study comparing a baseline f32 DiT model trained
from scratch against one initialized with parameters from a pre-trained f8 model (excluding latent-
dependent layers). As shown in Fig. 2, the f8-initialized model consistently outperforms the randomly
initialized baseline. This confirms that priors learned in the f8 space are transferable. However, a
persistent performance gap remains. This saturation suggests that static parameter initialization is
insufficient. This motivates our proposal of MixTeacher, which enables dynamic, interaction-based
knowledge transfer via joint latent modeling.
3.1
Joint-Latent Modeling via MixTeacher
To capture the correlation between high-frequency details (f8) and compact semantics (f32), we
introduce MixTeacher, a unified diffusion model parameterized by Φ. We consider the data as tuples
in a product space Xjoint = Xf8 × Xf32. The MixTeacher approximates the joint score function
∇log p(xf8, xf32). To provide reliable cross-space guidance, we design MixTeacher as a mixed-token
dual-branch DiT. As illustrated in Fig. 3, the f8 and f32 branches maintain separate parameters—
including QKV projections, FFNs, normalization layers, patch embeddings, and output heads—but
share the self-attention mechanism across tokens in each layer. This design enables cross-latent space
interaction while respecting the distinct distributions of each space.

4.1
Implementation Details
We employ a Diffusion Transformer (DiT) architecture that models text and image tokens as a unified
sequence. Specifically, text embeddings (via T5-XXL [Raffel et al., 2020]) and image latents are
concatenated along the sequence dimension and processed through a shared bidirectional transformer
backbone. We use a patch size of 1 for image embeddings. For latent encoding, we utilize high-
capacity VAEs with latent channel dimensions of Cf8 = 16 and Cf32 = 64, corresponding to 8×
and 32× downsampling factors, respectively. We apply channel-wise unit-variance normalization
to both f8 and f32 latents before they enter the DiT backbone. We first train a DiT in the f32 latent
space under two settings: (i) from scratch, and (ii) initialized from the f8 model’s weights, except
for the shape-dependent patch embedding and output heads, which are randomly initialized. For
MixTeacher, the f8 branch is initialized from a strong pretrained f8 T2I model, and the f32 branch is
initialized from the previously trained f32 model with f8 initialization. We train the teacher at 512
and 1024 resolutions with varying aspect ratios. All UltraDiff stages use 64 NVIDIA A100 GPUs
with FSDP. After filtering and deduplication, the available corpus contains approximately 0.37B
text–image pairs. Table 2 reports the optimization steps and aggregate compute for each stage. The
three stages consume 463.0 GPU-days in total, about 26% of the 1792 GPU-days required to train
our lower-quality f32 baseline directly. These costs treat the fixed f8/f32 VAEs and pretrained f8
anchor as available components; further optimization settings are provided in the appendix.

4.2
Ablation Study
Evaluation criteria. We evaluate models using GenEval [Ghosh et al., 2023], CLIP similarity [Rad-
ford et al., 2021], and ImageReward (IR) [Xu et al., 2023] at a resolution of 512 × 512 for fast
iteration. CLIP and IR are computed on a 3k subset of MJHQ-30k [Li et al., 2024] to further reduce
evaluation cost.
Train in f32 space. We train the T2I model in the f32 latent space for 120k iterations until convergence.
The results show that the model with f8 initialization exhibits much better performance than the
model trained from scratch. As shown in Figure 2, the model initialized from f8 (green curve)
consistently outperforms the scratch-trained model (blue curve) according to GenEval [Ghosh et al.,
2023] throughout training. Table 1 reports similar gains on CLIP and IR metrics, showing the benefit
of transferring parameters from the f8 model. However, simple initialization still leaves a large gap
compared with the f8 model, as shown in Figure 2 and Table 1. This suggests that, in the highly
compressed f32 space, good initialization helps but is not sufficient. Even with f8 initialization,
optimization remains difficult, and the model cannot fully match the f8 teacher under a purely data-
driven objective. Leveraging the strong f8 model, we distill its behavior to enhance f32 performance.
Exploration of MixTeacher Routing Strategy. To identify the optimal strategy for training the
MixTeacher, we evaluate three routing configurations in Table 5. First, exclusive joint training
(pjoint = 1.0) performs poorly; the lack of single-branch constraints causes the f32 branch to exploit
f8 shortcuts, leading to representation collapse. Second, mixing only Joint and f8-Solo modes (e.g.,
pjoint = 0.5, pf8 = 0.5) also fails, as the f32 branch remains under-trained, creating a bottleneck
that drags down the joint performance. These results underscore a critical insight: maintaining
the independent generative capability of each branch is a prerequisite for learning a robust joint
distribution. Consequently, we adopt a balanced configuration (pjoint = 0.4, pf32 = 0.4, pf8 = 0.2).
This strategy explicitly enforces the marginal capability of the f32 branch, ensuring it provides a
high-quality supervision target. We assign a lower weight to f8-Solo (0.2) since the strong pre-trained
f8 branch requires less tuning, allowing us to allocate more budget to the f32 and joint objectives.
Analysis of MixTeacher Inference. Having determined the suitable routing configuration, we
further analyze the inference behavior of the MixTeacher (Table 3). By comparing the inference
modes, we observe that the Mixed Mode effectively bridges the gap between the coarse f32 latent
space and the high-fidelity target. While the f32-Solo baseline (0.62 GenEval) yields suboptimal
performance due to the aggressive compression, the Mixed Mode (0.74 GenEval) demonstrates
that the teacher successfully utilizes the f8 context to resolve structural ambiguities. Crucially, as
visualized in Figure 4, the images generated by the f32 and f8 branches in Mixed Mode are visually
indistinguishable, matching in both high-frequency details and semantic alignment. This indicates
that although the two branches reside in distinct latent spaces, the MixTeacher successfully projects

Distillation strategy We explore four strategies in Table 4 and Fig. 5: (1) Baseline — an f32 model
initialized from the f8 model (no distillation). (2) DINOv2 teacher — align the DiT’s internal features
to DINOv2 following REPA [Leng et al., 2025]. (3) f8 feature teacher — align f32 features to
those from the f8 model following Ren et al. [2025a]. (4) MixTeacher (ours) — process f8 and f32
tokens together and use the f32 branch as the distillation signal. External semantic guidance from
DINOv2 brings little gain. Our baseline has already been pre-trained in f8 and then fine-tuned in
f32, so semantic priors are strong; additional supervision from a generic image encoder is redundant
and not matched to the diffusion objective. Moreover, feature alignment to DINOv2 requires grid
upsampling from f32, which induces checkerboard/ringing even with learned convolutional mapping.
The problem is amplified under variable aspect ratios (tall/wide samples), where DINOv2 offers
limited spatial guidance for the diffusion trajectories. Directly aligning to the f8 feature grid performs
similarly or worse for the same reason: the f8 lattice is 4× finer per dimension than f32, so supervision
again depends on learned upsampling across mismatched token layouts, introducing aliasing and bias.
In contrast, ours supervises in the f32 space using the f32 branch’s v-prediction. This avoids cross-
grid interpolation, matches the denoising objective, and yields sharper details and better text–image
consistency, as seen qualitatively in Figure 5 and quantitatively in Table 4.
Distillation versus high-resolution training. To isolate the effect of marginal score distillation,
we train a same-backbone control from the same f8-initialized f32 checkpoint, using identical high-
resolution scaling but without distillation. During scaling, 1K samples use both distillation and flow
matching, while 2K–4K samples use flow matching only. As shown in Table 6, UltraDiff improves
DPG, GenEval, and HPSv3 by 11.38, 0.078, and 1.71 at 1K, respectively, while also achieving better
FID, aesthetic score, and CLIP similarity at 4K. These results highlight the complementary roles of
distillation in transferring the f8 prior and high-resolution flow matching in adapting to larger spatial
scales.

5
Conclusion
In this work, we presented UltraDiff, a novel distillation framework designed to enable high-fidelity
image synthesis within highly compressed latent spaces (e.g., f32). To overcome the inherent difficulty
of training in such compact representations, we introduced MixTeacher, a dual-branch architecture
that establishes a direct, grid-aligned supervision signal for the student model. By distilling the
marginal vector field from a high-quality anchor directly into the target latent space, our method
effectively transfers complex structural details. Extensive experiments demonstrate that UltraDiff
substantially narrows the quality gap between f8 and f32 generation in the evaluated DiT pipeline
while retaining the inference efficiency of f32 architectures. With 20 sampling steps on a single
A100 GPU, UltraDiff generates a 2K image in 6.2 seconds, a 40.3× speedup over the f8 anchor, and
remains practical at 4K where the anchor runs out of memory. We hope this streamlined distillation
paradigm paves the way for more scalable and efficient generative models in the future.

A
Limitations and Future Work
While UltraDiff demonstrates the effectiveness of joint-latent distillation for efficient high-fidelity
synthesis, several limitations define the scope of the current evidence.
Architecture scope. We evaluate UltraDiff on one autoencoder–DiT pipeline. The method only adds
latent-specific branches and shared attention during teacher training, and the distilled student remains
a standard single-branch DiT. This makes the framework compatible in principle with other DiT-based
pipelines, but this architectural compatibility should not be interpreted as empirical validation across
backbones. Substantially different denoising architectures remain untested.
Dependence on pretrained components and multi-stage training. UltraDiff assumes access to a
strong f8 text-to-image anchor and fixed f8/f32 VAEs. Its f32-transfer, MixTeacher, and distillation
stages add training complexity, although their combined adaptation cost is lower than directly training
our f32 baseline (Section B). This complexity is confined to training; inference uses only the final
single-branch f32 student.
Independent latent spaces. The f8 and f32 VAEs are trained independently and are frozen during
UltraDiff training. MixTeacher therefore learns their relationship at the diffusion vector-field level
rather than enforcing explicit compatibility between their latent spaces. Combining UltraDiff with
compatible-VAE training [Yi et al., 2025, Zhao et al., 2024] may further improve transfer.
Reproducibility and data access. The training data and f8 anchor are proprietary, which limits
exact reproduction of the reported checkpoint. We provide the architecture, objectives, stage-wise
optimization settings, data scale, and compute cost so that the method itself can be implemented and
evaluated with alternative components. Validation with a fully open-source teacher–student pipeline
remains important future work.
Model scaling and conditioning. We have not explored very large f32 backbones (e.g., > 10B
parameters), and the current model uses T5-XXL rather than a modern vision-language model for
conditioning. Studying larger backbones, stronger multimodal conditioning, and additional DiT
families is left for future work.
B
Implementation Details
B.1
Training Strategy
To train our model, we employ a multi-stage training strategy designed to stabilize the learning
process.
Phase 1: F32 Pre-training. We first train the DiT model in the f32 latent space for 120,000 iterations.
This model is initialized from an f8 model, while the patch embedding and output prediction heads
are randomly initialized. We refer to this checkpoint as f32-phase1.
14


Phase 2: Teacher Preparation. For the teacher model training, we utilize a dual-branch initialization:
the f8 branch is initialized with the f8 model, and the f32 branch is initialized with the f32-phase1
checkpoint. We train this teacher model for 5,000 iterations at resolutions of 5122 and 10242 of
varying aspect ratio.
Phase 3: Distillation and Scaling. Following teacher preparation, we freeze the teacher model and
initialize a student model using f32-phase1 to perform distillation. The student model is first trained
at resolutions of 512 and 1024 for 50,000 iterations. Subsequently, we scale the training resolution to
1K, 2K, 3K, and 4K mixed resolution for 10,000 iterations.
High-resolution distillation protocol.
Our MixTeacher is prepared at 512 and 1024 resolutions,
where the teacher provides stable and reliable supervision. We therefore apply the distillation loss
only during the 512–1K training stage. For higher resolutions, including 2K, 3K, and 4K, we disable
the teacher-based distillation loss and continue training the student using the standard flow-matching
objective. This design avoids relying on an unstable or prohibitively expensive high-resolution teacher.
In practice, the 1K distillation stage transfers the high-fidelity f8 priors into the f32 student, while
the subsequent high-resolution flow-matching stage adapts the same student to larger resolutions
efficiently.
Loss Function: For 1K resolution, we employ a distillation loss and flow matching loss. For higher
resolutions (2K, 3K, 4K), we exclusively calculate the flow matching loss to maximize computational
efficiency.
Distributed Training. All training is conducted on 64 NVIDIA A100 GPUs. We utilize Fully
Sharded Data Parallel (FSDP) with a hybrid sharding strategy to manage memory consumption
effectively during distributed training.
B.2
Training Cost and Practical Setup
The f8/f32 VAEs are trained separately before UltraDiff and then kept fixed, while the f8 text-to-
image anchor is treated as an available pretrained component. Table 2 in the main paper reports the
stage-wise optimization steps and aggregate GPU-days required to adapt these fixed components into
the final f32 student. The multi-stage process is used only during model development; inference uses
the final standalone f32 student without the teacher or f8 branch.
B.3
Model, Data, and Open-source Plan
The strong f8 text-to-image model used in our pipeline is an internal proprietary model. The filtered
corpus size is reported in the main paper, and stage-wise training resources are summarized in Table 2.
All data were collected and curated under copyright-compliant data governance.
For scale context, SD3-medium reports 1B pretraining images followed by 30M aesthetic fine-tuning
images and 3M preference images [Esser et al., 2024]. PixArt-Σ reports a 33M-image high-resolution
corpus and is initialized from PixArt-α, whose pretraining used 25M images [Chen et al., 2024, 2023].
SDXL, Playground v2.5, FLUX.1, and Sana do not disclose directly comparable unique-image counts
in their primary reports. Because filtering criteria, repeated sampling, and training stages differ across
systems, these figures provide scale context rather than a controlled data-efficiency comparison.
We plan to release the training and inference code for UltraDiff, including the MixTeacher training
pipeline and marginal score distillation procedure. However, since the current implementation relies
on internal teacher/student backbones and proprietary training data, the exact pretrained weights used
in this work may not be releasable.
UltraDiff is not tied to a specific proprietary implementation, but the present experiments validate only
one DiT family. The framework can in principle be instantiated with open-source f8 text-to-image
models and compressed-latent f32-style DiT backbones without changing the student’s inference
architecture. Empirical validation on fully open-source and substantially different backbones remains
future work.

NeurIPS Paper Checklist
The checklist is designed to encourage best practices for responsible machine learning research,
addressing issues of reproducibility, transparency, research ethics, and societal impact. Do not remove
the checklist: The papers not including the checklist will be desk rejected. The checklist should
follow the references and follow the (optional) supplemental material. The checklist does NOT count
towards the page limit.
Please read the checklist guidelines carefully for information on how to answer these questions. For
each question in the checklist:
• You should answer [Yes], [No], or [N/A].
• [N/A] means either that the question is Not Applicable for that particular paper or the
relevant information is Not Available.
• Please provide a short (1–2 sentence) justification right after your answer (even for [N/A]).
The checklist answers are an integral part of your paper submission. They are visible to the
reviewers, area chairs, senior area chairs, and ethics reviewers. You will also be asked to include it
(after eventual revisions) with the final version of your paper, and its final version will be published
with the paper.
The reviewers of your paper will be asked to use the checklist as one of the factors in their evaluation.
While [Yes] is generally preferable to [No], it is perfectly acceptable to answer [No] provided a
proper justification is given (e.g., error bars are not reported because it would be too computationally
expensive” or “we were unable to find the license for the dataset we used”). In general, answering
[No] or [N/A] is not grounds for rejection. While the questions are phrased in a binary way, we
acknowledge that the true answer is often more nuanced, so please just use your best judgment and
write a justification to elaborate. All supporting evidence can appear either in the main paper or the
supplemental material, provided in appendix. If you answer [Yes] to a question, in the justification
please point to the section(s) where related material for the question can be found.
