Abstract
Blind all-in-one image restoration must adapt a shared model
to heterogeneous and sometimes mixed degradations with-
out a task label at test time. We introduce WaFIR, a de-
coder module that decomposes features into Haar subbands,
summarizes each subband with a compact Fourier descrip-
tor, and routes it to prompt-conditioned spectral modu-
lation experts. Unlike image-level frequency conditioning,
WaFIR makes routing decisions at the subband level, en-
abling frequency-specific processing while retaining the task
context learned by the backbone. Under a unified training
and evaluation protocol for denoising, deraining, and dehaz-
ing, WaFIR improves average PSNR over the three tasks by
0.48 dB over the PromptIR baseline and by 0.38 dB over
the recent Up-Restorer. Matched-capacity and routing con-
trols show that the gain is not explained by additional pa-
rameters alone. On unseen noise intensities, descriptor-based
routing is more robust than a discrete label-conditioned al-
ternative and remains on par under compound degradations,
while routing analyses show stable subband-dependent expert
use across degradation types. These results support subband
frequency structure as an interpretable complement to prompt-
based conditioning for blind image restoration. Code is
available at https://anonymous.4open.science/
r/WaFIR/README.md.

Introduction
Real-world images suffer from diverse degradations such as
sensor noise, haze and rain streak. Classical restoration han-
dles these with separate task-specific models Zhang et al.
(2017); Zamir et al. (2021); Wei et al. (2018), an approach
that scales poorly as the number of tasks grows. All-in-one
restoration instead recovers multiple degradations with a sin-
gle model Li et al. (2022); Valanarasu, Yasarla, and Patel
(2022); Potlapalli et al. (2023); Cui et al. (2025); Zamfir et al.
(2025), where the central question is how to adapt computa-
tion to each input. Existing methods answer this with different
conditioning signals, including contrastive degradation em-
beddings Li et al. (2022), learnable prompts Potlapalli et al.
(2023), physics-inspired latent variables Liu et al. (2025), and
uncertainty-based task weighting Wu et al. (2025). Despite
architectural differences, all of them condition adaptation on
a representation that summarizes the feature map without re-
solving its spectral organization. Such signals identify the
overall restoration context but carry little information about
how degradation energy is distributed across frequency sub-
bands, so feature regions with distinct spectral characteristics
may receive similar routing decisions. This limitation mat-
ters most when models must generalize beyond their training
conditions. Degradations that produce similar global repre-
sentations often differ sharply in spectral distribution. Haze
alters low-frequency components, rain streaks concentrate di-
rectional energy in middle-to-high-frequency subbands, and
additive noise, though approximately uniform across the spec-
trum, dominates high frequencies because natural-image spec-
tra decay with frequency. These characteristics are more nat-
urally distinguished in frequency subspaces than by global
summaries, and because they are computable directly from
intermediate features (Supplementary Figure S1), they remain
available even when degradation types or intensities are un-
seen at test time. We therefore ask whether subband-level
spectral structure provides a more informative routing sig-
nal than spectrally unresolved representations, with prompt
features supplying complementary task context. Building on
this, we propose WaFIR (Wavelet–Fourier Image Restora-
tion), which augments PromptIR Potlapalli et al. (2023) with
a Spectral Mixture-of-Experts (SPMoE) module at each
decoder stage. SPMoE decomposes each decoder feature into
four Haar subbands (LL, LH, HL, HH), extracts a compact six-
dimensional Fourier descriptor per subband, and routes each
subband through a sparse top-𝑘gate to specialized Fourier
experts that perform prompt-conditioned magnitude modula-
tion. Routing is conditioned jointly on the descriptor and the
pooled prompt embedding. Wavelets localize where and at
what scale spectral information sits, whereas Fourier modula-
tion adapts which frequencies are corrected. Analyses support
this principle. Routing weights organize primarily by wavelet
subband while carrying only chance-level linearly decodable
degradation information, indicating that experts self-organize

Our contributions are:
1. Subband-level spectral routing. We introduce per-
subband Fourier descriptors as an input-derived routing
signal for unified restoration, jointly conditioning sparse
expert routing with pooled prompt features.
2. Wavelet-Fourier expert design. We propose the SP-
MoE module, pairing Haar decomposition with prompt-
conditioned Fourier spectral-modulation experts, stabi-
lized by a zero-initialized residual gate and a per-subband
load-balancing loss.
3. Comprehensive evaluation and interpretable routing.
We show that WaFIR improves the PromptIR backbone
on denoising and deraining, is competitive with the state
of the art overall, and generalizes without fine-tuning
to unseen datasets and an industrial domain. We further
show that its experts self-organize by frequency subband,
and that descriptor-based routing is more robust than a
label-conditioned alternative under unseen noise inten-
sities.

Related Work
Blind All-in-One Image Restoration: Unified restoration
methods recover multiple degradation types with a single net-
work by learning adaptive conditioning signals from the input.
PromptIR Potlapalli et al. (2023) extracts learnable prompts
from decoder features, AdaIR Cui et al. (2025) mines and
modulates low- and high-frequency feature components, and
more recent methods such as OneRestore Guo et al. (2024b)
and FAPE-IR Liu et al. (2026) strengthen feature adaptation
for harder degradation scenarios. Despite these differences,
adaptation is applied at a coarse granularity—a global condi-
tioning signal or a two-way low/high-frequency split—so fine-
grained frequency regions within the same feature map are
not resolved into separately specialized computations. WaFIR
instead conditions after resolving the feature into wavelet sub-
bands, letting different spectral regions receive different expert
assignments while remaining jointly conditioned by prompt
context.
Mixture-of-Experts for Adaptive Restoration: MoE en-
ables adaptive computation by activating a sparse subset of
experts per input Shazeer et al. (2017). Restoration models
such as MoCE-IR Zamfir et al. (2025) and MOERL Wang
et al. (2025) route experts by degradation-aware embeddings,
restoration complexity, or global feature representations, orga-

Frequency-aware Image Restoration: Frequency represen-
tations are widely used in restoration. MWCNN Liu et al.
(2018) applies wavelet decomposition for multi-scale repre-
sentation, and FFC Suvorov et al. (2022) introduces Fourier
convolutions to enlarge the receptive field. Among unified
methods, AdaIR Cui et al. (2025) is closest to our work: it
likewise operates in the frequency domain, mining low- and
high-frequency components from the features (guided by the
degraded image’s spectra) and modulating them through a
bidirectional operator. We take a different approach to pro-
cessing the frequency domain: WaFIR extracts per-subband
Fourier descriptors over four Haar subbands and uses them
directly for sparse top-𝑘routing, so different spectral regions
activate different experts. This is a frequency-resolved routing
mechanism rather than a global frequency-modulation stage.
Prompt conditioning, frequency representations, and sparse
expert routing each improve unified restoration independently.
WaFIR connects them through a different question: can fre-
quency subbands themselves serve as an interpretable ba-
sis for expert specialization? Rather than combining existing
modules, WaFIR shows that routing self-organizes by wavelet
subband rather than degradation category, giving a frequency-
grounded routing principle that generalizes more robustly to
unseen degradations.

4
Experiments
4.1
Implementation Details
WaFIR is implemented in PyTorch and is trained end-to-end
on all degradation types simultaneously. All experiments use
the AdamW optimizer Loshchilov and Hutter (2017) with an
initial learning rate of 2 × 10−4, 𝛽1 = 0.9, 𝛽2 = 0.999, and
weight decay 10−2. The learning rate follows a linear warm-up
over 15 epochs, then decays to zero via cosine annealing over
the remaining epochs. We train for 150 epochs with a batch
size of 32 and an input patch size of 128×128 pixels, randomly
cropped from training images. Data augmentation consists of
random horizontal and vertical flips. Training uses BFloat16
mixed precision Kalamkar et al. (2019) on 2 NVIDIA A100
GPUs.
The total loss combines pixel-wise ℓ1 reconstruction with
the auxiliary per-subband load-balance term (Eq. 16), with
𝜆=0.01 fixed throughout all experiments. WaFIR contains
42.15M parameters in total, of which 35.59M belong to the
PromptIR backbone and +6.56M are introduced by the three
SPMoE levels (+18.4%). Inference is evaluated at full image
resolution on a single GPU with no test-time augmentation.
4.2
Datasets and Evaluation Metrics
We follow the standard all-in-one protocol of AirNet Li et al.
(2022) and PromptIR Potlapalli et al. (2023) across three
degradation types. For denoising, we train on BSD400 Martin
et al. (2001) and WED Ma et al. (2016) (5,144 images) and
test on BSD68 Roth and Black (2005) and Urban100 Huang,
Singh, and Ahuja (2015) at 𝜎∈{15, 25, 50}. Urban100
serves as a cross-dataset generalization test: the AWGN pro-
cess matches training, but the urban-texture image domain
is unseen. For deraining, we train on Rain100L Yang et al.
(2017) (200 pairs, repeat factor 120 for multi-task balance Li
et al. (2022)) and test on the Rain100L test set (100 pairs).
For dehazing, we train on the full RESIDE dataset Li et al.
(2019) (72,135 training hazy images) and evaluate on the
500-image Synthetic Objective Testing Set (SOTS), follow-
ing the same standard protocol as the baselines. Quantitative
results are reported using PSNR, SSIM Wang et al. (2004), and
LPIPS Zhang et al. (2018) on full RGB images without task-
specific post-processing. We further provide qualitative and
interpretability analyses using residual maps, Fourier spec-
tral maps, and Local Attribution Maps (LAM) Gu and Dong
(2021) to evaluate reconstruction fidelity, frequency restora-
tion, and the model’s spatial attribution behavior.

Results
Comparisons with SOTA models:

Descriptor and prompt are complementary (A12–A14):

Decomposition and cross-subband interaction (A7, A9):
Removing the grouped 1×1 cross-subband convolution (A7,
−0.16 dB) shows inter-frequency interaction before IDWT
aids coherent reconstruction, and replacing Haar DWT with
average pooling (A9, −0.10 dB) shows structured decomposi-
tion is needed for subband-specific descriptors.
Phase modulation (A4): The bounded, zero-initialized phase
branch converges near identity: removing it changes mean
PSNR by only +0.04 dB (within run-to-run variance), so mag-
nitude modulation carries the restoration-relevant informa-
tion. We keep the full expert for consistency with the released
code; the magnitude-only variant is equivalent.
Seed robustness: To confirm the ablation margins are not
seed artifacts, we retrain both the full model (A3) and the no-
SPMoE baseline (A0) with two random seeds (best-validation
checkpoints; Supplementary Table S6). A3 attains FULL =
30.29±0.02 dB versus A0 29.83±0.04 dB, and the +0.46 dB
SPMoE gain holds on both seeds (+0.45 and +0.47 dB), far
exceeding the seed spread. Because A3’s seed deviation on
FULL is only 0.02 dB, the component deltas of ≥0.14 dB in
Table 3 all lie well outside seed noise.
Routing under distribution shift (A3 vs. A13): The com-
parisons above are in-distribution, where a label-conditioned
router (A13) receiving the ground-truth degradation label is
marginally stronger (+0.06 dB on average on BSD68). This
edge inverts off-distribution (Table 4; full results in Sup-
plementary Table S4): A3 exceeds A13 by +0.45 dB PSNR
(+0.046 SSIM) at extrapolated 𝜎=70 and by +0.67 dB on
rain+noise. On haze+noise the two are indistinguishable
(15.755 vs. 15.739 dB), and both degrade sharply (roughly
12–18 dB below single-degradation performance), a shared
failure mode rather than an advantage for either. In short,
when the ground-truth label is unavailable or ill-defined, A3’s
continuous descriptor stays well-defined while A13 has no
fallback, making label-free frequency routing the more robust
choice off-distribution.

5
Conclusion
We presented WaFIR, which inserts a Spectral Mixture-of-
Experts (SPMoE) module into each PromptIR decoder level
to decompose features into Haar subbands, summarize each
with a compact 6-D Fourier descriptor, and route it through
a learned sparse gate to specialized Fourier experts. Under
a unified all-in-one protocol, WaFIR improves over Promp-
tIR by +0.48 dB on average, and its routing self-organizes
primarily by frequency subspace rather than by degradation
identity, as supported by t-SNE, NMI/probe analysis, and an
amplitude-invariance control. The novelty lies in the granular-
ity and input-derived nature of the routing signal, which com-
plements the task context a prompt-based blind restorer infers
from the input. Moreover, our findings suggest that input-
derived frequency priors and generalization across datasets
and restoration tasks provide a promising foundation for med-
ical and scientific imaging, and vision-language restoration
with large language models; further limitations are discussed
in Supplementary Section S6.

Supplementary Material
The central thesis of WaFIR is that its contribution lies in the
granularity and nature of the routing signal frequency-aware
expert routing performed at the level of individual wavelet
subbands, which complements the task context inferred by a
prompt-based blind restorer rather than merely in operating
without an explicit degradation label at test time. Label-free
inference is a consequence of this design, not its novelty;
the novelty is that routing decisions are resolved per subband
from an input-derived spectral descriptor. This supplementary
material substantiates that thesis and expands the method,
analysis, and experimental claims summarized in the main
manuscript. It is intended to be read together with the main
paper: Section S1 expands the Method section, Section S2
supports the routing claims in the Introduction and Analy-
sis section, Section S3 expands the main ablation table, and
Sections S4–S5 provide the additional results and complexity
evidence referenced from the Experiments section. The orga-
nization follows the evaluation logic of the study. Section S1
gives the complete SPMoE forward pass and the per-subband
load-balance formulation. Section S2 examines how routing
self-organizes and includes matched identity-conditioned con-
trols. Section S3 reports component, routing, and regulariza-
tion ablations. Section S4 provides additional qualitative, and
out-of-distribution evaluations. Section S5 analyzes param-
eter and computational overhead, and Section S6 discusses
limitations and future work.

Sparse spectral routing is only useful if the four experts remain
distinct. This section explains why a load-balance term is
required, why we apply it per subband, and quantifies its
effect.
Why Regularization Is Required
Sparse top-𝑘routing is prone to expert collapse: without reg-
ularization the router quickly favors one or two experts for all
inputs, so the rest receive no gradient and remain uninitialized
(Fedus, Zoph, and Shazeer, 2022; Lepikhin et al., 2020). In
WaFIR this is fatal, because the entire rationale for four ex-
perts is that each specializes in a distinct frequency subband.
Under collapse, all subbands route to the same pair and SP-
MoE reduces to a dense layer with extra parameters and no
specialization.

Why Per-Subband, Not Global
A global penalty Í
𝑒( ¯𝑤𝑒−1/𝐸)2, with ¯𝑤𝑒averaged over all
subbands, admits a degenerate optimum: the router can send
LL →{𝐸1, 𝐸2} and LH →{𝐸3, 𝐸4} exclusively routing each
subband to a fixed pair while appearing globally balanced.
More generally, any permutation assigning a fixed subset of
subbands to a fixed pair satisfies a global constraint while pro-
viding no per-subband specialization. The per-subband form
removes this global loophole by penalizing persistent expert
starvation separately within each subband. It does not force
the final router to remain uniform; rather, it keeps all ex-
perts trainable early in optimization while allowing nonuni-
form, subband-dependent specialization to emerge as the re-
construction objective becomes dominant.

S2.3
Controlled Identity-vs-Frequency Test
Oracle label-conditioned control (A13).
The analyses
above characterize what WaFIR does; A13 tests whether the
frequency-based principle is preferable to an identity-based
one under matched capacity. A13 is architecturally identical
to WaFIR (A3) but replaces the spectral descriptor with a
routing signal derived from the oracle degradation label: ev-
ery subband of an image is routed by the same one-hot label.
As expected, A13’s routing weights carry far stronger degra-
dation information (Deg-MI = 0.519 vs. 0.091), uniformly
distributed across subbands (LL/LH/HL/HH ≈0.50–0.57),
and subband organization collapses (Sub-NMI = 0.024 vs.
0.243). The two models thus operate under genuinely differ-
ent principles.
Crucially, oracle labels do not translate into uniformly bet-
ter restoration. Under the controlled reduced-budget evalua-
tion (Table S4), A13’s in-distribution advantage is marginal
(+0.06 dB on average on BSD68) and reverses under distribu-
tion shift, where A3’s continuous descriptor stays well-defined
while a one-hot label has no fallback. WaFIR attains this with-
out any degradation classifier, whereas A13 requires ground-
truth labels that are unavailable in deployment. The mech-
anism is that the descriptor encodes noise energy continu-
ously (var_mag ∝𝜎2), enabling per-image adaptation within
a noise level that a one-hot label cannot represent.

S3.2
Interpretation of Component Ablations
In this section, we conduct controlled ablation studies to evalu-
ate the contribution of each component in WaFIR. All ablation
variants are trained on reduced subsets of the original datasets
(1,200 images for denoising, 200 images for deraining, and
391 images for dehazing) while preserving the network archi-
tecture outside the modified component, optimization settings,
training schedule, and evaluation protocol. This matched set-
ting is designed to attribute performance differences to the
ablated component rather than to changes in data or optimiza-
tion.
Both decoder placement and three-level design are critical
(A1, A2, A15). Adding SPMoE at L3 only (A1, Δ=−0.14) or
at L2+L3 (A2, Δ=−0.20 dB) falls significantly short of the full
three-level configuration. We attribute this to the complemen-
tary nature of multi-scale frequency correction: the coarsest
block (L3, 𝐶=384) modulates global spectral shape, while
shallower blocks (L2, L1) address scale-specific textures. Ex-
tending SPMoE to encoder levels as well (A15, Δ=−0.41 dB)
actively degrades performance, indicating that spectral expert
modulation is beneficial specifically at decoder levels—where
prompt context is injected and reconstruction occurs—rather
than throughout the network.
Learned routing and soft interpolation are both essen-
tial (A5, A6). Random subband-to-expert assignment (A5,
Δ = −0.23 dB) demonstrates that the learned router captures
meaningful frequency-specialized routing patterns rather than
arbitrary assignments. Restricting the router to top-𝑘= 1 (A6,
Δ = −0.35 dB) results in the largest performance drop, high-
lighting the importance of soft interpolation between multi-
ple experts. As illustrated by the arc geometry in Figure S2,
weighted combinations of two experts allow each subband to
adapt continuously to intermediate frequency characteristics.
In contrast, winner-take-all routing forces each subband to rely
on a single expert, reducing routing flexibility and limiting the
representation of mixed spectral patterns.
The spectral descriptor and prompt play complementary
roles (A12–A14). Prompt-only routing (A12, Δ=−0.26 dB)
both degrades performance and reorganizes routing around
task identity rather than frequency subspace. Descriptor-only
routing (A14, −0.15 dB) retains more of the full model’s per-
formance than prompt-only routing in this controlled setting,
showing that intrinsic spectral characteristics provide informa-
tive routing cues. The remaining gap to the jointly conditioned
gate indicates that prompt-derived task context is complemen-
tary, particularly on deraining, where degradation spectra can
overlap. A label-conditioned router that receives the ground-
truth degradation label (A13) is slightly stronger in distribu-
tion (+0.06 dB on average on BSD68; Table S4), confirming
that frequency-based routing is a competitive input-derived
proxy for a signal WaFIR never accesses at inference.
Haar DWT and cross-subband interaction both contribute
(A7, A9). Removing the grouped 1×1 cross-subband convo-
lution (A7, −0.16 dB) confirms that inter-frequency interac-
tion before IDWT is necessary for coherent multi-scale re-
construction. Replacing Haar DWT with average pooling (A9,
−0.10 dB) shows that structured, lossless frequency decompo-
sition is needed for the spectral descriptor to access subband-
specific statistics.
Phase modulation is optional; magnitude modulation
alone is already sufficient (A4). Disabling phase modula-
tion (𝜑′=𝜑) yields a marginal improvement (+0.04 dB aver-
age on the FULL metric), confirming that the zero-initialized
phase projector remains near-identity throughout training and
that the reported gains stem primarily from subband-resolved
magnitude modulation. All WaFIR numbers reported in the
main paper use the full configuration with phase modulation
(A3) for consistency; the magnitude-only variant (A4) is an
equally strong and slightly lighter simplification.
Load-balance regularization is essential (A8, A10–A11).
Removing the load-balance loss (A8, 𝜆=0) causes expert col-
lapse: expert utilization approaches 1.0 and PSNR drops by
−2.66 dB relative to the adopted 𝜆=0.01 (A3) at a matched
epoch 105 on the fast-subset run (Table S5). The sweep over
𝜆establishes 𝜆∈[0.001, 0.01] as the effective operating range;
the full convergence analysis and formulation rationale (com-
parison with Switch Transformer and entropy losses) are given
in Section S1.3.

S4.2
Effectiveness on Line Artifacts and Rain
Structures
A recurring failure mode of purely spatial restorers is that
they process each pixel from a local window, so on long, thin,
repeated structures they either leave residual artifacts or over-
smooth the structure. WaFIR modulates the global Fourier
magnitude within each subband, so its correction is spatially
coherent along an entire structure rather than filtered piece-
wise. We examine the two structure-dominated cases directly.
Line structures (Urban100).
On the repeated line and lat-
tice patterns of Urban100 (Figure S6), the spatial baselines
introduce localized ringing or block artifacts along thin edges,
whereas WaFIR restores the full line coherently without such
artifacts. Because a periodic line structure maps to a compact,
oriented signature in the frequency domain, subband-wise
magnitude modulation corrects the whole structure at once in-
stead of reconstructing it segment by segment precisely where
local-window models break the line into artifacts.
Rain streaks (out-of-distribution CDD-11).
Rain streaks
concentrate directional energy in the LH/HL subbands.
Evaluated without fine-tuning on the unseen CDD-11 rain
(Figure S10), WaFIR removes residual streaks more com-
pletely while preserving the underlying texture, showing that
subband-resolved routing transfers to rain statistics not seen
during training.
Attribution analysis (LAM / Diffusion Index).
We quan-
tify why using Local Attribution Maps (LAM) (Gu and Dong,
2021) and their Diffusion Index (DI), which measures how
widely the contributing input pixels are spread (Figure S7).
WaFIR shows a substantially broader attribution footprint and
the highest DI on both examples (43.0 and 36.5, versus 18.1–
25.1 for the prompt/frequency baselines and ≤3.5 for AirNet):
each output pixel draws on long-range, structure-consistent
frequency evidence rather than a local window. This wide,
frequency-coherent attribution field is the mechanistic expla-
nation for the artifact-free line and rain recovery above, and
directly reflects the design thesis, subband-wise global Fourier
modulation trades the local receptive field of purely spatial
models for a wide, structure-aware one.

S6
Limitations and Future Work
Our model has several limitations that should be acknowl-
edged.
• Parameter overhead. The +18.4% parameter overhead
(42.15M total) places WaFIR above lightweight competi-
tors (AnyIR Ren et al. (2026): 6M, MoCE-IR Zamfir et al.
(2025): 25M). Two directions could reduce this cost. The
first is knowledge distillation from WaFIR to a compact
student. The second exploits the interpolation properties
of the spectral (Fourier) domain: since the per-frequency
magnitude and phase modulations produced by the ex-
perts vary smoothly across frequency, they could be pre-
dicted on a coarse frequency grid and interpolated back
to full resolution, so that the spectral experts operate
on a reduced set of frequency coefficients and cut both
parameters and compute.
• Dehazing. WaFIR trails its PromptIR reference on de-
hazing (29.70 vs. 30.58 dB on SOTS), despite iden-
tical full-RESIDE training. We attribute this to the
degradation and the training schedule, not the SP-
MoE module. Under the atmospheric scattering model
18


𝐼=𝐽𝑡+𝐴(1−𝑡) He, Sun, and Tang (2009), haze is a
smooth, orientation-free, low-frequency degradation that
lacks the fixed directional signature rain and noise pro-
vide, so the subband router has little structure to key
on. Empirically, the shared schedule also under-serves
dehazing (repeat factor one, versus heavily up-sampled
rain and noise), yet under a matched budget SPMoE still
improves SOTS over PromptIR (27.56 vs. 27.06 dB), in-
dicating an optimization-balance effect rather than an ar-
chitectural limit. Two directions could close the gap: task-
balanced scheduling (e.g., uncertainty-based weighting),
and adaptively up-weighting the prompt context when
the spectral descriptor is uninformative, as for haze.
• Degradation scope and compound cases. WaFIR cur-
rently covers noise, rain, and haze; extending to deblur-
ring and real-world images is a natural direction. Com-
pound degradations further remain a shared failure mode:
on haze+noise, both learned and label-conditioned rout-
ing collapse to ≈15.7 dB (Table S4), and at 𝜎=60 WaFIR
does not lead on every metric (Table S7); robust mixed-
degradation handling remains open.
• Spectral descriptor. The amplitude-invariance control
in Section S2.4 suggests that ratio-based spectral de-
scriptors (D1) may outperform the current absolute-
magnitude descriptor. Substituting D1 and measuring the
impact on PSNR is a concrete future experiment.
