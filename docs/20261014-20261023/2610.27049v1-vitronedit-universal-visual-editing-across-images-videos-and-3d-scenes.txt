1arXiv:2610.27049v1 [cs.CV] 19 Oct 2026 Abstract 

Recent advances in unified visual editing have begun to consolidate image and video editing within a single model, while 3D scene editing remains largely separate and constrained by scarce paired training data. Given the rich image and video editing supervision now available and the common editing semantics shared across modalities, a natural question is whether this knowledge can be transferred to data-scarce 3D editing. We present VitronEdit , a universal framework for image, video, and 3D scene editing. VitronEdit adopts a shared sequence repre-sentation and progressive multimodal training to transfer editing knowledge across modalities. We further introduce geometry-aware post-training , which leverages a pretrained geometry re-construction model to provide depth and camera-pose supervision. Surprisingly, applying geometric supervision only to 3D samples yields the best overall performance, while image and video editing also improve despite receiving no direct geometric supervision, suggesting that geometric regularization transfers through the shared backbone. Our analysis further shows that image and video training mainly transfers editing semantics to 3D, whereas 3D supervision primarily improves multi-view consistency. 

# 1. Introduction 

Instruction-driven visual editing has rapidly progressed from images to videos and 3D scenes. Recent works have begun to unify image and video editing within a single model, enabling editing capabilities to be shared across the two modalities (Ju et al., 2026; Mou et al., 2025; Xia et al., 2025; Wei et al., 2026). Meanwhile, generalizable 3D editing increasingly builds on pretrained image or video models to propagate edits consistently across multiple viewpoints (Zhao et al., 2026; Chen et al., 2026b; Huang et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2026c). These developments suggest that editing knowledge may extend beyond individual visual modalities. This motivates a broader question: can image, video, and 3D editing be learned jointly within a single model, allowing knowledge to be shared across all three domains? 

Two complementary opportunities make such unification promising, as illustrated in Figure 2. First, editing semantics are largely shared across modalities, while editing supervision is highly asymmetric. Editing operations such as object addition and removal, appearance modification, and style transfer share common semantics across modalities, providing a basis for transferring editing knowledge (Ju et al., 2026; Mou et al., 2025). At the same time, large-scale image and video editing datasets are increasingly available (Brooks et al., 2023; Yu et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2025b; Bai et al., 2026), whereas high-quality paired multiview editing data remain scarce (Chen et al., 2026c; Wang et al., 2026). This combination of shared semantics and asymmetric supervision makes cross-modal transfer particularly valuable: a unified model can learn general editing semantics from image and video data, while using comparatively limited 3D data to learn how these edits should remain consistent across viewpoints. 

Second, shared editing semantics do not by themselves ensure geometric consistency. Image editing requires geometrically valid spatial structure, while video and multiview editing further require consistency across frames or viewpoints. Prior work has shown that explicit geometric cues and geometry foundation models can improve structural consistency in image, video, and multiview generation (Wu et al., 2024; Li et al., 2025b; Parihar et al., 2025; Du et al., 2026; Yin et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026; Ma et al., 2026). This suggests that geometric knowledge can serve as a shared structural prior across modalities, complementing semantic transfer within a unified editor. Based on these insights, we introduce VitronEdit , a universal framework for image, video, and 3D editing. VitronEdit represents all three modalities as conditional frame sequences and jointly trains them with a shared backbone and modality-specific tokens, enabling editing knowledge from image and video data to transfer to data-scarce multiview editing. We further introduce geometry-aware post-training with depth and camera-pose supervision provided by a frozen geometry teacher, and investigate how geometric objectives should be applied across the three modalities. Our experiments reveal a clear division of roles across modalities. Image and video training transfers general editing semantics to multi-view inputs even without 3D supervision, whereas adding limited 3D data mainly strengthens cross-view consistency. As a result, VitronEdit outperforms dedicated 3D editing baselines in multi-view consistency while remaining competitive on image and video editing. We further find that geometry-aware post-training works best when restricted to 3D samples, improving overall 3D editing performance while also yielding gains on image and video through the shared backbone. The main contributions of this paper are: • We introduce VitronEdit, a unified framework that formulates image, video, and multiview 3D editing as conditional frame-sequence generation within a single shared model. • We jointly train VitronEdit across modalities to transfer editing knowledge from images and videos to data-scarce multiview editing, and use geometry-aware post-training that distills depth and camera cues from a frozen geometry teacher to regularize structural consistency. • We evaluate VitronEdit on image, video, and 3D editing, showing cross-modal transfer under limited 3D supervision and the effect of geometry-aware post-training under different supervision scopes. 

# 2. Related Work 

Unified Visual Editing. Early attempts at cross-modal visual editing adapt pretrained image diffusion models to structured visual inputs such as panoramas, videos, and 3D scenes through shared inference-time mechanisms (Kim et al., 2023; Kwon et al., 2024). While these methods provide a common editing procedure across modalities, they do not learn shared editing capabilities through joint multimodal training. More recent works unify image and video generation and editing within a single model using shared sequence representations and generative backbones (Ju et al., 2026; Wei et al., 2026; Mou et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2026a; Pan et al., 2026), enabling editing knowledge to transfer between images and videos. Unified visual models have also begun to incorporate geometric prediction and novel-view synthesis (Yang et al., 2026a), but instruction-driven 3D scene editing remains largely separate from image–video editing. VitronEdit bridges this gap by jointly learning image, video, and multiview 3D editing within a single shared model. 

3D Scene Editing. 3D editing commonly transfers powerful 2D editing priors to multiple rendered views and propagates the edits back to an underlying 3D representation. Early methods iteratively optimize NeRFs or 3D Gaussians using edited views (Haque et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2024b), with later approaches improving cross-view consistency through joint attention, feature fusion, or explicit geometric 3constraints (Chen et al., 2024a; Lee et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2024; Li et al., 2025b). Despite strong editing quality, these approaches typically require per-scene optimization or iterative 2D-3D updates. Recent methods instead learn generalizable multiview editors or exploit pretrained video priors to generate consistent edited views in a feed-forward manner (Zhao et al., 2026; Chen et al., 2026b; Huang et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2026c; Wang et al., 2026; Zhu et al., 2026). Related work further shows that treating multiview observations as pseudo-video sequences can combine temporal priors with explicit geometric correspondences (Ma et al., 2026). These methods adapt pretrained models into dedicated 3D editors, whereas VitronEdit keeps image and video editing data as joint supervision and retains all three tasks in one model. 

Geometry-Aware Visual Generation. Generative models trained primarily for visual fidelity may still produce geometrically inconsistent structures, motivating the integration of explicit geometric super-vision. One line of work injects geometric information directly into generative models through depth conditioning, geometric feature alignment, or auxiliary dense prediction (Wu et al., 2024; 2026; Bai et al., 2025c; Rao et al., 2026). A complementary line of work uses pretrained geometry or reconstruction models as teachers, evaluators, or reward models for generative post-training (Go et al., 2026; Du et al., 2026; Yin et al., 2026; An et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026). Such approaches demonstrate that geometry foundation models can provide effective supervisory signals beyond conventional reconstruction objec-tives. VitronEdit extends this idea to unified visual editing, using a frozen geometry teacher to derive target-conditioned depth and camera supervision applicable to image, video, and multiview editing, and studying its supervision scope.

3.4. Geometry-Aware Post-Training 

The unified sequence representation enables knowledge sharing across modalities, but does not explicitly enforce the spatial structure or geometric consistency of the edited outputs. We therefore introduce geometry-aware post-training using a frozen latent-native geometry extractor built with the model-stitching strategy of Go et al. (2026). The extractor predicts depth maps and camera poses directly from both predicted and target latents, allowing the geometry extracted from the target latents to supervise the predicted geometry.

# 4. Experimental Settings 

Datasets. We curate the training data from existing image, video, and 3D datasets (Yu et al., 2025; Kuprashevich et al., 2026; Song et al., 2026; He et al., 2026; Lin et al., 2026; Ye et al., 2026; Ling et al., 2024). Since large-scale 3D scene editing data are not readily available, we additionally construct a multiview 3D scene editing dataset with substantial viewpoint variation, comprising 8K samples. The construction pipeline is provided in Appendix D. In total, our training set contains approximately 2.53M samples. The detailed data composition is summarized in Table 6 in Appendix A. 

Baselines and evaluation metrics. For image editing, we compare against instruction-based methods ICEdit (Zhang et al., 2025), VAREdit (Mao et al., 2026), and ChordEdit (Lu et al., 2026), as well as ACE++ (Mao et al., 2025) and FLUX.1 Kontext (Black Forest Labs et al., 2025), which additionally support reference-conditioned editing. For video editing, we include the instruction-based baselines VideoCoF (Yang et al., 2026c) and Omni-Video 2 (Yang et al., 2026b), together with VACE (Jiang et al., 2025), UniVideo (Wei et al., 2026), VINO (Chen et al., 2026a), Kiwi-Edit (Lin et al., 2026), and OmniWeaving (Pan et al., 2026), which support both instruction-based and reference-conditioned editing. For 3D editing, we compare with GaussianEditor (Chen et al., 2024b), DGE (Chen et al., 2024a), and EditSplat (Lee et al., 2025). We evaluate image editing in terms of structural preservation and semantic alignment with the editing instruction. Video editing is additionally assessed for temporal consistency and visual quality, while 3D editing focuses on instruction alignment and multi-view consistency. Detailed evaluation protocols are provided in Appendix C. 

Training details. We initialize VitronEdit with OmniWeaving (Pan et al., 2026), which adopts HunyuanVideo-1.5 (Wu et al., 2025a) as the generative backbone and Qwen2.5-VL (Bai et al., 2025b) as the MLLM. We apply LoRA (Hu et al., 2022) to the DiT backbone with a rank of 32 and a scaling factor 𝛼 = 32, and extend the MLLM vocabulary with modality-specific tokens. Stage 1 follows a progressive multimodal training schedule: we first train on image-editing data for 10K steps, then introduce video-editing data for an additional 7K steps, and finally incorporate 3D multiview editing data for 3K steps. Starting from the Stage-1 checkpoint, Stage 2 performs geometry-aware post-training for 500 steps with a frozen latent-native geometry extractor constructed following the model-stitching strategy of Go et al. # 5. Results and Discussion 

In our experiments, we want to address four questions as follows: • RQ-1 : How does VitronEdit perform across image, video, and 3D editing? • RQ-2 : How do image/video training and subsequent 3D training contribute to 3D editing? • RQ-3 : How do the type and scope of geometric supervision affect editing performance across modalities? • RQ-4 : How does sequence construction affect detail preservation in image and 3D editing?

3D Editing. We evaluate whether VitronEdit can effectively transfer editing capability to 3D while preserving cross-view consistency. As demonstrated in Table 3, VitronEdit shows the clearest advantage in multi-view consistency compared with baselines. VitronEdit achieves the best 3D consistency score and the lowest photometric reprojection error, while remaining close to EditSplat in semantic alignment, ranking second on both CLIPDir and CLIPSim. This indicates that the unified model preserves competi-tive editing semantics while producing substantially more consistent results across viewpoints. Figure 6 further illustrates this behavior. VitronEdit applies clothing, material, and appearance changes consis-tently across views, as shown by the suit, jade-carving, and snowy-bonsai examples. The snowy example also exposes a limitation: surrounding regions are modified more broadly than requested, showing that cross-view coherence does not necessarily imply precise editing locality. Its main advantage therefore lies in maintaining strong semantic editing while substantially improving multi-view consistency. 

5.2. Cross-Modal Transfer to 3D Editing (RQ-2) 

To disentangle cross-modal semantic transfer from 3D-specific consistency learning, we compare the initialization model with Stage-1 training without and with 3D editing data. As shown in Figure 7, training only on image and video data substantially improves semantic editing on multiview inputs: CLIPDir increases from 9.80 to 12.10 and CLIPSim from 27.00 to 27.30, while 3D Consistency changes only marginally from 69.20 to 70.10. This indicates that editing knowledge learned from images and videos transfers effectively to multiview editing even without paired 3D supervision. Adding 3D editing data produces only modest further gains in CLIPDir and CLIPSim, but raises 3D Consistency substantially from 70.10 to 73.98. The contrasting gains suggest that image and video supervision primarily transfers editing semantics, whereas limited 3D supervision mainly teaches how those edits should be realized consistently across viewpoints.

Restricting both depth and camera losses to 3D samples (Geo-3D ) improves every reported metric over w/o Geo and consistently outperforms Geo-All . Notably, image and video performance also improves despite receiving no direct geometric supervision. This suggests that geometry learned from 3D data acts as a transferable regularizer through the shared backbone, rather than requiring explicit supervision for every modality. We attribute this behavior to the alignment between geometric supervision and the underlying data structure. For multiview 3D scenes, depth and camera poses directly constrain cross-view geometry, whereas the same signals are less tightly aligned with image and video editing, particularly for videos containing object motion. We therefore adopt Geo-3D as the default configuration. 

5.4. Modality-Specific Sequence Design (RQ-4) 

Although VitronEdit represents all modalities as frame sequences, directly using the same sequence construction is suboptimal because the pretrained video backbone assumes temporally dense inputs. We therefore adapt the sequence representation to the characteristics of 3D views and static images. 

3D: Replicating Each View. We observed noticeable detail loss when directly encoding multiview 3D sequences with the pretrained temporal VAE. Unlike ordinary videos, adjacent 3D views can exhibit large viewpoint changes, making temporal compression prone to discarding high-frequency details. Reconstructing the original multiview sequences with the HunyuanVideo-1.5 VAE (Wu et al., 2025a) yields only 26.02 dB PSNR and preserves 41.9% of the high-frequency content. # 6. Conclusion 

We presented VitronEdit , a unified framework for image, video, and 3D scene editing, together with geometry-aware post-training for improving structural consistency. Our experiments reveal comple-mentary cross-modal transfer: image and video training primarily contributes editing semantics to 3D, while limited 3D supervision mainly strengthens multi-view consistency. We further find that restricting geometric supervision to 3D yields the best overall performance, with modest gains also transferring to image and video editing through the shared backbone. VitronEdit thus retains competitive editing capabilities across modalities while achieving its clearest advantage in multi-view 3D consistency.

# C. Evaluation Protocols 

C.1. Image Editing 

C.1.1. Benchmark 

We evaluate image editing on the PIE-Bench (Ju et al., 2024) and the reference-based editing subset of ICE-Bench (Pan et al., 2025). For evaluation, the edited images produced by the baseline methods are resized to match the dimensions of their corresponding input images. The PIE-Bench dataset contains 700 samples across ten editing categories: random editing (140); object replacement, addition, and removal (80 each); content, pose, color, and material changes (40 each); and background and style changes (80 each). Each sample provides a source image, an editing instruction, source and target descriptions, and an editing-region mask. The reference-based editing subset of ICE-Bench contains 518 samples: face swapping (95), reference-guided style editing (186), subject-guided inpainting (140), and virtual try-on (97). Each sample provides a source image, an editing instruction, a target description, and a reference image.

C.2. Video Editing 

C.2.1. Benchmark 

We evaluate video editing on FiVE-Bench (Li et al., 2025a) and the reference-based editing subset of VIE-Bench (Mou et al., 2025). Our FiVE-Bench evaluation set comprises 419 editing instances from 100 distinct source videos: rigid object replacement (100), non-rigid object replacement (100), color change (100), material change (100), object addition (9), and object removal (10). The VIE-Bench reference-based editing subset contains 20 instances: reference-guided object addition and object replacement (10 each). Each instance has a source video and a reference image. To accommodate the input frame requirements of the baseline methods, we uniformly sample 65 frames from each source video and set the frame rate to 24 fps. Source frames and masks are aligned by temporal index, and all methods are evaluated on output frames. Before computing frame-level metrics, we resize the source frames, edited frames, and masks to a common resolution according to the source video’s aspect ratio: 624 × 352 (landscape), 480 × 480 (square), or 352 × 624 (portrait). 

C.2.2. Metrics 

We evaluate video editing along three dimensions: Consistency , CLIP Semantics , and Temporal Quality . The evaluation uses both frame-level image metrics and video-level metrics: PSNR, LPIPS, SSIM, and CLIP similarities are computed for each frame and averaged over the valid frames of each video, whereas MFS, MFS-Edit, and DOVER (Wu et al., 2023) are computed on the complete video. Consistency measures the preservation of unedited content using PSNR, LPIPS, and SSIM. CLIP Semantics measures alignment with the target description and reference image using Whole, Edited, and Reference. Temporal Quality 20 assesses motion fidelity and video quality using MFS, MFS-Edit, and DOVER. Reference is evaluated only on the VIE-Bench reference-based editing instances. The frame-level PSNR, LPIPS, SSIM, and CLIP metrics follow the image-editing definitions above. FiVE-Bench provides frame-wise editing masks. For VIE-Bench object replacement, we use manually verified seed boxes to prompt SAM 2 (Ravi et al., 2024) to segment and propagate the source object through the video. For object addition, we use full-frame masks; these instances have no unedited region and are excluded from PSNR, LPIPS, and SSIM. For the CLIP metrics, the target description is taken from 

target_prompt when available and otherwise from the editing instruction. Whole compares the full edited frame with this description, whereas Edited first zeros pixels outside the editing mask. Reference compares the full edited frame with the full reference image. For Temporal Quality, MFS uses the FiVE-Bench implementation of CoTracker (Karaev et al., 2024) to track points in the source and edited videos and compare the directions of their displacements between adjacent frames. MFS is computed over the full frame. MFS-Edit restricts tracking to the bounding box enclosing the union of all nonempty frame-wise editing masks, yielding a fixed region across frames. DOVER evaluates each edited video and outputs a video quality score. For the combined instruction-and reference-based evaluation, each shared metric is aggregated by weighting the two subset means by their respective numbers of valid editing instances for that metric. Each editing instance contributes one score after any frame-level averaging; Reference is computed only on the reference-based subset. Instances excluded from a metric, such as reference-based object addition for PSNR, LPIPS, and SSIM, are excluded from its aggregation weights. 

C.3. 3D Editing 

C.3.1. Benchmark 

Our 3D editing evaluation follows the scene and editing-instruction setup of EditSplat (Lee et al., 2025). The benchmark comprises eight scenes, each with two editing instructions, for a total of 16 editing tasks. The scenes are drawn from Instruct-NeRF2NeRF (Haque et al., 2023), Mip-NeRF 360 (Barron et al., 2022), and BlendedMVS (Yao et al., 2020). We use the corresponding benchmark annotations for the editing instructions and the source and target text descriptions. To standardize the inputs and rendering conditions, we first reconstruct an unedited 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) representation (Kerbl et al., 2023) for each source scene from the same images and camera data, training it for 30,000 iterations. 

Camera trajectory and source views. For each scene, we interpolate 26 camera poses along a continuous segment of the dataset’s calibrated training-camera trajectory. Only the two endpoint cameras coincide with the original dataset cameras; the held-out test cameras are not used. We render the unedited 3DGS at these poses to form the source sequence. The angular spans are 119 ◦ for bear and bicycle, 70 ◦ for bonsai, 71 ◦ for face and person, 53 ◦ for fangzhou, 119 ◦ for garden, and 122 ◦ for stone_horse. 

Joint editing and scene reconstruction. VitronEdit uses the image-based reconstruction route for all reported 3D evaluations, with direct latent-to-3DGS decoding disabled. The 26 source views are jointly edited using 50 denoising steps. Keeping the first view once and repeating each subsequent view four times produces 101 pixel frames, corresponding to 26 latent frames. After VAE decoding, we extract the 26 corresponding edited images. The generated images have a long-side resolution of 448 pixels and are resized with Lanczos interpolation to the calibrated image resolution (480 × 480 or 624 × 352) before reconstruction. We initialize a new vanilla 3DGS from the original scene’s SfM sparse points, without reusing the unedited scene’s Gaussian parameters. We train it on the 26 edited images and their associated calibrated cameras for 30,000 iterations using 0.8 𝐿 1 + 0.2 𝐿 D-SSIM . Densification runs from iteration 500 to 15,000 at intervals of 100 iterations, with a gradient threshold of 2 × 10 −4. Opacity is reset every 3,000 iterations, and the spherical-harmonic degree is 3. The reconstruction uses the same dataset-derived camera poses as source rendering, rather than camera predictions from the geometry extractor. 21 Evaluation views. We select eight views with zero-based indices {0, 4, 7, 11, 14, 18, 21, 25 } from the 26-view trajectory. All methods render their edited scenes from these same cameras, yielding 16 tasks and 128 rendered images per method. These eight views are a subset of the views used to reconstruct the edited 3DGS; evaluation therefore measures performance at reconstruction training views, not held-out novel views. All reported 3D metrics are computed on the reconstructed scene renderings and evaluate the complete editing-and-reconstruction pipeline. When computing the editing direction, we use the rendering of the unedited 3DGS from the same camera as the source image. We follow the EditSplat task set and recompute the scores of all methods under this unified view setting.

# D. Construction of Multi-view Editing Data 

We construct multi-view editing data from videos of static indoor scenes recorded by a moving camera. Frames from each video show the same scene from different viewpoints. Each sample contains a source sequence, an editing instruction, and an edited sequence. The pipeline consists of source sequence preparation, instruction construction, and paired edit generation. 22 D.1. Source Sequence Preparation 

We use the RealEstate10K (Zhou et al., 2018) subset of RealCam-Vid (Zheng et al., 2025) and retain clips at a resolution of 1280 × 720. For each clip with at least 72 frames, we uniformly sample 72 frames over its full duration. This preserves coverage of the original camera trajectory. We encode the sampled frames as a 3-second video at 24 fps. We filter the sampled sequences using two measures, computed on grayscale frames 𝐼 𝑡 resized to a width of 640 pixels. We compute the Laplacian variance for each frame,

D.2. Target Selection and Instruction Construction Target selection. We use Qwen3-VL-30B-A3B (Bai et al., 2025a) to describe the objects in each sequence and estimate how reliably they can be identified across views. We select one target object for local editing. Local operations can modify, move, replace, or remove this object. For object addition, it serves as a reference for placing the new object. Candidate objects must satisfy the following conditions, based on the model’s predictions: • Recognition confidence of at least 0.45. • Normalized temporal span of at least 0.55. • Tracking stability of at least 0.45. • Center proximity of at least 0.30. • At least two distinguishing visual cues. • Identification as a single instance. • Instance-count confidence of at least 0.60. The normalized temporal span measures the interval between the object’s first and last appearance relative to the sequence duration. Tracking stability indicates how reliably the model identifies the same object across frames. Center proximity indicates how close the object is to the image center. We rank eligible objects using a weighted score. The score favors salient objects that are identified with high confidence. It also favors objects that remain visible over time, are tracked consistently, and lie near the image center. We penalize objects that may be confused with others and select the highest-ranked candidate. Sequences without an eligible target are discarded. 

Editing operations. We consider ten editing operations: • Local edits. Object addition, removal, replacement, and movement. Local edits also include changes to the target’s color, material, or texture. • Global edits. Background change, tone transfer, and style change. We use Qwen3.5-27B (Qwen Team, 2026) to generate four instruction groups for each scene. Each group describes one editing intent using three English paraphrases. The target and operation remain the same within a group. For global edits, the prompt provides six candidates from the corresponding background, tone, or style pool. 

Instruction validation and selection. We check whether each instruction describes a specific, observable edit and matches its assigned operation. The checks reject: 23 • Ambiguous references or references to multiple target objects. • Internal object identifiers. • Viewpoint-dependent directional terms. • Requests that conflict with the assigned operation. For example, a removal instruction must not request a replacement. A replacement instruction must name both the original object and its replacement. Each instruction is limited to 30 English words and 180 characters during post-processing. A group is discarded if it contains an invalid instruction or an instruction that duplicates another in the same scene. Each scene retains up to three valid groups, with one paraphrase selected from each group. We then choose one instruction per scene to bring the overall operation distribution closer to the desired proportions.

We provide Kling-O1 (Kling Team et al., 2025) with the source sequence and selected instruction to generate the edited sequence. The prompt asks the model to follow the original camera trajectory and perspective, while preserving scene layout, depth relationships, and content except where changes are required by the editing instruction. # E. Limitation 

One limitation is that the same geometric objectives do not benefit all modalities equally, as shown by our ablation study. Future work could explore modality-adaptive geometric supervision, particularly for videos where camera motion and object motion are entangled. In addition, our current 3D setting focuses on multi-view editing of static scenes. Extending the unified framework to dynamic 3D or 4D scene editing is also an interesting direction for future work. 27
