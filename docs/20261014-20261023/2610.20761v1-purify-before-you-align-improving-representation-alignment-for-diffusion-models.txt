## ABSTRACT 

Representation alignment improves and accelerates diffusion training by encour-aging intermediate features to match a pretrained visual encoder. However, stan-dard alignment matches pretrained representations without distinguishing what information may be more useful for generation. We introduce local appearance purification (LAP), which improves generation quality and makes encoders such as MAE more effective alignment targets by removing locally predictable content and aligning to the residual. LAP modifies only the alignment target, and requires no changes to diffusion training or inference. Through controlled interventions within a fixed encoder, we show that effective alignment depends on what infor-mation the target emphasizes, not simply how much image information it contains. Our theory gives a variational interpretation of standard alignment and, for fixed local predictors, a sum of token-wise information bounds conditioned on local appearance. To understand why purification benefits some encoders more than others, we derive an exact constant-readout test that measures how well the target can be matched without using the diffusion model’s hidden state. LAP improves generation quality across five pretrained representations (MAE, AIMv2, CLIP, DINOv2, DINOv3), with the largest gain for MAE: on ImageNet-256 with SiT-B/2, it reduces FID from 53 .1 to 42 .3. Its gains persist with stronger alignment recipes and tokenizers. Computed before diffusion training, the constant-readout score predicts the benefit of purification (Spearman ρ = 0 .94 across 13 encoders). However, one important choice remains largely empirical: which encoder should provide the alignment target? Different pretrained encoders lead to very different generation quality, and we still lack a clear principle for what makes a representation useful for alignment. The puzzle is sharpest for MAE (He et al., 2022). Since diffusion models learn to recover clean images from noise, features that preserve reconstructive information might seem especially suitable. MAE features predict clean-image content far better than DINOv2 (Oquab et al., 2024), yet yield smaller generation gains in our development comparison (Figure 3). Recent work shows that tar-get processing matters, including spatial operations in iREPA (Singh et al., 2026) and other target transformations (Shin et al., 2026; Ning et al., 2026). We ask a more basic question: 

What information should an alignment target ask the denoiser to match? 

To answer this, we hold the pretrained encoder fixed and intervene on the information selected for alignment. For DINOv2, aligning only to the locally predictable component performs worse than no alignment, while its residual retains the full benefit (Figure 2). Likewise, making the aligned representation more predictive of the denoising target improves prediction but worsens generation (Section 3.1). Together, these results show that effective alignment depends on what information the target emphasizes, not simply how much image information it contains. These results suggest a simple view: the denoising objective already provides a strong reconstruction signal, so alignment is most useful when it emphasizes information beyond what denoising already encourages. This view also suggests a simple fix without replacing the encoder. Local appearance purification (LAP) removes target content predictable from local appearance and aligns the denoiser to the residual, using linear or nonlinear local predictors (Figure 4). Across five visual teachers (MAE, AIMv2, CLIP, DINOv2, DINOv3), LAP improves generation quality, with the largest gains for MAE. On ImageNet-256 with SiT-B/2, LAP reduces MAE-based FID from 53 .1 to 42 .3. Its gains persist when combined with stronger alignment recipes and tokenizers, showing that target purification complements improvements elsewhere in the alignment pipeline. Beyond natural im-ages, LAP could make self-supervised reconstructive encoders useful alignment teachers in domains such as medical and scientific imaging, where suitable discriminative teachers may be unavailable or poorly matched to the data. We also develop an information-theoretic view of purified alignment. Standard alignment maximizes a variational lower bound on the information that the hidden state carries about the teacher target. For a fixed local predictor, purified alignment instead bounds a sum of token-wise information quantities, each conditioned on that token’s local appearance. However, a high alignment score need not imply an informative hidden state: if target tokens share a common direction, even a constant readout that ignores the hidden state can achieve a high cosine score. We derive the exact optimum of this constant readout in closed form, giving a diagnostic that can be computed before diffusion training. 2Preprint In a matched comparison of 13 encoders, this score strongly predicts the benefit of purification (Spearman ρ = 0 .94 ), supporting its use to identify teachers likely to benefit from LAP (Section 5.5). 

Contributions. (1) Through fixed-encoder interventions, we show that reconstruction predictability alone does not determine alignment quality. In particular, locally predictable content can hurt gen-eration even when it is easy to match. (2) We introduce LAP, a family of target transformations that removes locally predictable content before alignment. LAP improves generation across multiple visual teachers, composes with stronger alignment recipes and tokenizers, and requires no encoder change or inference overhead. (3) We develop an information-theoretic view of raw and purified alignment and derive a closed-form constant-readout diagnostic for alignment achievable without the hidden state. Computed before diffusion training, this score predicts the benefit of purification across 13 encoders.

## 3 WHY PURIFY THE ALIGNMENT TARGET ?

Across MAE, MoCo v3, DINOv2, I-JEPA, and CLIP, better clean-image prediction generally ac-companies worse generation (Figure 3). MAE predicts clean-latent patches best but yields smaller 3Preprint generation gains than the other four encoders in this development comparison. This raises a question about what the denoiser needs from its teacher: reconstruction information, or a different constraint on its intermediate representation? A cross-encoder comparison cannot identify the cause because architecture, data, and pretraining objective change together. We therefore hold DINOv2 fixed and intervene in two ways. First, we encourage the aligned representation to predict the denoising target more accurately. Second, we separate locally predictable target content from its residual and align to each alone. Applying the same auxiliary loss through a fixed random or separately learned projec-tion also worsens generation in development controls, so this degradation is not specific to the aligned projection (Appendix D). The unmodified REPA model shows why prediction alone is insufficient. DINOv2 features are de-codable from the hidden state (presence R2 = 0 .207 ), but the denoiser relies only weakly on the aligned subspace: ablating it increases velocity error by only 12 .2% of the full-layer effect, compared with 22 .7% for rank-matched random subspaces and 14 .0% for the untrained projector subspace in the unaligned model (Figure 16). Decodability does not imply reliance. This ablation tests reliance on the specific aligned subspace, but does not imply that all reconstruction-related information is unimportant. 3.2 LOCALLY PREDICTABLE CONTENT DOES NOT DRIVE THE ALIGNMENT BENEFIT 

We next ask whether locally predictable content is responsible for the benefit of a strong alignment target. We use an affine predictor from clean-latent patches to DINOv2 tokens to decompose each target feature into a locally predictable component and a residual, then align to each component separately while keeping the encoder and training recipe fixed. The predictable component has rank at most 17 and accounts for about 2% of DINOv2’s centered variance at full scale (Appendix D). The residual corresponds to the linear LAP variant defined in Section 4.1. The two components have sharply different effects (Figure 2). Aligning only to the locally predictable component worsens FID from 53 .92 without alignment to 56 .71 , whereas aligning to the residual reaches 41 .47 , preserving the gain of raw DINOv2 at 41 .75 . This difference is not explained by alignment difficulty: the predictable component is easier to match despite producing worse generation. A fixed random rank-17 projection remains within the REPA seed range in a development control, while a frozen dataset-level predictable component loses the benefit. These controls argue against rank reduction or batch refitting alone explaining the result (Appendix D). Together with the previous result, this supports a view in which alignment is useful not because it provides more reconstruction information, but because it can shape intermediate representations beyond what denoising already encourages. This motivates purification. We next formalize this idea as a target construction, develop its information-theoretic interpretation, and introduce a diagnostic for evaluating alignment targets.

We define locally predictable content as the part of an encoder representation predictable from local clean-image appearance. Let z = f (I) be the frozen encoder features and Xp the patchified clean latent. We fit a predictor dϕ from Xp to z and align to the residual: 

Lpred = E ∥z − dϕ(Xp)∥2 , ypure = sg[ z − dϕ(Xp)] . (3) Here sg denotes stop-gradient. Replacing y with ypure in Equation (1) gives the LAP objective. The predictor selects what to remove and receives no gradients from diffusion training. The denoising objective, model architecture, and inference procedure remain unchanged (Figure 4). We consider two variants of dϕ: an affine patch predictor and a nonlinear local predictor. 

LAP-L: linear purification. We fit an affine map from each clean-latent patch to its corresponding target token and subtract its prediction. This gives the residual used in Section 3.2. The affine predictor is refitted independently on each minibatch, with no gradient through the fit (Appendix D). 

LAP-N: nonlinear purification. We train a small convolutional predictor to recover the target from a local neighborhood of clean-latent patches. The predictor is fitted before diffusion training, frozen, and reused throughout training. Details are given in Appendix E. What LAP removes depends on the predictor: local patches may still contain semantic informa-tion (Brendel & Bethge, 2019), and a limited predictor may not capture all locally predictable con-tent. We therefore do not interpret the residual as a purely semantic representation, but instead evaluate its content and effect on generation empirically in Section 5. Purification is used only to construct training targets and adds no inference cost. 4.2 A V ARIATIONAL VIEW OF PURIFIED ALIGNMENT

5.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

Unless stated otherwise, we train class-conditional SiT-B/2 on ImageNet-1k at 256 × 256 resolution for 400K steps with batch size 64, the SD-VAE, and representation alignment at block 8. We eval-uate generation without guidance under the ADM protocol (Dhariwal & Nichol, 2021), reporting FID-50K, Inception Score (IS), precision, and recall. Results are averaged over two training seeds. Evaluation details and individual runs are provided in Appendices H and L. For development exper-iments, we use a 200-class subset ( dev-200 ) and report FID-DINOv2 on 10K samples (Stein et al., 2023). These values are not directly comparable to FID-50K. Hidden-state diagnostics use disjoint probe-fitting and evaluation images, noise levels t ∈ [0 .3, 0.7) , and ablation rank 256 (Appendix C). 5.2 GENERATION QUALITY ACROSS TEACHERS 

Raw MAE barely improves over no alignment, whereas LAP-N reduces FID from 53 .05 to 42 .30 ,approaching DINOv2-based REPA at 41 .75 (Table 1). AIMv2 and CLIP also improve in both seeds, with lower FID accompanied by higher IS, precision, and recall. Using LAP-L as a common com-parison across all five teachers, mean FID improves by 7.9, 3.7, and 2.0 for MAE, AIMv2, and CLIP, but by only 0.7 and 0.3 for DINOv3 and DINOv2, respectively. The latter differences are within seed variation. Purifier capacity also matters differently across teachers: for MAE, LAP-L reaches about 45 .2 FID, while LAP-N further improves to 42 .3. For CLIP, LAP-L captures most of the gain, whereas DINOv2 changes little across variants. Overall, purification can substantially improve otherwise weak alignment targets, but the magnitude of the benefit and the useful purifier capacity depend on the teacher. 5.3 LAP C OMPLEMENTS DIFFERENT REPA-STYLE RECIPES AND TOKENIZERS

LAP improves generation early and throughout training. By 100K steps, LAP lowers FID-10K by 14 .4 for MAE, 4.3 for AIMv2, and 4.5 for CLIP (Figure 5). These gains persist through 400K steps: MAE REPA remains close to the unaligned model, whereas MAE LAP reaches the same final FID as DINOv2 REPA (Table 29). Purification therefore improves the full training trajectory.

(2026) recommend for SiT-B/2, LAP also improves CLIP and AIMv2 by 3.7 and 4.6 FID, more than at block 8 (Appendix F.2). The gains also persist under classifier-free guidance, where MAE, AIMv2, and CLIP improve in both seeds (Table 22). SCORE PREDICTS PURIFICATION BENEFIT 

C0 predicts which teachers benefit from LAP-L. Across 13 encoders evaluated under matched protocols, higher raw-target C0 is associated with larger FID gains from LAP-L (Figures 6 and 11). 9Preprint The Spearman correlation is ρ = 0 .94 at 100K steps (FID-10K, one-sided permutation p ≈ 3.0 ×

10 −6) and 0.79 at 400K steps (FID-50K, p = 0 .0010 ). After controlling for raw-target FID, the corresponding partial rank correlations remain 0.83 and 0.69 , indicating that raw-target generation quality alone does not explain the association. MAE illustrates why this diagnostic is useful: its raw-target C0 = 0 .93 is close to the trained projector’s cosine score of 0.957 , so much of its alignment score is attainable without using the hidden state. These results support C0 as a cheap diagnostic for linear-purification benefit under this recipe. The separate pre-specified eight-encoder test shows the same direction (Appendix H.2). 

C0 does not explain the full benefit of LAP. For MAE, LAP-L and LAP-N both reduce C0 to about 

0.01 –0.02 , yet LAP-N achieves better generation quality. This suggests that suppressing the shared direction measured by C0 is not sufficient to explain LAP-N’s additional gain. Instead, LAP-N removes substantially more target variation: it eliminates 35% of held-out target variance while pre-serving class information. On the 200-class pooled linear probe, the LAP-N residual reaches 68.7% accuracy, compared with 66.1% for raw MAE and 68.5% for the LAP-L residual (Appendix E). To-gether, these results suggest that LAP-N removes additional image-dependent local content beyond the shared component captured by C0.

## 6 CONCLUSION 

Prediction utility and alignment benefit need not coincide. Our fixed-encoder interventions motivate LAP, which removes locally predictable content before alignment and makes otherwise weak targets such as MAE effective. LAP complements existing REPA recipes and latent tokenizers without changing inference. Variational information bounds clarify what the alignment objective rewards, while the constant-readout score C0 measures alignment attainable without consulting the hidden state and empirically identifies teachers likely to benefit from LAP-L under our shared recipe. 

Limitations. Evaluation centers on SiT and ImageNet. Larger-model checks use two seeds and cross-domain runs are short. Gains depend on the teacher and recipe. LAP does not guarantee a semantic residual, and C0 does not provide calibrated gain predictions. REPRODUCIBILITY STATEMENT

The experimental use score holds the trained network fixed and measures its response to an in-tervention. In contrast, Uopt compares optimal predictors under an isotropic Gaussian model. In this special model, evaluating the full-state optimal predictor on (I − P )h also attains the retained-subspace optimum. That equivalence need not hold for a nonlinear denoiser. Experimental use scores need not be nonnegative or bounded by the full-layer effect in every possible setting. The model has no learning dynamics. Adding an auxiliary task cannot change h, and an unrestricted optimal velocity predictor already uses all available information. Consequently, these propositions do not establish gradient competition, semantic content, or the safety of residual alignment during training. Those interpretations must be assessed using the experiments. The accompanying verification script checks the covariance identities, the rank condition, and or-thogonal, shared, and oblique subspace examples. It also compares the analytical errors with held-out Monte Carlo predictions using 4 × 10 5 samples. These checks verify the implementation of the algebra, rather than adding empirical evidence about diffusion models.

Deterministic hidden states. Since h is a function of (xt, e ), Equation (17) gives I(h; z) ≤I (xt, e ); z, with equality whenever that function is injective. Local versus full-latent conditioning. Proposition 4 conditions each token only on its predictor’s local input Si, while h can carry information from outside that neighborhood. Conditioning instead on the full clean latent would remove this interpretation. To see why, let c be the class label and assume the forward noise and label-dropout randomness are independent of the clean data. At fixed

The local sum in Equation (18) avoids conditioning away all image input, but it can still be small when a local neighborhood already predicts its teacher token. It also changes when the neighborhood changes. The finite receptive field and capacity of dϕ define an operational target transformation, without guaranteeing a semantic residual (Section 6). 

Residualization is not conditional independence. A residual can remain predictable from its local input. For example, consider one target token with local input S uniform on {− 1, 0, 1} and target 

z = ( S2, 1) . Since S and S2 are uncorrelated, the least-squares affine prediction is (2 /3, 1) and 

r = ( S2 − 2/3, 0) . Reading h = S achieves cosine 1 through g(h) = ( h2 − 2/3, 0) , while the best constant direction attains only 1/3, even though I(h; z | S) = 0 . This example illustrates limited predictor capacity and is not a claim about a trained diffusion model. A conditional information bound therefore does not establish that the residual loss ignores local content. LAP’s selectivity comes from its predictor class and must be assessed empirically. 

No account of harm. Neither the information bounds nor the predictive-score identity establishes that redundant content harms generation. Explaining why the predictable-only target can be worse than no alignment requires an argument about optimization or model capacity, which this analysis does not provide.

This supplies a formal connection to information beyond those neigh-borhoods. The bound alone does not establish such a separation in the learned representation: a raw-target decoder also admits a token-wise conditional bound, and residuals can remain locally predictable. The substantive change is the target supplied to the restricted projector. C.11 SEED COVERAGE AND UNCERTAINTY 

Core generation comparisons use two independent training seeds. Slash-separated pairs give seeds 0 and 1. A lone generation value is a single evaluated run unless identified as a mean. Diagnostic coverage is specified separately. The range between two seeds describes the observed variation and is not a confidence interval. The dev-200 REPA pair, 1551 .2/1518 .6, gives a sample standard deviation s = |1551 .2 −

1518 .6|/√2 ≈ 23 .0. Adding the repeat at 1553 .3 gives s ≈ 19 .5. Even this one additional run changes the estimate. Variation can also depend on the configuration. Under an independent, equal-variance approximation, the standard error of the difference between averages of n and m runs is sp1/n + 1 /m . With only two runs per configuration, this approxima-tion does not justify precise significance claims or equivalence tests. We therefore report individual values, compare consistent directions across seeds and metrics, and avoid ranking closely perform-ing methods. Evaluating another metric on the same generated images checks sensitivity to the feature space. Drawing new samples checks sampling variation. Evaluating intermediate checkpoints shows the training trajectory. None of these replaces independent training seeds. LAP-L uses the batchwise affine fit in Appendix D. This appendix describes LAP-N’s frozen nonlin-ear predictor, its validation and selection, and its interaction with other alignment transformations. Both variants subtract local predictions, but their fitting protocols and predictor inputs differ. 39 Preprint E.1 ARCHITECTURE AND VALIDATION CRITERIA 

The predictor operates on the 16 × 16 patch grid. It consists of a k × k convolution from 16 to 512 channels, GELU, a 1 × 1 convolution from 512 to 512 channels, GELU, and a final 1 × 1

convolution to the encoder dimension D. We fit it with mean-squared error for 20K steps using batch 256, AdamW at learning rate 10 −3, and a cosine learning-rate schedule. We use weight decay 

10 −4 and 500 warmup steps. A seed-0 permutation reserves 4,096 images for validation and fits the predictor on the remaining images. Fitting takes approximately two A100-hours per predictor. Locality refers to the latent-token grid, not a strict receptive-field bound in image pixels. On held-out data, we define the explained-variance score 

ρ = 1 − E∥z − dϕ(Xp)∥2

E∥z − ¯z∥2 . (66) This score measures prediction error relative to a constant mean-token predictor. It is an operational measure of removable local predictability, not a semantic-content estimate. We assess class-information retention with linear softmax probes fit on 21,000 images and evalu-ated on the 4,096 images held out from purifier fitting. Using the same seed-0 permutation, probe fitting takes indices 8192:29192 and evaluation takes 0:4096 ; their image IDs are disjoint. We test mean-pooled features and token features, sampling eight positions per image with replacement and averaging their logits at evaluation. Features are centered by their own fitting-set mean and scaled by the raw target’s fitting-set per-dimension standard deviation plus 10 −6. Probes use full-batch Adam with learning rate 10 −2 and weight decay 10 −4 for at most 2,500 iterations; an improvement below 

10 −4 in cross-entropy between 500-iteration checks triggers early stopping. The linear-residual ref-erence is formed in extraction batches of 128 images. These batches can straddle probe subsets, so its affine coefficients are not restricted to the probe-fit images; the class-probe fitting and evaluation image IDs remain disjoint. The implemented selection rule compares each residual to the linear-residual target and to the com-ponent removed by the predictor. We additionally compare pairwise patch-token cosine matrices on 512 development images. The images are the last 512 entries in dataset order and were not explicitly excluded from puri-fier fitting. ## G AN ENCODER -F REE ALIGNMENT CONTROL 

This experiment asks whether a residual target from the diffusion model’s own teacher can provide guidance comparable to a pretrained encoder. We call the residual variant alignment to residual context (ARC) and compare it with an otherwise identical raw self-teacher target. G.1 CONSTRUCTION AND EVALUATION 

The student’s block-8 readout is aligned to an EMA teacher’s layer-10 hidden state. The teacher processes an independently noised copy of the same image at t = 0 .25 . For ARC, we remove the part of the teacher state predicted by local clean and noised patches using an affine fit. ARC-raw skips this removal. Both variants then standardize each feature dimension across batch tokens. Residualization is the manipulated variable. Both variants use dev-200 and batch 64. The alignment coefficient increases linearly from zero to 

0.5 over the first 10K steps. We evaluate the pre-specified 25K, 50K, 75K, and 100K checkpoints. In Table 23, the residual-minus-raw difference ranges from −17 .7 to +5 .6 across the trajectory, without a consistent advantage for residualization. H.2 PRE -S PECIFIED ENCODER SWEEP AND AN EXTERNAL CHECK OF C0

Motivation. The five teachers of Table 1 order the LAP-L gain by raw C0, but C0 and raw-target quality are correlated under the paper’s own hypothesis, since a large shared direction is what makes a raw target easy to match without image-specific computation. Five points cannot separate the two. We therefore (i) test C0 against the published results of Singh et al. (2026) and (ii) run a pre-specified sweep of eight further encoders. 

External check. We measured C0 (dev-200, protocol of Appendix C.8) for 37 encoders, includ-ing every encoder of Singh et al. (2026) that we could load (Table 27 lists those used below; the full screen is released with the code). Against their published SiT-XL/2 results at 100K steps ( 25 

encoders; their appendix bar-chart labels, two of which differ slightly from their tables), raw C0

has Spearman correlation 0.81 with REPA FID and 0.70 with the gain from REPA to iREPA (both 

p < 10 −3), but the partial rank correlation of the gain with C0 given REPA FID is −0.13 (p = 0 .55 ). Their SiT-B/2 400K table ( 16 encoders) gives 0.54 , 0.49 , and 0.23 (p = 0 .03 , 0.06 , 0.38 ), and their SiT-L/2 table ( 8 encoders) 0.25 , 0.24 , and 0.10 . The shared direction thus predicts which teachers align poorly and gain most from their spatial normalization, but in published data it cannot be sep-arated from teacher quality. Their gain also includes a convolutional projector, which their ablation credits with 0.5–7.4 FID on its own. 

Sweep design. Eight encoders were selected by a rule specified before their generation results were available (the design and amendment accompany the code): candidates were stratified by raw C0

into four bins with two encoders each, preferring, within a bin, encoders off the C0-versus-quality diagonal (using iREPA’s REPA FID as the quality proxy) and then size variants of families already in Table 1; encoders above one billion parameters were excluded for cost. Each is trained with the raw target and with LAP-L, which needs no fitted purifier, for 100K steps on ImageNet-1k with the base recipe and two seeds, and evaluated with the Inception FID-10K protocol of Figure 5. ## I COMPUTATIONAL RESOURCES 

Runs use one A100 unless noted; the REPA-family baseline, CLIP, and tokenizer runs used H100 or A100 GPUs. In the base batch-64 recipe, a 400K-step ImageNet-1k run takes approximately 18–36 hours, depending on the encoder. A 100K-step development run takes about nine hours, and a 50K-step run takes about three to four and a half hours. Fitting one purifier takes about two hours. During diffusion training, LAP-N also evaluates the frozen predictor once per batch in FP32 without gradients. The predictor is discarded for sampling. The experiments do not include matched wall-clock or peak-memory benchmarks, so per-step convergence gains do not establish an advantage in total compute. Standard 50K-image sampling takes approximately eight to ten A100-hours per model at sampling batch 64. The subsequent ADM metric calculation runs on CPU. Image-disjoint validation, includ-ing target extraction and probe fitting, averaged about 40 seconds per frozen checkpoint on one H100 over nine checkpoints. Batch sizes denote images per optimizer update. For the raw-target and LAP-N SiT-L/2, batch-256 runs with MAE and DINOv2, seed 0 uses one GPU with four accumulation steps of 64 images, whereas seed 1 uses four GPUs with 64 images each and no accumulation. The larger-regime LAP-L runs at effective batch 256 (SiT-B/2, L/2, and XL/2) use four GPUs with 64 images each; the development-scale batch-256 LAP-L runs instead use one GPU with four accumulation steps of 64. LAP-L forms targets on the current local batch, without gathering features across GPUs or accumulation steps. In every reported LAP-L training run, each affine fit therefore uses 64 images, or 64 × 256 token rows. Optimizer steps, EMA updates, and checkpoint counts advance after the effective-batch update.

## K RELATED WORK 

K.1 WHERE AND WHEN REPRESENTATIONS GUIDE GENERATION 

REPA aligns intermediate diffusion features with pretrained visual representations (Yu et al., 2025). Related approaches change where that guidance enters the pipeline. REPA-E jointly tunes the VAE and diffusion model (Leng et al., 2025), while representation autoencoders use pretrained encoder features as the generative representation space (Zheng et al., 2026). SRA and LayerSync derive guidance from the model’s own representations or layers (Jiang et al., 2026; Haghighi et al., 2026). U-REPA extends alignment to U-Net architectures (Tian et al., 2025); its multi-resolution matching has no counterpart in the SiT backbone used here, so we do not compare against it. Other methods change the timing or strength of supervision. HASTE combines early-stopped align-ment with attention-map supervision (Wang et al., 2025b), and VA-REPA adapts alignment using the variance structure of flow matching (Yang et al., 2026). TARO studies timestep-adaptive align-ment for video-to-audio generation (Ton et al., 2025). These approaches motivate studying when alignment helps, whereas our interventions ask which content within a fixed target is useful. K.2 TRANSFORMING THE TARGET OR MATCHING OBJECTIVE 

The most directly related work is iREPA (Singh et al., 2026). It compares 27 visual encoders and argues that spatial structure is more predictive of generation performance than global classification accuracy. It also modifies target normalization and the projection head. Our question concerns locally predictable content within a fixed encoder. The interaction experiments in Appendix E test whether the corresponding operations and LAP-N offer additional benefits when combined. They do not establish that spatial structure and local predictability are mutually exclusive explanations. PixelREPA diagnoses a regression shortcut in pixel-space image transformers and introduces a masked transformer adapter (Shin et al., 2026). Spectrum Matching studies spectral properties and applies a difference-of-Gaussians transformation to REPA targets (Ning et al., 2026). SARA and structural representation alignment modify how representation structure is matched (Chen et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2026). These works show that target processing and the matching objective can matter beyond encoder identity. Guidance can also come from the generative pipeline. AHPA uses timestep-dependent hierarchical VAE priors (Min et al., 2026), and SRA 2 uses VAE representations (Wang et al., 2026). Repre-sentation entanglement introduces representation guidance within the generative architecture (Wu et al., 2025). Our method keeps the pretrained encoder fixed and changes the target supplied to the existing alignment loss. K.3 MECHANISTIC ANALYSIS AND DISTILLATION 

AG-REPA uses causal layer interventions in audio flow matching and distinguishes stored informa-tion from contribution to the velocity prediction (Zhang et al., 2026). This is an important precedent for separating decodability from functional contribution. Our study focuses on target content and an auxiliary prediction intervention in image generation, rather than selecting alignment layers. 62 Preprint REED develops a theoretical framework for representation guidance and training curricula (Wang et al., 2025a). Our linear-Gaussian analysis has a narrower purpose: to illustrate why decodability and predictive contribution can differ. It does not replace a theory of diffusion optimization. Knowledge-distillation methods such as task-aware layer-wise distillation and variational informa-tion distillation adapt what a student learns from a teacher (Liang et al., 2023; Ahn et al., 2019). Their objectives are not equivalent to the clean-latent prediction utility measured here. Likewise, linear concept-erasure methods remove specified decodable information under explicit linear cri-teria (Ravfogel et al., 2020; Belrose et al., 2023). LAP shares the idea of selective removal. Its affine and nonlinear local predictors define operational residuals, without guaranteeing removal of all reconstruction information. Finally, representation regularization can help without an external encoder (Wang & He, 2025). The limited self-teacher result in Appendix G should therefore be read as a result for that construction, not a general argument against encoder-free guidance.
