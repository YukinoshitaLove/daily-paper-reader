Abstract
Generative-prior-based super-resolution (SR) methods leverage implicit knowledge
learned from large-scale models to enhance low-resolution inputs. However, such
priors are inherently stochastic and optimized for diverse generation, conflicting
with the deterministic, high-fidelity reconstruction required for SR. It is also tied
to the base model, costly to update, and prone to hallucinations as real-world data
evolves. To address these limitations, we introduce WorldSR, a novel framework
that augments SR with explicit, entity-aligned visual world knowledge. WorldSR
automatically decomposes the input into semantic entities and performs entity-wise
retrieval of structurally and semantically aligned images from a large-scale external
corpus. For efficiency and reliability, we incorporate a memory mechanism to reuse
previously retrieved results and a multi-step filtering process to refine candidate
references. The selected references as world knowledge are then integrated through
a novel multi-reference super-resolution model, which provides fine-grained con-
straints for detail synthesis and structural recovery. Experiments on real-world SR
benchmarks show that WorldSR consistently improves structural fidelity and visual
quality, outperforming existing generative super-resolution methods. Our results
suggest that entity-level grounding in external visual world knowledge provides a
simple and effective complement to model-bound generative priors.

Introduction
Image super-resolution aims to reconstruct high-resolution images from low-resolution inputs by
recovering lost fine details. Recent advances leverage powerful generative priors embedded in large-
scale text-to-image models [1–5], which are trained on massive datasets and encode rich general
visual knowledge in their parameters. This implicit knowledge enables recent works [6–13] to
enrich low-resolution inputs with visually plausible details, substantially boosting perceptual quality.
However, these globally applied generative priors lack explicit grounding in real-world semantics,
often leading to hallucinated details [14–16] that are visually plausible but semantically incorrect.
To mitigate hallucinations, recent approaches explore multimodal large language models (MLLMs)
to provide textual descriptions of low-resolution inputs [17–19]. These descriptions are then used
to synthesize semantically aligned images that serve as references to guide the diffusion-based
super-resolution process, improving semantic consistency to some extent. Nevertheless, such caption-
derived references are prone to out-of-distribution artifacts, which can introduce structural inconsis-
tencies and degrade the fidelity of the final super-resolved outputs.
More direct approaches [20–23] leverage reference images with high visual similarity (e.g., different
views of the same subject or scene) to guide the super-resolution process. In domain-specific settings
such as face restoration [24–27], multiple reference images are sometimes employed to aggregate
complementary facial details. However, these methods typically rely on a single or a small set of
pre-collected references, significantly restricting their ability to exploit diverse and complementary
visual cues. Additionally, in complex scenes with multiple semantic entities, the lack of region-
level correspondence between inputs and references often leads to structural inconsistencies and
hallucinated details, thereby limiting their robustness in real-world scenarios.
In recent years, retrieval-augmented approaches have shown strong effectiveness in mitigating
hallucinations by grounding model predictions in external, dynamically retrieved knowledge, thereby
improving factual consistency in MLLM-based vision-language tasks [28–31]. However, directly
extending this paradigm to image super-resolution is non-trivial, as existing methods typically rely
on holistic image-level similarity, which is insufficient for handling complex scenes with multiple
semantic entities and fine-grained region-level variations. To address this limitation, we propose
WorldSR, an MLLM-empowered agent-style framework that reformulates image super-resolution as
an entity-level grounding problem using explicit visual world knowledge.
Specifically, WorldSR first leverages an MLLM to parse the input low-resolution image and identify
real-world entities, enabling structured semantic understanding. It then retrieves up-to-date reference
images from web sources and local databases conditioned on these entities, and further filters them
based on entity-level visual similarity and quality. Finally, a world-grounded super-resolution model is
carefully designed to integrate the selected multi-reference knowledge into the reconstruction process.
To improve efficiency, a memory verifier is introduced to cache previously retrieved results and
avoid redundant queries. Through iterative refinement, WorldSR progressively reduces hallucinations
and produces outputs that are better aligned with real-world semantics. We evaluate WorldSR on
multiple benchmark datasets and demonstrate consistent improvements over state-of-the-art super-
resolution methods in visual quality, structural fidelity, and semantic consistency. Both quantitative
and qualitative results show that WorldSR is effective in reconstructing well-defined objects and
real-world landmarks in complex scenes, while significantly reducing hallucinated content.

Prior-Based Image Super-Resolution
Conventional image SR methods [32–36] typically rely on local structural priors and realistic degra-
dation simulation pipelines [35] to tackle the challenges associated with real-world super-resolution.
However, these methods often face significant difficulties in generating high-frequency details that
faithfully reproduce natural textures. StableSR [6] was the first to incorporate generative priors by
adapting a pretrained text-to-image (T2I) model for super-resolution, substantially improving the high-
frequency content of SR outputs. Following this, generative priors from pretrained T2I models have
been widely integrated into contemporary SR architectures [7–13], marking a significant advancement
in SR research. Nevertheless, with these generative capabilities, the issue of hallucinations in SR
results has become increasingly prominent. To mitigate this challenge, CoSeR [17] proposes guiding

2.2
Agentic Systems for Visual Tasks
Agentic systems have revolutionized visual task processing by introducing autonomous reasoning,
tool-use capabilities, and multimodal collaboration to address complex visual challenges. In the
realm of high-level visual generation and editing, JarvisArt [37] leverages LLM-powered agents to
automatically interpret user instructions and operate Lightroom tools for professional image retouch-
ing, while Gen-Searcher [38] equips agents with internet search capabilities to autonomously gather
up-to-date text-image pairs for refreshing training datasets. Additionally, Unify-Agent [39] proposes
an agentic image generation pipeline of five steps: world-grounded image synthesis, encompassing
prompt understanding, multimodal evidence searching, grounded recaptioning, and final synthesis.
Regarding low-level vision tasks, RestoreAgent [40] leverages an agent to autonomously assess the
type and extent of degradation in input images and then executes a restoration pipeline using multiple
restoration models. Similarly, AgentIR [41] follows this design paradigm, but further introduces a
rollback mechanism to determine the optimal restoration strategy. JarvisIR [42] constructs a large-
scale instruction-response dataset to fine-tune the underlying MLLM, thereby improving overall
agentic system robustness. Furthermore, MAIR [43] improves restoration performance by scaling
up the number of agents to optimize pipeline scheduling, and 4KAgent [44] expands its restoration
model zoo to further enhance the agent’s capability to upscale images to 4K resolution. All the
aforementioned works [40–44] utilize agents to invoke specific neural networks for targeted image
restoration. In contrast, this paper presents an agent-style framework to acquire world knowledge and
mitigate generative hallucination issues in super-resolution.

WorldSR
3.1
Overview
We introduce WorldSR, a novel agent-style grounded image super-resolution framework that inte-
grates world knowledge search to effectively mitigate hallucinations and improve super-resolution
quality. As shown in Fig. 2, the overall pipeline is an iterative process that undergoes multiple
refinements. It consists of five key components, each responsible for a specific task: planning,
verification, searching, filtering, and super-resolution.
The Agentic ROI Planner (Sec. 3.2) leverages a MLLM to analyze the image content and identify
potential regions of interests (ROIs) that connect visual entities to real-world knowledge. The
Memory Verifier (Sec. 3.3) queries the Memory Bank using the extracted ROI knowledge bounding
boxes. If any entity memory is missing, the subsequent search and filter module will be triggered.
If all entities are hit, the SR result generated in the previous round is directly returned as the final
output. The World Knowledge Searcher (Sec. 3.4) retrieves relevant candidate images for all ROI
regions from web and local databases. The World Knowledge Filter (Sec. 3.5) further selects the
optimally matched image from these candidates images via image quality assessment, query-aware
similar content extraction, and reference-guided ranking. Ultimately, the World-Grounded Image
Super-Resolution Model (Sec. 3.6) injects all optimally matched real-world knowledge into the
low-resolution input to generate a realistic and world-grounded super-resolution output.
3.2
Agentic ROI Planner
The Agentic ROI Planner module comprises two core processes: entity-level semantic reasoning and
the extraction of corresponding ROI bounding boxes. Specifically, we first leverage an MLLM as
the core perception tool to perform entity-level semantic reasoning on the input image I, enabling a
comprehensive understanding of real-world knowledge entities. Subsequently, the MLLM produces a
set of bounding boxes {Bi}N
i=1, leveraging its inherent capability for open-world entity discovery.
These generated bounding boxes are constrained by a carefully designed system prompt to ensure
that the regions are precise and their contents are grounded in real-world knowledge. Guided by these
bounding boxes, N corresponding image patches {Pi}N
i=1 are automatically cropped from the input
image for the downstream Memory Verifier and World Knowledge Searcher. However, extracting
real-world entities from low-resolution images remains challenging. To mitigate this, we introduce an
iterative refinement loop that leverages the intermediate SR results as visual feedback to progressively
suppress hallucinations, as shown in Fig. 2. Formally, the input image I at each step n is either the
initial low-resolution image I(0) or the rolled-back intermediate super-resolution output I(n−1) from
the preceding iteration. For simplicity, we omit the iteration superscript (n) hereafter.
3.3
Memory Verifier
Since our framework employs an iterative refinement loop strategy to progressively suppress hal-
lucinations, the Agentic ROI Planner may generate overlapped ROIs across successive iterations.
To manage these overlapping regions and avoid redundant computations, we propose the Memory
Verifier. This module includes two core components: a Memory Bank for retaining historical context
and a Decision-Making Controller for adaptive action routing.
Memory Bank
The Memory Bank is designed to cache previously explored regions along with
their associated contextual knowledge. Specifically, it stores ROI bounding boxes together with their
corresponding retrieved and filtered world knowledge, organized as a set of paired entries (Bj, R∗
j).
Decision-Making Controller
The Decision-Making Controller determines the appropriate action
routing for the current iteration. It begins by calculating the Intersection over Union (IoU) between the
newly proposed query ROI Bi and all ROIs stored in the Memory Bank. If the maximum IoU score
s∗
i exceeds a pre-defined threshold sIoU, the controller bypasses redundant operations and outputs
its corresponding knowledge R∗
i . Otherwise, the controller routes the queried image patch Pi to the
World Knowledge Searcher to execute a new retrieval operation. Furthermore, if all newly queried
ROIs result in memory hits, it indicates that the iterative refinement has converged. Accordingly, the
system terminates the loop and outputs the SR image generated in the preceding iteration.

World Knowledge Searcher
The World Knowledge Searcher is responsible for retrieving external real-world knowledge relevant
to the queried image, providing grounded visual evidence to mitigate hallucinations during super-
resolution. To ensure both comprehensive and highly accurate information retrieval, we adopt a
dual-engine retrieval strategy that integrates web-scale search and local knowledge base search.
Web-scale Search
For the image patch Pi output by the Agentic ROI Planner, a web-scraping tool
Fweb automatically uploads the image patch Pi to the public internet and utilizes reverse image search
technology to retrieve the top-Kweb most similar images {Rj

3.5
World Knowledge Filter
Since the retrieved images originate from diverse sources and acquisition pipelines, the candidate
set often exhibits various forms of image degradation, partially irrelevant content, and insufficient
semantic relevance. To address these issues, we introduce a dedicated World Knowledge Filter
module to select the optimal reference image R∗
i that best aligns contextually with the query patch
Pi. Note that R∗
i and its associated ROI bounding box Bi are stored as a pair in the memory bank.
Quality-Aware Candidate Filtering
To maintain the high visual quality of the candidate images,
we introduce a Quality-Aware Candidate Filtering strategy, which employs dedicated image quality
assessment models for reliable and robust quality scoring. Specifically, we utilize RALI [46] and
Q-Insight [47] to compute image quality scores. Any candidate image receiving a score lower than
the predefined threshold sfilter from either model is strictly discarded.
Irrelevant Region Pruning
Some candidate images contain regions unrelated to the query. To
remove such noise, we leverage an MLLM to localize query-relevant regions in each candidate image.
Specifically, the query image, candidate image, and a grounding prompt are jointly fed into the model
to predict bounding boxes that align semantically with the target. The candidate images are then
cropped accordingly, and the resulting patches are forwarded to the subsequent module.
Semantic-Aware Reference Ranking
To further select the world knowledge that best matches
the query semantics, we employ a Semantic-Aware Reference Ranking module to evaluate the
remaining candidates. Specifically, a multimodal ranking model (e.g., Qwen3-VL-Reranker [48]) is
used to compute semantic similarity scores between the query and the cropped candidates. The top-1
candidate, ranked by semantic similarity, is then selected as the optimal reference R∗
i .

to further enhance the visual quality of the retrieved references. Specifically, the model follows

Reference-Guided Super-Resolution
To effectively transfer rich textures from the refined refer-
ence images to the target LR image, we formulate the reference-guided super-resolution as unified
sequence modeling. Specifically, the LR image ILR and the refined references { ˆR∗
i }N
i=1 are encoded
into latent tokens, flattened, and concatenated along the sequence dimension. Since the degraded
LR tokens and high-quality reference tokens originate from distinct data distributions, sharing iden-
tical model parameters restricts the ability of the one-step super-resolution network to effectively
disentangle these domains. To bridge this domain gap, we introduce a dual-LoRA architecture (see
Fig. 3) to explicitly decouple domain-specific representations. A dedicated LR-domain LoRA (θLR)
processes LR tokens, while a reference-domain LoRA (θRef) encodes reference tokens. By projecting
the tokens into a unified feature domain, the proposed architecture enables unconstrained global
interactions. This design allows the model to implicitly learn spatial correspondences and effectively
inject high-fidelity world knowledge from multiple reference images into the LR representation,
resulting in more faithful texture reconstruction and structure preservation.
Implementation Details
To efficiently assign the proper LoRA adapters to arbitrary tokens, our
proposed dual-LoRA architecture in Reference-Guided Super-Resolution model follows the adapter
modulation strategy of OmniControl [50]. We adopt a one-step training strategy where only LoRA
parameters are optimized. The training objective combines pixel-level and perceptual supervision,
including the MSE loss L2, the OC-EA-DISTS perceptual loss [13] Lp, and a DINOv3-based [51]
adversarial loss Lg. To improve robustness under varying reference conditions, we randomly sample
the number of reference images from 0 to Nmax during training. In the zero-reference case, all
reference inputs are dropped, and only the LR-domain LoRA parameters θLR are updated. More
implementation details are provided in Sec. A.2 of the supplementary material.

Experiments
4.1
Experimental Settings
Across all our experiments, we adopt Qwen3.5-35B-A3B [52] as the unified multimodal vision-
language model, and leverage the state-of-the-art text-image-to-image model, Flux.2-dev [49], to
serve as a generative prior for our world-grounded image super-resolution model. The QLoRA [53]
training strategy is employed for GPU memory efficiency. Specifically, we first quantize the pretrained
DiT backbone to 4-bit precision using the NF4 data type, and subsequently apply three LoRA adapters,
θRQR, θLR and θRef, with a rank of 16. We utilize the Prodigy optimizer [54] to train the models for 30k
iterations with a batch size of 16 on 8 NVIDIA H800 GPUs. We empirically set sfilter = 3.8 across
all experiments. Following widely adopted metrics in previous generative prior-based image super-
resolution methods [9, 8, 23, 55], we employ three reference-based metrics (PSNR, SSIM [56], and

Comparison Methods
We compare our method with several representative state-of-the-art (SOTA)

super-resolution models, namely Real-ESRGAN [35], OSEDiff [10], PISA-SR [11], OMGSR [13],
and HYPIR [12], which perform restoration without external references. The agentic-based restoration
method AgenticIR [41] is also compared. We further benchmark against reference-based super-
resolution approaches, including SeeSR+ReFIR [20] and AdaRefSR [23], to assess the capability
of transferring details from reference images. For the Face dataset, we also include state-of-the-art
reference-based face super-resolution models, InstantRestore [24] and Ref-LDM [25].
4.2
Comparison with State-of-the-art Methods
Quantitative Comparison
We evaluate our method against current SOTA methods on five datasets,
with the quantitative results detailed in Tab. 1. Overall, the proposed method establishes a new SOTA
across all no-reference metrics, outperforming previous methods by a substantial margin. While our
method does not achieve the best performance with the reference-based metrics on the Bird and Face
testset, this is a deliberate design choice rather than a limitation. Specifically, WorldSR prioritizes the
effective transfer of world-grounded knowledge and high-level semantic consistency over pixel-wise
fidelity. Our design leads to slightly lower PSNR/SSIM scores, as these metrics favor pixel-perfect
reconstruction and may penalize semantically meaningful modifications introduced by reference
guidance. Such a trade-off is well-recognized in generative restoration literature [8]. Importantly, our
method demonstrates clear advantages in preserving identity and recovering fine-grained details. For
instance, on the Face dataset, our method surpasses the second-best face-specific reference-based
approach by 6.4% in terms of the IDS metric. This validates the effectiveness of our approach
in faithfully transferring reference-guided textures and facial ID features into the final SR images.

Effectiveness of the World-Grounded Image Super-Resolution Model
For a fair comparison,
we adopt the same world knowledge as references to compare our proposed world-grounded SR
model with Ada-RefSR [23] and SeeSR-ReFIR [20]. As illustrated in Fig. 5 and Tab. 2 (Rows
1–3), our designed network achieves the best quantitative performance and significantly mitigates
hallucinations, generating visual results that are highly consistent with real-world structures.
Effectiveness of Individual Modules
We present a comprehensive analysis of each individual
module in the WorldSR pipeline. The corresponding results are detailed in Fig. 5 and Tab. 2 (Rows
4–7). Ablating the Iterative Refinement Loop or Agentic ROI Planner leads to misalignment of
certain entities with real-world knowledge, accompanied by a slight drop in non-reference metrics.
Removing the World Knowledge Searcher makes the model independent of any external information,
which introduces severe hallucinations in visual results. While removing the World Knowledge Filter
does not introduce visual hallucinations, it significantly degrades quantitative performance.
Effectiveness of the World Knowledge Searcher
We analyze our World Knowledge Search
module on the Face and Bird datasets, as shown in Fig. 5 and Tab. 2 (Rows 8–13). As a baseline, we
remove all external knowledge and super-resolve the LR input with the RQR model. Building upon
this, the introduction of local knowledge effectively mitigates hallucination artifacts and enhances
quantitative performance. Furthermore, when equipped with web-scale search capabilities, our
method gains access to up-to-date, high-quality, and real-world-aligned reference images, ultimately
boosting overall super-resolution performance and ensuring greater image fidelity.
5
Conclusion
In this paper, we present WorldSR, a novel framework that augments generative-prior-based super-
resolution with explicit, entity-aligned visual world knowledge to mitigate hallucinations and improve
reconstruction fidelity. Unlike conventional SR methods that rely on implicit, model-bound priors,
WorldSR performs entity-level semantic reasoning to identify key objects, retrieves high-quality
reference images through world knowledge search, and filters them via semantic-aware ranking to
obtain the most relevant guidance. A newly designed world-grounded SR model then incorporates
these references to produce realistic and consistent outputs. To further address the challenges of low-
resolution inputs, we design an iterative refinement mechanism with a memory verifier, progressively
improving results and suppressing hallucinations. Extensive analysis shows that WorldSR reduces
hallucinations and generates semantically faithful high-resolution images, highlighting the value of
grounding super-resolution in external visual world knowledge.
Limitation and Potential Negative Impacts
While WorldSR benefits from rich explicit visual
world knowledge, its performance depends on accurate fine-grained entity decomposition, where
errors may propagate through retrieval and reconstruction. Additionally, the method is constrained by
the coverage of the large-scale external visual corpus and may struggle with rare, unseen or long-tail
entities. Finally, integrating multiple references introduces ambiguity, and the retrieval and refinement
stages incur non-negligible computational cost. Overall, we do not identify direct negative societal
impacts beyond general limitations of image super-resolution systems.

A.1
Prompts
In WorldSR, we leverage MLLMs to perform precise reasoning over real-world entities and their
corresponding bounding boxes within the Agentic ROI Planner (Sec. 3.2). We further employ
MLLMs to localize query-relevant regions in each candidate image within the Irrelevant Region
Pruning module of the World Knowledge Filter (Sec. 3.5). To ensure consistent and structured
outputs, we design a carefully crafted system prompt that constrains the model to follow a predefined
JSON schema. In this section, we present the complete set of prompts used in WorldSR, as follows:

System Prompt for Irrelevant Region Pruning
# ROLE & OBJECTIVE
You are a **Visual Similarity Assessment and Object Localization Agent**.
You will receive two images in the following order:
1. **Image A (Reference Object Image)** — showing a primary object.
Image A may be **low-quality or degraded**, such as being blurry, noisy,
low-resolution, partially occluded, or affected by compression artifacts.
2. **Image B (Search Image)** — containing zero or more objects.
Your task is to identify the object in Image B that is **most visually similar**
to the primary object in Image A, and to localize it.
15


# SIMILARITY-FIRST POLICY
- Always attempt to find the **most visually similar object** in Image B.
- When Image A is low-quality or degraded:
- Focus on **stable and high-level visual cues** such as overall shape,
color distribution, proportions, and distinctive structural features.
- Do not rely excessively on fine-grained textures or sharp details
that may be unreliable in Image A.
- Visual similarity should be judged mainly by object appearance:
shape, color patterns, overall structure, and distinctive visual features.
- Differences in background, environment, lighting, pose, or viewpoint are acceptable.
- If no object is strongly similar, still select the closest candidate
and assign a low similarity score.
# LOCALIZATION
- Output a tight and reasonable bounding box around the selected object in Image B.
- The bounding box may be **slightly expanded beyond the exact object boundary**
to ensure the full object is covered.
- Bounding box format: `[x, y, w, h]`, normalized to `[0.0, 1.0]`
- Round all values to **3 decimal places**.
- Ensure the bounding box stays within image boundaries.
# SIMILARITY SCORE (0–100)
- `"confidence"` represents **visual similarity**, not a binary decision.
- The score must be an **integer between 0 and 100**.
- When Image A is degraded, the score should reflect **overall perceptual similarity**
rather than penalizing missing fine details.
- Use the full range consistently:
- 90–100: Nearly identical appearance
- 70–89: Clearly similar
- 50–69: Moderately similar
- 30–49: Weak similarity
- 1–29: Very weak similarity
- 0: No relevant object present
# MATCH FLAG
- Set `"match_found": true` if any relevant object exists in Image B.
- Set `"match_found": false` only if Image B contains no relevant object at all.
- Downstream systems should primarily rely on `"confidence"`.
# NO-OBJECT CASE
If no relevant object exists in Image B:
- `"match_found": false`
- `"bbox": [0.0, 0.0, 0.0, 0.0]`
- `"confidence": 0`
# OUTPUT CONSTRAINTS
- Output **only** a valid JSON object.
- Do not include explanations, reasoning, or additional text.
- Do not use markdown formatting.
# OUTPUT FORMAT

{
"match_found": true,

The Reference Quality Refinement and Reference-Guided Super-Resolution models are trained
independently, yet they share the same training data and training objective. Following the training set
configuration established in AdaRefSR [23], we construct the training dataset consisting of degraded
LR images, their high-resolution (HR) ground truths, and multiple corresponding reference images.
To further augment the reference images, we additionally apply random spatial erasing and color
jittering during their synthesis process. Notably, reference images are excluded during the training
of the Reference Quality Refinement module. We use a fixed general prompt, “High-resolution, 4K
and All-In-Focus Image”, as the input text for the text encoder, which produces the corresponding
text tokens. The same prompt is used for all experiments to provide general quality guidance. For
the entire world-grounded image super-resolution model, we use the one-step training strategy in
which only the LoRA adapters are updated. The overall training objective combines L2 loss L2,

More Inference Details
We report the GPU memory usage and inference time of each module
across our experiments conducted on the H800 GPU in Tab. 3. However, since the processing time
depends on the specific LR image, we provide only the approximate average inference time per image
on the RealLQ250 dataset. The Agentic ROI Planner is powered by the Qwen 3.5-35B-A3B [52]
model operating in a deep thinking mode, which inherently increases the overall inference time. The
processing time of the World Knowledge Searcher is heavily dependent on network speed and server
performance. Thus, scaling up the number of required search images substantially increases the total
processing time. The World Knowledge Filter module incurs significant computational overhead, as
it sequentially applies IQA models for quality assessment, an MLLM (Qwen 3.5-35B-A3B [52]) for
region extraction, and a large multimodal VLM (Qwen3-VL-Reranker [48]) for similarity ranking.

substantially reduces inference time while maintaining comparable performance, showing a favorable
trade-off.
A.3
Evaluation Metrics
Following widely adopted metrics in previous generative prior-based image super-resolution meth-
ods [9, 8, 23, 55], we employ three reference-based metrics (PSNR, SSIM [56], and LPIPS [57])
and three no-reference metrics (CLIPIQA [58], MUSIQ [59], and MANIQA [60]) to quantitatively
evaluate the performance of our model. Furthermore, we additionally utilize reasoning-based Image
Quality Assessment (IQA) models, namely RALI [46] and Q-Insight [47], which are optimized
through reinforcement learning and exhibit exceptional generalization capabilities. For the Face
dataset, we further compute the Identity Similarity (IDS) metric [61, 62] using the face recognition
model ArcFace [63]. This metric effectively quantifies the reference information transfer capability
by measuring the identity consistency between the super-resolution outputs and ground-truth images
in the discriminative embedding space of ArcFace. Following [14], we also evaluate the hallucination
score (HS) by feeding LQ, GT, Ref, and SR images into a large vision-language model.

NeurIPS Paper Checklist
1. Claims
Question: Do the main claims made in the abstract and introduction accurately reflect the
paper’s contributions and scope?
Answer: [Yes]
Justification: The abstract and introduction accurately reflect our paper’s contributions and
scope.
Guidelines:
• The answer [N/A] means that the abstract and introduction do not include the claims
made in the paper.
• The abstract and/or introduction should clearly state the claims made, including the
contributions made in the paper and important assumptions and limitations. A [No] or
[N/A] answer to this question will not be perceived well by the reviewers.
• The claims made should match theoretical and experimental results, and reflect how
much the results can be expected to generalize to other settings.
• It is fine to include aspirational goals as motivation as long as it is clear that these goals
are not attained by the paper.
2. Limitations
Question: Does the paper discuss the limitations of the work performed by the authors?
Answer: [Yes]
Justification: The limitations have been included in conclusion section.
Guidelines:
• The answer [N/A] means that the paper has no limitation while the answer [No] means
that the paper has limitations, but those are not discussed in the paper.
• The authors are encouraged to create a separate “Limitations” section in their paper.
• The paper should point out any strong assumptions and how robust the results are to
violations of these assumptions (e.g., independence assumptions, noiseless settings,
model well-specification, asymptotic approximations only holding locally). The authors
should reflect on how these assumptions might be violated in practice and what the
implications would be.
• The authors should reflect on the scope of the claims made, e.g., if the approach was
only tested on a few datasets or with a few runs. In general, empirical results often
depend on implicit assumptions, which should be articulated.
• The authors should reflect on the factors that influence the performance of the approach.
For example, a facial recognition algorithm may perform poorly when image resolution
is low or images are taken in low lighting. Or a speech-to-text system might not be
used reliably to provide closed captions for online lectures because it fails to handle
technical jargon.
• The authors should discuss the computational efficiency of the proposed algorithms
and how they scale with dataset size.
• If applicable, the authors should discuss possible limitations of their approach to
address problems of privacy and fairness.
• While the authors might fear that complete honesty about limitations might be used by
reviewers as grounds for rejection, a worse outcome might be that reviewers discover
limitations that aren’t acknowledged in the paper. The authors should use their best
judgment and recognize that individual actions in favor of transparency play an impor-
tant role in developing norms that preserve the integrity of the community. Reviewers
will be specifically instructed to not penalize honesty concerning limitations.
3. Theory assumptions and proofs
Question: For each theoretical result, does the paper provide the full set of assumptions and
a complete (and correct) proof?
Answer: [N/A]
27


Justification: Our paper does not include theoretical results.
Guidelines:
• The answer [N/A] means that the paper does not include theoretical results.
• All the theorems, formulas, and proofs in the paper should be numbered and cross-
referenced.
• All assumptions should be clearly stated or referenced in the statement of any theorems.
• The proofs can either appear in the main paper or the supplemental material, but if
they appear in the supplemental material, the authors are encouraged to provide a short
proof sketch to provide intuition.
• Inversely, any informal proof provided in the core of the paper should be complemented
by formal proofs provided in appendix or supplemental material.
• Theorems and Lemmas that the proof relies upon should be properly referenced.
4. Experimental result reproducibility
Question: Does the paper fully disclose all the information needed to reproduce the main ex-
perimental results of the paper to the extent that it affects the main claims and/or conclusions
of the paper (regardless of whether the code and data are provided or not)?
Answer: [Yes]
Justification: We have include the implementation details in the Experiments Section and
the Supplemental materials.
Guidelines:
• The answer [N/A] means that the paper does not include experiments.
• If the paper includes experiments, a [No] answer to this question will not be perceived
well by the reviewers: Making the paper reproducible is important, regardless of
whether the code and data are provided or not.
• If the contribution is a dataset and/or model, the authors should describe the steps taken
to make their results reproducible or verifiable.
• Depending on the contribution, reproducibility can be accomplished in various ways.
For example, if the contribution is a novel architecture, describing the architecture fully
might suffice, or if the contribution is a specific model and empirical evaluation, it may
be necessary to either make it possible for others to replicate the model with the same
dataset, or provide access to the model. In general. releasing code and data is often
one good way to accomplish this, but reproducibility can also be provided via detailed
instructions for how to replicate the results, access to a hosted model (e.g., in the case
of a large language model), releasing of a model checkpoint, or other means that are
appropriate to the research performed.
• While NeurIPS does not require releasing code, the conference does require all submis-
sions to provide some reasonable avenue for reproducibility, which may depend on the
nature of the contribution. For example
(a) If the contribution is primarily a new algorithm, the paper should make it clear how
to reproduce that algorithm.
(b) If the contribution is primarily a new model architecture, the paper should describe
the architecture clearly and fully.
(c) If the contribution is a new model (e.g., a large language model), then there should
either be a way to access this model for reproducing the results or a way to reproduce
the model (e.g., with an open-source dataset or instructions for how to construct
the dataset).
(d) We recognize that reproducibility may be tricky in some cases, in which case
authors are welcome to describe the particular way they provide for reproducibility.
In the case of closed-source models, it may be that access to the model is limited in
some way (e.g., to registered users), but it should be possible for other researchers
to have some path to reproducing or verifying the results.
5. Open access to data and code
Question: Does the paper provide open access to the data and code, with sufficient instruc-
tions to faithfully reproduce the main experimental results, as described in supplemental
material?
28


Answer: [No]
Justification: The source code is not currently open-sourced due to policy restrictions.
Guidelines:
• The answer [N/A] means that paper does not include experiments requiring code.

public/guides/CodeSubmissionPolicy) for more details.

reproduce the results. See the NeurIPS code and data submission guidelines (https:

• The authors should provide instructions on data access and preparation, including how
to access the raw data, preprocessed data, intermediate data, and generated data, etc.

7. Experiment statistical significance
Question: Does the paper report error bars suitably and correctly defined or other appropriate
information about the statistical significance of the experiments?
Answer: [No]
Justification: The error bars are not reported because it would be too computationally
expensive.
Guidelines:
• The answer [N/A] means that the paper does not include experiments.
• The authors should answer [Yes] if the results are accompanied by error bars, confidence
intervals, or statistical significance tests, at least for the experiments that support the
main claims of the paper.
• The factors of variability that the error bars are capturing should be clearly stated (for
example, train/test split, initialization, random drawing of some parameter, or overall
run with given experimental conditions).
• The method for calculating the error bars should be explained (closed form formula,
call to a library function, bootstrap, etc.)
• The assumptions made should be given (e.g., Normally distributed errors).
29


• It should be clear whether the error bar is the standard deviation or the standard error
of the mean.
• It is OK to report 1-sigma error bars, but one should state it. The authors should
preferably report a 2-sigma error bar than state that they have a 96% CI, if the hypothesis
of Normality of errors is not verified.
• For asymmetric distributions, the authors should be careful not to show in tables or
figures symmetric error bars that would yield results that are out of range (e.g., negative
error rates).
• If error bars are reported in tables or plots, the authors should explain in the text how
they were calculated and reference the corresponding figures or tables in the text.

• The conference expects that many papers will be foundational research and not tied
