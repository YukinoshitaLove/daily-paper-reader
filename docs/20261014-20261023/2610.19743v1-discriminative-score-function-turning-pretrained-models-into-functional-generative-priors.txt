Abstract
Discriminative models are trained with task-specific objectives such as classifi-
cation, detection, or representation learning, yet their practical utility depends
on generalizing beyond the training task to unseen samples from the same do-
main. This suggests that a discriminative model may retain an implicit trace of
the data manifold beyond its task labels. We ask whether this trace can be ex-
posed as a generative signal without task-specific guidance. We introduce the
Discriminative Score Function (DSF), which extracts an image-space generative
field from the loss-gradient geometry of a pretrained discriminative model. DSF
provides a training-free update rule that moves images toward model-consistent
visual regions without retraining or task-specific targets. We apply DSF to diverse
pretrained discriminative models, including ResNet classifiers, DETR detectors,
and DINOv2 ViT encoders. DSF generates unconditional images from noise and
supports conditional guidance, editing, inpainting, and explanation through the
same field. DSF-generated samples also provide effective zero-shot quantization
calibration when real images or labels are unavailable, preserving feature geometry
better than data-free calibration baselines. These results show that discriminative
training leaves a generative functional signal for synthesis and calibration. Project

Introduction
“What I cannot create, I do not understand.”
— Richard P. Feynman [14]
Discriminative models are trained through task objectives such as classification, detection, or repre-
sentation learning, but their usefulness depends on something broader: they must generalize to unseen
samples from the same visual domain. This generalization suggests that a discriminative model
captures data-manifold structure beyond the specific task used to train it. Such structure is closely
related to the goal of generative modeling, which is to move toward or sample from the data manifold
itself. If this view is correct, the model should be able to induce unconditional generation without a
task prior or a specified generation objective. We ask whether this implicit manifold structure can be
exposed and reused as a standalone generative objective in a general way.
Existing discriminative-to-generative methods typically rely on external or task-specific handles,
including class labels, activation objectives, reconstruction losses, architecture-specific statistics such
as BatchNorm priors, or auxiliary generative models [17, 31, 51]. These handles already specify
what should be produced, reconstructed, or activated. As a result, the generated images do not by
themselves show that the discriminative model exposes the data manifold; they also do not provide
a general solution for arbitrary discriminative models. This limitation is especially visible beyond

classification, where detectors and self-supervised encoders may not provide class probabilities or
labels at all. A suitable answer therefore needs a label-free interface that avoids task objectives and
external generators while applying across diverse pretrained discriminative models.
We answer this question by introducing the Discriminative Score Function (DSF), a functional
generative field extracted from a pretrained discriminative model. DSF converts the model’s loss-
gradient geometry into an image-space update direction. Unlike prior work, DSF evaluates whether
a candidate image lies on the model-implied manifold by measuring its alignment in functional
space. This alignment moves the image toward visual structure that is consistent with the pretrained
model itself. In this operational sense, DSF turns a discriminative model into a generative model and
provides a solution for discriminative models operating in continuous, high-dimensional image space.
The key challenge is that the manifold structure captured by a discriminative model is implicit.
We therefore reinterpret score-based generation as motion toward a data manifold, and replace the
inaccessible image-space manifold distance with a functional distance induced by the Loss Tangent
Kernel (LTK) [5], a loss-aware variant of NTK [24]. This yields a computable field in which
candidate images are updated according to their alignment with the pretrained model’s loss-gradient
geometry. As a result, DSF provides a functional score induced by a discriminative model. With a
label-free augmentation-invariance surrogate in LTK functional space, the same formulation applies
to classifiers, detectors, and self-supervised models under one rule across architectures.
We show that DSF is a general solution for reusing pretrained discriminative models as generative
models, and that this solution is practically useful. DSF generalizes across both architecture and
objective, spanning convolutional classifiers such as ResNet [19], detection transformers such as
DETR [3], self-supervised ViT encoders such as DINOv2 [11, 41], and contrastive vision-language
models such as CLIP. In all cases, DSF generates model-consistent images and support conditional
generation, editing, inpainting, and explanation through a unified interface. Finally, we show that
DSF is not only a generative interface but also a practical tool for model compression. In zero-shot
quantization, DSF-generated images provide effective calibration data without access to real data
or labels, outperforming existing data-free alternatives and demonstrating the broader utility of the
model-induced generative geometry.

Related works
Score-based generative models
generate samples by approximating the probability distribution of
the target data [23, 46]. Since direct approximation is infeasible, a diffusion process is used: noise
transforms the target distribution into a known distribution such as Gaussian [21, 45], and the reverse
process recovers the target. In essence, these models iteratively transition from a Gaussian distribution
to the data manifold. Training such models requires delicate methodologies, including learning a
score function instead of modeling the full distribution [20], and handling data differently at each
time step. Noisy classification also enables class-conditional generation along the trajectory [10].
Discriminative models as generative models
are uncommon outside cases where discrimination
and generation already overlap, such as autoregressive next-token prediction. In standard vision,
discriminative models do not define an image-generation process, while generative models are often
reused for discriminative tasks [28, 34]. DSF studies the inverse direction: extracting a generative
field from a fixed pretrained discriminative vision model. Prior attempts to generate images from
classification models mainly follow two directions: gradient-based visualization and maximal-margin
reconstruction. Gradient-based methods, including DeepDream [38, 40], feature visualization, and
related energy-based approaches, optimize images to activate a specified class, neuron, or energy.
Meaningful synthesis typically requires additional handles such as architecture-specific priors [51],
frequency regularization [13], total variation [37], auxiliary generative models [16], classifier fine-
tuning [50], or joint generative-discriminative training [42]. Maximal-margin methods instead use
stationarity conditions and links between support vector machines and neural networks to reconstruct
or generate training data [2, 17, 36]. These methods often rely on overfitted classifiers, simple
architectures, or small datasets [15]; even higher-resolution extensions [31] remain confined to
classification. DSF targets a broader setting: extracting a label-free functional field from arbitrary
pretrained discriminative vision models for generation in continuous image space.

Probing and reusing learned structure.
Related studies expose learned structure through different
interfaces. Deep Edge Filter [30] filters deep features to improve generalization, while CSF [33]
probes compositional semantic responses to identify model lineages. The representation-space
analysis in Do Not Think About Pink Elephant! [22] examines why negated concepts can still appear
in generated images. These studies analyze features or observable responses; DSF constructs an
image-space generative field. Template Infilling [32] likewise explores capabilities through inference
design, but starts from a diffusion language model already trained for generation.
Position of our paper.
(1) Motivation: We expose and reuse the data-distribution-like visual
structure hidden inside task-trained discriminative models. (2) Framework: DSF extracts a label-free
functional score from loss-gradient geometry, giving fixed classifiers, detectors, and self-supervised
encoders a unified generative interface. (3) Evidence: Generation results show that the interface
works across architectures and objectives, while zero-shot quantization shows that DSF provides a
practical calibration solution without real data, labels, or auxiliary generators in this setting.

Preliminaries
Kernel Methods
are conventional machine learning techniques where a bivariate kernel k(·, ·)
represents the inner product in a high-dimensional feature space, i.e., k(x, y) = ⟨ϕ(x), ϕ(y)⟩.
Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Space (RKHS) guarantees the existence of the space ϕ where the
closeness of ϕ(x) and ϕ(y) implies the closeness of x and y. Fixing one input gives the functional
object k(·, x).
Neural Tangent Kernel
tries to explain the generalization ability of deep learning models using
conventional kernel methods. It suggests that for every deep learning network being trained, a
corresponding kernel exists. NTK Θ(x, x′) is defined by the gradients of the given deep learning
model, Θ(x, x′) = ∇θf(x)⊤∇θf(x′), reflecting how the model trains in the input space. Because
NTK ignores loss dynamics, Loss Tangent Kernel (LTK) [5] adds loss derivatives:
K(x, x′) = ∇fℓ(f(x; θ))⊤Θ(x, x′)∇fℓ(f(x′; θ)).

In this section, we derive the Discriminative Score Function (DSF) as a
functional generative field induced by a pretrained discriminative model.
First, Sec. 4.1 reviews the geometric view of score-based generation as
motion toward a data manifold. Sec. 4.2 then introduces a training map
that sends datasets to trained models, allowing us to discuss the manifold
through functional objects. Finally, Sec. 4.3 replaces this ideal map with
an LTK-based surrogate and defines the DSF update that moves candidate
images toward model-consistent visual structure through the same field.

Experiments
Experimental setup.
We use public pretrained ResNet [19], DINOv2 [41], and DETR [3] models
without post-training. Images are optimized directly from random noise using the DSF update.
For generation experiments, we report unconditional and conditional samples across classification,
self-supervised representation learning, and object detection models. For zero-shot quantization, we
use DSF-generated samples as calibration data for a fixed DINOv2 feature extractor and compare
them with real-image and data-free calibration sources. Appendix C provides optimization details,
architecture-specific layer choices, and quantization protocols used in all reported experiments.

5.1
Unconditional Generation
Unconditional generation provides the most direct test of whether a fixed discriminative model
induces a usable generative field. In this setting, no class label, text prompt, target activation, real
image, or external generator is provided. We initialize images from random noise and optimize them
only with the DSF update in Eq. (12). If the optimized samples exhibit coherent visual structure, the
signal must come from the pretrained discriminative model’s functional geometry.
We evaluate this setting across three representative discriminative objectives: classification with
ResNet, object detection with DETR, and self-supervised representation learning with DINO. All
models are public pretrained checkpoints and are used without modification. Fig. 3 shows the
resulting unconditional samples. DSF produces model-dependent visual structure in all cases: the
classifier tends to generate object-centric patterns, DINO produces broad natural-image structure
from its self-supervised representation, and DETR yields scene-like samples with multiple object-like
regions. These results demonstrate that DSF exposes a reusable functional prior across pretrained
discriminative models with different architectures and objectives, indicating that discriminative
training can leave a functional-space direction without generator training from fixed models.

Generative Properties
Beyond synthesis and calibration, DSF acts as a reusable image-space field induced by a fixed
discriminative model. We study qualitative uses of this field: since the update acts directly on
images without labels, masks, or a trained generator, it supports editing, restoration, explanation, and
sampling heuristics with minimal task-specific changes.
6.1
Image Editing
What a diffusion model learns is the reverse process of converting an image to pure noise. Based
on this property, editing tasks in diffusion include sending the object image to a noise-like image
and retrieving it back. The condition is injected into this process in the form of masking [8]. For
the case of DSF, what it conducts is not the denoising process but the direct estimation of manifold

6.2
Image Inpainting
Image inpainting visually restores damaged or omitted parts of a target image. To enable this, a model
must generate new content according to the surrounding context; diffusion models are suitable for
inpainting tasks in this sense. Similarly, DSF can serve as an effective inpainting model. To evaluate
its performance, we masked random image regions with empty patches. Although DSF is calculated
on the entire image, updates were restricted to masked areas. To guide restoration, we introduced a
simple boundary-consistency regularizer. With this straightforward setup and direct DSF application,
the missing regions were successfully restored, as shown in Fig. 6.
6.3
Global Explanation
DSF can also be used as a global explanation tool. Unlike naive feature visualization, which often
follows adversarial activation gradients, DSF constrains optimization by a functional prior induced by
the pretrained model. This distinction is important: if DSF produces more complete and contextually
plausible images than activation maximization, then the generated samples can reveal dataset biases
and feature entanglements in the model’s learned representation. For example, when applied to a
ResNet trained on ImageNet, DSF repeatedly generates “analog clock” images showing 10:10 and
“plate” images with contextual elements such as wine glasses and dishes. These outputs suggest that
DSF can expose common co-occurrences and biases encoded by the discriminative model, while
comparison with [13] separates manifold-guided visualization from activation-driven artifacts.

6.4
Unlocking the potential of DSF through diffusion model
DSF is an image-space generative field, so standard sampling heuristics can be used to improve
the trajectory. Our base experiments use a deliberately simple fixed-step update; diffusion models,
however, typically rely on step-size schedules and intermediate noise injection to correct bias and
improve sample quality [20, 45]. We therefore apply the simplest version of these ideas: linear
step-size decay with proportional noise injection. As shown in Fig. 8, unconditional generation from
DINOv2 [41] exhibits fewer artifacts and more coherent structures than the fixed-step baseline in
Fig. 3. This improvement shows that DSF can benefit from better samplers and suggests a direct path
for improving discriminative-model-induced generation with no change to the field.
7
Conclusion
In this paper, we introduced the Discriminative Score Function (DSF), a training-free mechanism
for extracting a generative field from a pretrained discriminative model. DSF does not require
labels, data, or architectural modification; instead, it uses the model’s own loss-gradient geometry for
synthesis. Our experiments show that this geometry can support generative ability to discriminative
ones. Practically, DSF-generated samples improve zero-shot quantization calibration, indicating that
the induced field captures model-specific activation structure rather than merely producing visual
artifacts. The broader implication is that discriminative models can serve as more than predictors:
they can expose reusable generative structure tied to their learned representations.
Limitations.
DSF is viewed as a training-free generative interface induced by a discriminative
model, not a replacement for high-fidelity diffusion generators. Its gradient proxy can depend
on architecture and training recipe, and parameter-gradient updates make sampling costlier than
first-order image optimization. Broader domains, and stronger samplers remain future work.

A
Impact Statement
DSF is a methodological contribution for reusing fixed pretrained discriminative vision models as
training-free generative priors. A positive impact is that it can reduce the need to train separate
generative models for some inspection, calibration, and diagnostic workflows, which may lower
computation and data-access requirements. Its zero-shot calibration use case is especially relevant
when real calibration data cannot be shared because of privacy, licensing, or deployment constraints.
The same capability also has risks. Generated images should not be interpreted as faithful recon-
structions of the original training set or as complete descriptions of the data manifold; they are
model-induced visualizations and may amplify biases, spurious correlations, or artifacts encoded in
the pretrained model. If used to synthesize calibration or debugging data, DSF samples may inherit
undesirable correlations from the source model. We therefore frame DSF primarily as an analysis
and deployment-support tool, report its limitations, and do not release a new high-risk pretrained
generator or dataset.

Parameter direction as a data-free aggregate-gradient proxy
Here we spell out why the nor-
malized alignment objective contains a plus between g and θ. The LTK alignment in Eq. (10) is
naturally written using a dataset-level aggregate gradient. This matters in finite-width networks:
individual parameter-gradient features are sample-dependent and anisotropic, so practical kernel-
gradient constructions use an averaged or aggregate direction as the representative dataset-side object.
Let LD(θ) = P
i ℓ(f(ζi; θ)) be the empirical training objective and let gD(θ) = ∇θLD(θ). If the
training set were available, gD could be estimated directly. DSF instead uses the pretrained parameter
direction as a data-free proxy for the aggregate descent direction used by DSF.
Gradient descent updates parameters by
θt+1 = θt −ηtgD(θt),
(14)
so the learned parameter displacement records accumulated negative empirical loss-gradient direc-
tions. Thus, a candidate image whose loss-gradient is consistent with the training-induced aggregate
should produce an update direction −g(x) that points along θ, up to normalization and layerwise
scaling. This is why we minimize ∥gℓ/∥gℓ∥+ θℓ/∥θℓ∥∥: making gℓand θℓopposite is exactly
making the update direction −gℓalign with the model-side descent trace θℓ. The sign is fixed by the
gradient-descent update convention rather than treated as a tunable hyperparameter.
This anchor also agrees with two standard views of trained discriminative models. In a regularized
stationary point, the first-order condition has the following form at convergence in practice
∇θLD(θ) + ρθ ≃0,
(15)
which directly identifies the empirical loss-gradient aggregate with −θ up to the regularization scale,
or equivalently identifies the empirical update direction with θ. In separable or homogeneous settings,
implicit-bias and directional-convergence analyses show that gradient-based training drives the weight
direction toward margin- or training-gradient-induced directions even when the norm continues to
grow [25, 36, 52]. DSF does not claim that −θ equals every sample gradient, nor that the equality is
exact for every modern pretrained architecture. Rather, −θ is a model-side proxy for the unavailable
dataset-average gradient representer. The practical assumption is that the learned parameter direction
summarizes a useful trace of training-induced aggregate descent.
About θ proxy
The construction above is analogous to several standard ways of replacing many
sample-dependent objects by one aggregate representative. In kernel mean embeddings, a distribution
is represented in an RKHS by the mean feature vector µD = |D|−1 P
i ϕ(ζi), and evaluating a new
point uses ⟨ϕ(x), µD⟩rather than a single sample feature [39, 43]. The LTK view makes the same
16


move in a loss-gradient feature space: the feature is ϕθ(z) = ∇θℓ(f(z; θ)), and the dataset-side
object is the aggregate ¯gD = |D|−1 P
i ϕθ(ζi) [5, 24]. This is natural in finite-width networks, where
individual gradient features vary across samples, while the aggregate serves as the representative
direction used by kernel alignment.
DSF differs only in that the training set is unavailable. We therefore need a model-side replacement
for ¯gD. The final parameters provide such a replacement because training trajectories are built by
accumulating dataset-gradient updates. This is the same general source of information exploited by
trajectory-matching dataset distillation, where synthetic data are optimized to reproduce parameter
trajectories [4], and by training-data reconstruction analyses, where stationarity and weight decay
reveal data-dependent gradient information in trained weights [2, 17]. Under the ideal ℓ2-regularized
stationary condition, the replacement is exact up to scale; outside this ideal case, the parameter
direction is an operational aggregate-gradient proxy, not a pointwise equality. Layerwise normal-
ization and late-layer selection preserve this directional interpretation while reducing sensitivity to
architecture-specific scale, outliers, and parameterization across architectures in practice.
Layers and normalization
The subset S is chosen for computational tractability, and late layers
provide the high-level representation geometry used by DSF. We use layerwise normalized ℓ1
alignment to compare directions robustly under layer-scale variation and outlier coordinates.

Optimization and compute
Images are optimized directly as trainable tensors and clamped to
[0, 1] after updates. The augmentation policy used in the main sweeps includes crop, additive noise,
color jitter/drop, and cutout. The crop operation preserves the original tensor size by padding the
cropped view back to the input resolution. For scheduled runs, learning-rate and TV multipliers use
the same piecewise schedule: 1.0, 0.8, a linear decay from 0.8 to 0.25, a linear decay from 0.25 to
0.05, and then 0.05, with thresholds scaled by the run-specific step-rate. The Langevin-noise schedule
uses 5 · 10−4, 2 · 10−4, a linear decay to 10−4, and finally 10−5. Because Step 3 in Algorithm 1
preserves the parameter-gradient graph, DSF requires mixed second-order differentiation: the first
derivative forms gℓ= ∇θℓℓaug, and the image update differentiates the alignment objective through
gℓ. In A6000 sweeps with batch size 50, this costs about 1.4–1.7 seconds per image.
