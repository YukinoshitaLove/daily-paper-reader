## Abstract 

Emotional image editing requires more than applying af-fective filters or modifying predefined visual factors: an effective edit must discover what a particular image can afford for a target emotion. Existing affective image ma-nipulation (AIM) methods, including recent agentic vari-ants, still largely operate within bounded strategy spaces— retrieving, selecting, or refining edits from predefined fac-tor taxonomies, knowledge libraries, or conventional edit-ing templates. As a result, they improve execution control but often miss image-specific, context-grounded strategies that humans immediately recognize as emotionally mean-ingful. We introduce EmoScope, a multi-agent framework that reframes the problem from “how should I edit?” to “what can I edit?” EmoScope first discovers an image-specific editable space through emotion-conditioned affor-dance reasoning, then uses a semantic hierarchy of an-chors, variables, and context to navigate the consistency– expressiveness tradeoff before executing and verifying the edit. Because the plan is expressed as image-specific affor-dances rather than retrieved templates, this design also nat-urally exposes the editing strategy as an interactive surface, letting users refine emotional expression at the plan level rather than at the pixel level. Through a large-scale hu-man evaluation across all eight Mikels emotion categories (4,693 valid responses across 1,824 pairwise questions), humans strongly prefer EmoScope over two competitive baselines (88.1% mean preference); attribution data fur-ther shows that EmoScope selects target-emotion-adaptive strategies rather than applying a uniform template. The same affordance-level plan also supports lightweight user refinement, examined in a small interactive pilot. We addi-tionally show that classifier-based metrics (Emo-A, Emo-S) exhibit emotion-conditional blind spots toward non-stereotypical, context-grounded edits, and we present a rel-ative content–emotion preference-affinity landscape reveal-ing that EmoScope’s advantage varies systematically across image–emotion combinations. 

## 1. Introduction 

Image editing has evolved from well-defined operations— adjusting brightness, applying filters, removing blemishes—to tasks driven by abstract human inten-tions. A request like “make this image feel happier” has no single correct answer; it requires understanding the image’s visual content, the cultural connotations of its elements, and the multifaceted nature of emotional expression. This class of tasks, broadly termed affective image manipulation (AIM) [37], sits at the intersection of image editing and affective computing, demanding not just technical proficiency but visual-emotional reasoning over an open-ended space of possible edits. EmoEdit [37] constructs a hierarchical emotion factor tree decomposing affect into object, scene, action, and facial expression factors. EmoEditor [15] separates global context from local semantic regions via a dual-branch architecture. These approaches differ in mechanism—factor trees, dual branches, noise-level mappings, knowledge graphs, even test-time optimization [31]—but share a common prop-erty: the source of editing strategies is bounded by each method’s representational substrate, whether an explicit taxonomy or an implicit parameterization. A more re-cent generation of agentic AIM systems—EmoAgent [16], EmoFeedback 2 [13], Emotion-Director [12]—adds plan-ning, critique, and feedback loops, contributing significant gains in controllability and error correction; the strategies on which these loops operate, however, are still drawn from a pre-existing source (knowledge library, factor templates, or conventional editing instructions), so the loops sharpen execution within an inherited strategy space rather than en-arXiv:2607.23920v1 [cs.CV] 27 Jul 2026 SOURCE BASELINES (A MUSEMENT )

While such bounded strategy spaces enable strong ex-ecution control within predefined boundaries, they leave a substantial unexplored territory of context-grounded strategies—the editable space of each image is image-specific and far larger than any predefined factor taxonomy can enumerate. Figure 1 makes this concrete. Given a minimalist room with two empty picture frames and a sin-gle chair (hardly a typical “amusement” scene), EmoEdit applies its factor-tree taxonomy and scatters colored balls across the chair and walls, while EmoEditor pursues an amusement-classifier signal so aggressively that it loses the subject entirely. EmoScope, in contrast, identifies that the empty frames are themselves a canvas and fills them with playful animal portraits paired with a matching subject in the chair—a strategy outside the vocabulary of any factor tree, requiring an understanding of what this specific image affords. The same affordance is then used differently for other emotion targets: cursed portraits for Fear , and—most tellingly— deliberately empty frames for Sadness , where the system treats absence itself as the expressive choice. What unites these strategies across emotions is a single principle: they emerge from per-image affordance reasoning rather than retrieval, refinement, or selection within a pre-existing strategy space. Crucially, a plan–verify loop alone does not necessarily solve this failure mode if the planner still chooses among stereotyped strategy sources—it requires changing what the planner does, not just adding verification on top. This observation motivates a paradigm shift in how we approach emotional image editing. Instead of asking 

“how should I edit this image?” (selecting from prede-termined strategies), we first ask “what can I edit in this image?” —discovering the image-specific editable space 

through emotion-conditioned reasoning about the image’s visual elements. In this view, the planner’s role is not to choose the best operation from a known menu, but to con-struct the menu from the image’s affordances under a target emotion. Once the editable space is identified, a vision-language model (VLM) can generate creative, contextually grounded editing strategies within it, unconstrained by a fixed strategy source. While the idea of discovering edit-ing possibilities has been explored in general creative edit-ing [19, 26], to our knowledge we are the first to apply it to emotion-specific editing, where the target emotion fun-damentally reshapes which elements are editable and how .Formalizing this view, we identify two orthogonal re-quirements for a successful emotional edit. Consis-tency : the edited image must remain recognizable as orig-inating from the original. Critically, consistency is not monolithic—it operates at multiple levels from pixels to narrative, and relaxing it at higher levels dramatically ex-pands the strategy space. This creates a consistency– expressiveness tradeoff that prior work has not explicitly modeled: existing agentic editors verify whether an edit succeeds, but rarely formalize which kinds of consistency may be relaxed to make a more expressive emotional edit possible. Plausibility : the edit must genuinely convey the intended emotion without being so extreme as to un-dermine its own message. Together, these two constraints define the true editable space—image-specific, emotion-dependent, and far richer than prior work assumes. Moreover, we raise a question that the field has only par-tially addressed: is every image equally amenable to ev-ery target emotion? AIM-Bench [2] observes that positive emotions are generally more editable than negative ones at the model level , but the variation at the individual image level —which specific visual properties make an image more or less amenable to a particular emotional transformation— remains unquantified. Intuitively, some (image content, tar-get emotion) combinations have natural affinity while oth-ers present fundamental resistance . As an empirical proxy for this larger question, we analyze how a method’s rela-tive human-preference advantage varies across content type and target emotion, providing the first image-level map of where one method’s gains over another concentrate or fade. We present EmoScope , a multi-agent framework that operationalizes these insights (Fig. 1). Unlike other agen-tic AIM systems whose planner retrieves from a library or instantiates templates, EmoScope’s Planner generates edit-ing strategies ab initio from per-image affordances under a target emotion. A Plan Verifier , Image Editor , and Re-sult Verifier then form two iterative refinement loops that navigate the consistency–expressiveness tradeoff at runtime by falling back from ambitious strategies to safer ones when needed. In this design, verification serves a differ-ent role than in earlier agentic AIM: not to compensate for a weak strategy source, but to keep open-ended discovery grounded, plausible, and consistent. Our contributions are: 1. We formalize the consistency–expressiveness trade-off for emotional image editing and argue that prior methods—including recent agentic AIM systems— operate within bounded strategy sources that leave the broader space of context-grounded strategies unex-plored. 2. We propose EmoScope, an agentic framework whose key contribution is an affordance-discovery Planner that constructs the editable space ab initio from per-image elements (not the plan–verify loop itself, now standard in image-editing agents), plus a semantic hierarchy that operationalizes the consistency–expressiveness tradeoff at runtime; because the plan is expressed at the affor-dance level, it also serves as an interactive surface for non-experts to refine edits at the plan level rather than the pixel level (pilot study in Sec. 4.8). 3. We present the first image-level analysis of a relative content–emotion preference-affinity landscape , quanti-fying how EmoScope’s human-preference advantage over baselines varies across image content and target emotion—complementing model-level observations [2] and static emotion–scene correlations [9]. 4. Through a large-scale human evaluation ( 4, 693 valid re-sponses across 1, 824 pairwise questions), we demon-strate that (a) humans significantly prefer our edits, (b) our method selects target-emotion-adaptive edit-ing strategies whose distribution shifts predictably with the target emotion, and (c) existing automated metrics (Emo-A, Emo-S) exhibit an emotion-conditional blind spot toward non-stereotypical, context-grounded edits. 

## 2. Related Work 

2.1. Affective Image Manipulation 

Affective image manipulation (AIM) aims to edit images so that they evoke a specified target emotion. Early approaches operated at individual visual layers: Affective Image Fil-ter [29] adjusts color tones, while C2A2 [20] modifies com-pound facial expressions. Recognizing that emotion is con-veyed through multiple factors simultaneously [23], recent methods attempt multi-level editing. EmoEdit [37] con-structs a hierarchical emotion factor tree (object, scene, action, facial expression) and edits each factor indepen-dently. EmoEditor [15] employs a dual-branch architec-ture integrating global context with local semantic regions. AIEdiT [43] maps emotional factors to diffusion noise levels for coarse-to-fine editing. EmoKGEdit [42] uses a knowledge graph to guide object-level adjustments fol-lowed by global atmospheric harmonization. These methods differ substantially in their editing mechanisms—factor trees, parallel branches, noise-level mappings, knowledge graphs—but they share a common property: the vocabulary of editing strategies is bounded by the method’s representational substrate (e.g., EmoEdit’s strategies are drawn from EmoSet [34] clusters, while con-tinuous approaches like MUSE [31] and AIEdiT’s noise-level mapping define the space of possible edits implicitly through their parameterization). Our work asks whether this bounded strategy space is sufficient, and demonstrates em-pirically that it is not. Concurrent works explore complementary dimensions: generate-from-scratch (EmoGen [35], EmotiCrafter [3]), polarity-decomposed (CoEmoGen [40]), and style-based (EmoStyle [36]) lines target different tasks than in-place emotional editing. Regressor-Guided Emotion Editing [7] finds that diffusion-based methods enable semantic-level changes that optimization-based methods cannot, consistent with our thesis that higher-level strategies produce quali-tatively different emotional effects. AIM-Bench [2] pro-vides the first AIM benchmark and observes a positivity bias, hinting at the emotion-dependent editability variation we systematically investigate. 

2.2. Agent-Based and Creative Image Editing 

The plan–edit–verify paradigm for complex image editing inherits its design from the broader literature on language-model agents—ReAct [38] for interleaving reasoning with tool actions, and AutoGen [30] for orchestrating multi-agent conversations—specialised here to vision-language models. Within the image-editing setting, GenArtist [28] decomposes goals into sub-tasks with step-by-step verifi-cation. GraPE [8] and IMAGAgent [25] implement plan-execute-reflect loops. MIRA [41] uses iterative perception-reasoning-action cycles. These architectures are now well-established; we adopt the dual-loop design not as a contri-bution in itself but as the infrastructure needed to support open-ended strategy exploration. Within AIM, EmoAgent [16] is the first multi-agent framework, employing Planning, Editing, and Critic agents. While its Planning Agent performs top-k retrieval from a fixed Emotion-Factor Knowledge library—claiming to move “beyond fixed mappings”—the strategy vocabulary remains bounded by what has been observed in EmoSet. EmoFeedback 2 [13] introduces LVLM-based feedback for iterative refinement, and Emotion-Director [12] mitigates affective shortcuts via chain-of-concept reasoning. Both operate within conventional editing strategies. Our Plan-ner differs in that it generates strategies ab initio through emotion-conditioned reasoning about visual elements: in-stead of retrieving from a library, it identifies what the im-age’s elements can become in the context of a target emo-tion. In broader creative editing, Creativity-VLA [26] and CoEditor++ [19] address general creative editing from vague hints or ”what vs. how” decomposition, and Be-yond Pixels [32] transfers creative essence from a reference image via schema-driven reasoning. Our planner differs in being emotion-conditioned and reference-free, yielding emotion-specific strategies rather than general creative sug-gestions. 

2.3. Emotion Editability and Evaluation 

Automated AIM evaluation relies on emotion classifiers: Emo-A (classification accuracy) and Emo-S (confidence) measure whether edited images are classified as the tar-get emotion [16]. LPIPS [44] and CLIP-I [22] quantify perceptual and semantic distance, complementing the older pixel-level family of structural-similarity (SSIM [27]) and Fr´ echet Inception Distance (FID [10]) measures used in im-age generation more broadly. These classifier-based met-rics, trained on images where emotion is conveyed through surface-level features [23], may fail to evaluate emotion conveyed through narrative mechanisms such as concep-tual incongruity or symbolic storytelling. Recent work has begun documenting metric–human disagreement at scale: TIEdit [6] finds limited correlation between automatic met-rics and 307K human ratings for general image editing, and FED-Bench [33] reveals “lazy editing” biases in facial ex-pression metrics. However, no prior work has stratified this disagreement by target emotion within the emotion do-main. Our experiments fill this gap, revealing that classifier-based metrics systematically undervalue non-stereotypical, context-grounded edits that humans strongly prefer, with the blindness varying nearly an order of magnitude across target emotions. The question of per-image emotion editability —whether every image is equally amenable to every target emotion— has received scattered attention. EmoScene [9] analyzes static correlations between affect, scene type, and percep-tual attributes across 1.2M images, establishing that emo-tional associations vary by scene. Theoretical and pre-dictive editability work (Editing on the Generative Mani-fold [11], ELECT [14], DreamSteerer [39]) treats editabil-ity as a model–image joint property at the timestep, mani-fold, or instance level. These works collectively suggest that editability is image-dependent, but none directly quantifies how emotional editability varies across target emotions for a given source image. Our analysis provides a first empiri-cal proxy for this question by measuring how EmoScope’s relative human-preference advantage over baselines varies across image content and target emotion. 

## 3. Method 

We present EmoScope, an agent-based framework that dis-covers and exploits the full editable space of each image through emotion-conditioned reasoning about its visual el-ements, letting a general-purpose vision-language model (VLM) generate open-ended editing strategies within that space. The framework operates through two iterative loops orchestrated by four specialized agents: a Planner , a Plan Verifier , an Image Editor , and a Result Verifier (Fig. 2). 

3.1. Problem Formulation: The Editable Space 

Given an input image I and a target emotion e (e.g., amuse-ment ), we define the editable space E(I, e ) as the set of all editing operations that satisfy two constraints: 

Consistency. The edited image I′ must remain recogniz-able as originating from I. Crucially, consistency is not a single binary property but operates across multiple se-mantic levels: pixel-level (color fidelity), structural (spatial layout), identity (subject appearance), and narrative (the-matic coherence). Different levels of consistency preser-vation yield fundamentally different editing strategies. For instance, preserving only narrative consistency—the viewer understands I′ as a variation of I’s story—permits far more expressive edits than requiring pixel-level fidelity. This cre-ates a consistency–expressiveness tradeoff : relaxing con-sistency constraints expands the space of viable edits, while tightening them restricts the editor to surface-level adjust-ments. 

Plausibility. The edit must genuinely convey the target emotion e. A technically flawless edit that evokes the wrong emotion—or whose intent is too absurd to interpret—fails this constraint regardless of its visual quality. Formally, E(I, e ) = {δ | C (I, I + δ) ≥ τc ∧ P(I +

δ, e ) ≥ τp}, where C measures multi-level consistency and 

P measures emotional plausibility, with thresholds τc and 

τp that vary with the desired editing intensity. 

Contrast with prior work. Existing methods implicitly define E as a small, fixed set of editing categories predeter-mined at design time (§2). Our key insight is that E(I, e )

is image-specific and far larger than any fixed taxonomy can capture: making Vermeer’s Girl with a Pearl Earring 

more amusing might modify her expression (within tradi-tional factor trees) or replace her with a cat wearing the same earring—a semantic-level conceptual substitution that no factor tree enumerates, yet humans immediately recog-nize as both a valid edit and genuinely funny. 3.2. Semantic Hierarchy 

To operationalize the abstract C/P constraints, we discre-tise consistency C into three semantic layers (Anchors, Vari-ables, Context) with the threshold τc mapped to the editing-intensity setting (Mild / Moderate / Strong); plausibility P

is enforced separately by the Result Verifier (Sec. 3.5). The hierarchy thus defines editing boundaries —what must not be changed at each intensity—rather than prescribing what should be changed: 

Layer 1: Anchors. Core identity elements that define “what this image is”: face identity, specific landmarks, iconic objects. These are never modified unless the edit-ing intensity is Strong , ensuring identity preservation by de-fault. 

Layer 2: Variables. Primary emotional vehicles: expres-sions, gaze, posture, lighting, and character interactions. These are the default targets for emotional modulation. 

Layer 3: Context. The environmental “stage”: back-ground, weather, atmospheric conditions, and symbolic ob-jects (e.g., an empty chair, a wilting flower). These elements can be freely modified, added, or removed. This hierarchy is fundamentally different from the fac-tor trees used in prior work. Factor trees enumerate what to edit ; our hierarchy defines what not to edit . Within the unconstrained space, the Planner freely discovers any strategy that satisfies consistency and plausibility. The hierarchy also implements the consistency–expressiveness tradeoff through intensity levels: Mild restricts edits to Layer 2 micro-adjustments; Moderate permits contextual shifts across Layers 2–3; Strong unlocks Layer 1, enabling full narrative transformations. Figure 3 illustrates this lad-der on two contrasting sources and surfaces a property that emerges naturally from the affordance-conditioned design: under the same target emotion, the sub-style chosen at each intensity level is itself a function of what the source image affords.

The output EditPlan contains: an execution plan specifying per-element actions ( MODIFY , ADD , DELETE ,or KEEP ) with layer assignments; negative constraints; and a natural-language final instruction prompt writ-ten as a director would instruct an artist. 

3.4. Plan Verification 

Before execution, the Plan Verifier audits the EditPlan 

across five dimensions: (1) pixel grounding —do elements marked for modification actually exist in I? Plans refer-encing non-existent elements are rejected as hallucinations; (2) narrative logic —does the proposed story follow natu-rally from the scene’s visual elements? (3) emotion purity —are drift risks adequately addressed? (4) intensity align-ment —is edit scope appropriate for the requested level? (5) anchor protection —are Layer 1 elements properly pre-served? Rejected plans return to the Planner with action-able feedback, forming the Plan-Refine loop (≤ Kp=5 iter-ations). 

3.5. Image Editing and Result Verification 

The Image Editor receives the verified plan’s 

final instruction prompt and applies it to I

using Qwen-Image-Edit [21] as the execution engine. Operating on natural-language instructions enables the open-ended strategies from our Planner without requiring specialized editing modules for each strategy type. The Result Verifier then evaluates the edited image I′ by comparing it with both I and the plan, assessing: (1) emo-tion achievement —does I′ evoke the target emotion and sub-style? (2) drift detection —has the image slipped into a neighboring emotion? (3) identity preservation —are an-chors intact? (4) generative quality —are there artifacts? Based on the assessment, the verifier issues one of three de-cisions: A PPROVE , R ETRY PIXELS (re-edit with adjusted prompts), or R EPLAN STRATEGY (restart from the Plan-ner, e.g., pivoting from Narrative to Atmospheric when the story fails to materialize). This forms the Edit-Refine loop 

(≤ Ke=3 iterations). 

3.6. Dual-Loop Architecture 

The complete pipeline chains the Plan-Refine and Edit-Refine loops described above. The separation of planning from execution is critical: unlike single-pass methods that conflate strategy selection with image generation, our de-sign ensures that editing strategy is grounded in actual im-age content before any pixels are modified, and that exe-cution failures are distinguished from strategic failures for targeted recovery.

Different evaluations require different scales (Table 2), and we keep these splits clearly sepa-rated throughout the paper: automated metrics use the full quantitative benchmark ( 7, 352 outputs per method); human pairwise preference uses a stratified survey subset ( 114 im-ages, 1, 824 pairwise questions); the metric–human corre-lation analysis joins these two at the (image, target emo-tion, baseline) level ( 1, 697 paired records); ablation re-uses the EmoEdit benchmark; and qualitative figures addition-ally draw on a 34 -image ArtEmis painting subset for cross-regime illustration only. Throughout the paper, every re-ported N corresponds to one of these splits and is named accordingly. 

Baselines. We compare against two state-of-the-art meth-ods: EmoEdit [37] (CVPR 2024), which constructs a hier-archical emotion factor tree and edits each factor indepen-dently; and EmoEditor [15] (ICCV 2025), which uses a dual-branch architecture integrating global context with lo-cal semantic regions. 

Baseline scope and editor-confound control. The two quantitative baselines are released as complete diffusion-based AIM systems with their own trained editors and we evaluate them using their official code; Emo-Scope uses Qwen-Image-Edit-2511 [21]. A fair head-to-head against agentic AIM systems (EmoAgent [16], EmoFeedback 2 [13], Emotion-Director [12]) at scale would also require unifying the generative backbone, which we leave to future work; instead we test the planning archi-tecture’s contribution within our pipeline through the “Ed-itor only” ablation (A5; Sec. 4.6), which runs raw Qwen-Image-Edit on the bare prompt “Make this image evoke [emotion]” with no planning, no verifier loops, and no agent at all. Full EmoScope substantially outperforms this same-editor baseline, evidence that the gains within our pipeline are not explained by the Qwen editor alone. A qualitative system-level comparison against EmoAgent on four repre-sentative cases shows the same planning difference at the per-image level: generic agentic AIM tends to apply broad affective transformations, whereas EmoScope grounds its edits in image-specific affordances. 

Implementation. All four agents (Planner, Plan Veri-fier, Image Editor controller, Result Verifier) use Gemini-3-Flash as the VLM backbone, accessed via the OpenRouter API; the image editor is Qwen-Image-Edit-2511 [21] in FP8-quantised form (using the publicly released checkpoint of [21]), invoked with 40 inference steps and classifier-free guidance scale 4.0. Default iteration limits are Kp = 5 

(plan-refine) and Ke = 3 (edit-refine), with seed unfixed (system seed) and editing intensity Moderate unless oth-erwise stated. Inference runs on a single NVIDIA RTX-5090 GPU. For reproducibility, we use Qwen-Image-Edit as the image editor and keep the Planner, Plan Verifier, and Result Verifier prompts fixed across all benchmark runs; dataset licences follow the original EmoEdit, EmoEditor, and ArtEmis releases.

Interpretation. The four findings are consistent with a single mechanism: current emotion classifiers behave as 

stereotype matchers , not as semantic understanders. Three implications follow: (i) per-emotion calibration is essential before automated metrics can substitute for human evaluation; (ii) the classifi-cation paradigm itself is limiting—a classifier trained on la-belled stereotypes cannot, in principle, recognise edits that succeed through non-stereotypical means; (iii) a research direction opens for VLM-based evaluators that can reason about why an image evokes an emotion, not just whether its features match a stereotype. The findings above jointly support H3 .

4.5. Relative Emotion Editability: Content– Emotion Preference Affinity 

We investigate a question that, to our knowledge, has not been directly addressed in prior work: is the relative human-preference advantage of our method uniform across image content and target emotion, or does it vary systematically? 

We group source images by visual content type (portrait, landscape, painting, architecture, urban, indoor, animal, ob-ject, action) and compute our Q1 preference rate for each (content type, target emotion) pair, yielding a 9 × 8 land-scape across 72 cells. We emphasise upfront that this mea-sures relative preference of ours over baselines, not the ab-solute editing difficulty of each cell—a low cell value can reflect either “hard for everyone” or “baseline happens to do well here”. We approximate absolute editability through this comparative landscape; an absolute Likert measure is left to future work. Figure 7 reveals a clear content–emotion preference affinity landscape : EmoScope’s advantage varies systemat-ically with both image content and target emotion. Excite-ment is the easiest target (95% mean, ≥ 89% on 8/9 content types); portraits (91% avg.) and urban scenes (92%) are uniformly editable. In contrast, anger shows the strongest content-dependence: nearly perfect on paintings (93%) but degraded on landscapes (69%) and architecture (73%). In-door scenes span 74% –97% across emotions; objects excel for positive emotions but resist fear (67%, everyday items resist becoming menacing); action scenes reach 100% for both fear and sadness . This within-category variance is the signature of the preference-affinity landscape: relative ad-vantage is a joint function of content and emotion, not an intrinsic image property. The systematic non-uniformity of cell values across the 

9 × 8 landscape supports H4 : EmoScope’s relative pref-erence advantage is a joint function of image content and target emotion rather than a uniform constant.

4.8. Interactive Editing Study 

Beyond automated-pipeline quality, emotional editing is also personal expression : a real user iterates on intent. We conduct a small-scale usability pilot with 10 non-expert participants and 96 plan-review-iterate sessions across the eight Mikels emotions. Participants review the affordance-level plan, optionally provide natural-language feedback, and then rate the accepted result. The sample precludes population-level claims; we report this as preliminary de-ployment evidence. 

Behavioural results. Every session concluded with an accepted result ( 100% ); the workflow converges quickly (1.72 plan and 1.67 edit iterations on average, median 161 send-to-end); 39% of sessions exercised the explicit plan-feedback channel rather than accepting the first plan; mean personal satisfaction reached 3.92 /5 with 72% of sessions rated ≥ 4.Post-session ratings. A post-session 8-item Likert ques-tionnaire from all 10 participants surfaces two findings. First, the dialog-based plan-feedback feature was endorsed by every participant ( 100% : 6/10 “very necessary,” the rest “somewhat necessary”), confirming that affordance-as-interactive-surface is functionally required, not optional polish. Second, planner-side ratings (initial-plan inspiring-ness 3.90 /5, follow-up understanding 3.80 /5) are uniformly higher than the executed image-quality rating ( 3.50 /5)— a pattern that converges with the Editor-only ablation (Sec. 4.6) and with participants’ open-ended “plan is good but image quality is the bottleneck.” This user-perception evidence independently corroborates that the planner does work the editor cannot. Together with the controlled pairwise study (Sec. 4.2), this pilot provides preliminary evidence for H6 (non-expert users can converge on personalised emotional edits within a small number of plan/edit iterations using EmoScope’s interactive workflow ); a larger confirmatory study is left to future work. 

## 5. Conclusion 

We argued that the solution space of emotional image edit-ing is far larger than existing methods assume, and Emo-Scope addresses this by shifting the paradigm from “how should I edit?” to “what can I edit?”—discovering image-specific strategies through emotion-conditioned reasoning rather than selecting from fixed categories. Three findings stand out: (i) humans significantly pre-fer context-driven edits over fixed-taxonomy ones; (ii) edit-strategy distribution shifts predictably with target emo-tion (narrative-heavy for amusement / disgust, atmosphere-heavy for awe / contentment), evidencing emotion-adaptive 

selection; (iii) classifier-based metrics (Emo-A, Emo-S) ex-hibit an emotion-conditional measurement blind spot to-ward non-stereotypical context-grounded edits, varying an order of magnitude across target emotions. We addition-ally present the first empirical analysis of a relative content– emotion preference-affinity landscape , with practical impli-cations for adaptive intensity selection and stratified bench-marking. 

Limitations. Our framework depends on the VLM’s rea-soning capabilities for editing potential discovery, and its quality will scale with future VLM improvements. The iter-ative dual-loop architecture, while effective, is slower than single-pass methods. Our user study covers all eight Mikels emotion categories with 4,693 valid responses across 1,824 pairwise questions from 252 unique participants; further scaling participants per emotion would tighten confidence intervals on per-cell measurements within the preference-affinity landscape, and an absolute Likert-scale editability survey would disentangle relative preference from intrinsic difficulty. 

Future work. Five directions: (1) formal measures of per-image emotion editability to guide intensity selection; (2) distilling editing-potential reasoning into an end-to-end model that retains strategy diversity without the iter-ative cost; (3) using the released Q4.1 bounding-box an-notations as narrative trigger regions to test stereotype-vs-semantic-understanding at the spatial level; (4) a fully editor-controlled head-to-head against agentic AIM sys-tems (EmoAgent, EmoFeedback 2, Emotion-Director) on a unified backbone; (5) per-image hierarchy decomposition (Anchors / Variables / Context) cross-referenced with the preference-affinity landscape.
