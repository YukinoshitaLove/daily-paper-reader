Density estimation with generative models is a common task in the physical sciences, for example in background estimation in particle physics. Existing machine learning techniques, however, lack methods to quantify uncertainties that can be propagated into a statistical test. That limits the validation of generative models to standard goodness-of-fit tests, in which the generative model is treated as exact (point-null hypothesis). But a model fitted to a finite training set is never exact, so if its uncertainties are neglected, any sufficiently powerful goodness-of-fit test will eventually reject it once the test set is large enough to resolve this estimation error. We propose SIGMA, an end-to-end framework combining density estimation with principled uncertainty quantification. We use Gaussian mixture models, for which uncertainties are tractable, and further construct a new goodness-of-fit test in which these uncertainties are profiled as nuisance parameters to account for the statistical error of the fit, promoting the conventional point-null test to a composite one. Arejection then signals genuine mismodeling rather than a test set large enough to resolve the error of the fit, provided the propagated uncertainties faithfully cover that error. We demonstrate the framework on a controlled two-dimensional toy model and on a collider physics benchmark modeling the QCD background process. With uncertainties included, the same model can be trusted on test sets up to an order of magnitude larger than the standard test allows. Our work thus provides a statistically grounded basis for a robust use of generative models in the physical sciences.

I. INTRODUCTION 

Many scientific analyses compare observed data with a reference model in search of significant deviations, which may signal new phenomena. The reliability of the ref-erence model is therefore critical: any mismodeling can be mistaken for a genuine signal and lead to a false dis-covery. Such reference models are often built from high-fidelity simulations that require substantial computing resources. In particle physics, for example, the back-ground from known processes is modeled by simulating the full chain of hard process, parton showering, and de-tector response. The demands on the reference model are particularly high in model-agnostic searches, for example anomaly detection [1], where any deviation from it across the full feature space may be reported as a signal. Generative models have emerged as a way to signifi-cantly reduce the cost of such expensive simulations, act-ing as fast surrogates (or even replacements) [2–7] or as learned likelihoods in simulation-based inference [8, 9]. Generated events are moreover not independent draws from the true density: they all come from a sin-gle model (or ensemble of models) fitted to a finite sam-ple, whose fluctuations are imprinted in the model. No amount of generated data can therefore add information beyond the training sample [10]. In practice, any trained model will eventually fail a statistical test if it is checked against enough data. This happens even when the model is, in principle, capable of describing the data perfectly (Sec. II). Whether this failure matters depends on the use case in the analysis workflow [11]. A few represen-tative examples are listed in Fig. 1. Errors can be tol-erable when the result does not impact the downstream task; this includes examples when the model’s output is further validated by exact downstream checks, or when only qualitative fidelity is required, as is the case in ex-ploratory studies or pretraining. But when the fitted density serves as the reference model of a statistical test, as a background estimate, a null hypothesis, or a cali-bration input, its error is indistinguishable from a signal. To be used reliably in this role, a generative model must therefore come with uncertainties, and these must enter the statistical test itself. For models with few parameters, estimating uncertain-ties follows standard procedures. However, for modern density estimators, particularly machine learning driven generative models, with high-dimensional inputs and large numbers of non-identifiable parameters, the prob-lem becomes technically challenging, and attributing ro-bust uncertainties to them remains an active area of re-search. To address this challenge, we propose SIGMA (Score-Induced Gaussian Mixture Approximation), a generative model whose uncertainties are tractable and can be prop-agated into a statistical test. To validate it, we further introduce an uncertainty-aware goodness-of-fit test that incorporates the derived uncertainties. SIGMA models the density with a Gaussian mixture model and describes its uncertainties in the tangent space spanned by its score functions around the best fit of the density. Unlike for neural networks, this simple Ansatz makes the asymptotic maximum-likelihood co-variance of the parameters tractable. The test then prop-agates these uncertainties by promoting the conventional point-null Neyman–Pearson likelihood-ratio test [12–14] (also referred to as simple hypothesis) to a composite one [15], in which the nuisance parameters encoding the uncertainties are profiled, as is common practice in LHC searches [16]. Unlike in searches, the nuisance parame-ters are not systematic uncertainties of the experiment, but the statistical uncertainties of the fitted generative model itself. Because they enter the test in the same stan-dard form, conventional systematic uncertainties can be profiled alongside them, so that a single composite test accounts for both the error of the fit and the systematics of the analysis. The test outcome is therefore determined by the compatibility of the data with the model within its uncertainties, rather than driven by the fit error. Calibrated with pseudo-experiments drawn from the composite-null, the test retains its nominal false-positive rate even when the number of test events exceeds the training sample size, provided that the uncertainties faithfully cover the error of the fit. The paper is organized as follows. In Sec. II, we mo-tivate the problem with a controlled one-dimensional ex-ample. Section III reviews related work. In Sec. IV, we describe the components of our algorithm, from the density model to the uncertainty quantification and the design of the goodness-of-fit test. Section V applies the method to a controlled two-dimensional toy study, where the true density is known, and to the QCD background in a learned embedding of particle jets, a setting relevant for anomaly detection searches. We conclude in Sec. VI and outline future research directions. In App. A we further discuss the intrinsic limits of 3generative models and standard goodness-of-fit tests. Appendix B explores a related approach to quantify-ing and propagating model uncertainties with ensem-bles of neural-network density estimators, and finds that the resulting uncertainties are insufficient for the composite-null goodness-of-fit test to close on large ob-served datasets. Appendix C contains technical details of the experiments and studies the effects of varying a hyperparameter, App. D derives the asymptotic distri-bution of the composite-null test statistic under the null hypothesis, and App. E studies the choice of σ, another hyperparameter in the goodness-of-fit test.  

> II. POWERFUL TESTS REJECT EVERY GENERATIVE MODEL

A generative model with fitted parameters ˆθ only ever provides an approximation pˆθ (x) of the true data distri-bution p0(x). Following Refs. [17, 18], the discrepancy has two classes of sources, both epistemic, i.e. uncertain-ties about the model rather than about the intrinsic ran-domness of the data: i) The model bias , a systematic uncertainty: the model may be unable to represent the true distri-bution exactly, however well it is trained. This bias depends on the choice of architecture and does not shrink with the size of the training sample. It is distinct from the systematic uncertainties usually considered in physics analyses, such as imperfect knowledge of the detector or of the theory, which concern how well the training data, typically a sim-ulation, describe reality. ii) The statistical uncertainty of the fit : the finite training sample limits how precisely the parameters can be determined (data variability), and training may converge to different solutions depending on its initialization (optimization variability). Both vanish in the limit of infinite data and ideal opti-mization. Our work addresses the statistical uncertainty ii). Even in the best-case scenario in which the model family con-tains the ground truth, the finite size of the training dataset places a fundamental limit on the accuracy of the fitted density. This limit has a direct and often under-appreciated consequence for the validation of generative models via goodness-of-fit tests. To understand the effect, we consider the following one-dimensional experiment: samples are drawn from a standard normal distribution, p0 = N (0 , 1), and the generative model is chosen to be perfectly well specified, 

pθ = N (μ, σ ) with trainable parameters θ = ( μ, σ ). The parameters are estimated by maximum likelihood optimization on Ntrain training points, and the fitted model is then compared with independent test samples of increasing size Ntest by means of a goodness-of-fit test. The fitted model, indistinguishable from the truth at small test sample sizes, is eventually rejected with high significance once Ntest grows past Ntrain . This degra-dation is not specific to the test choice: any consistent goodness-of-fit test eventually detects the finite-statistics error between pˆθ (x) and the true density p0(x), so the p-values decrease with Ntest regardless of the test used. The gray markers in Figure 2 show this trend for the Gaus-sian example and the point-null likelihood-ratio test that will be defined in Sec. IV C (see Eq. (17)) calibrated with pseudo-experiments drawn from the fitted model. This behavior is not a pathology of the example but a generic and quantifiable consequence of information lim-its [10] (see App. A for a derivation). How fast the p-value curve degrades depends both on the sensitivity of the goodness-of-fit test employed and on the quality of the model design, i.e. how well the model design matches the properties of the true distribution. Crucially, how-ever, the degradation itself cannot be avoided: no amount of architectural or training improvement makes a density estimated from Ntrain samples exact .How, then, can generative models be used reliably for science? It is worth being precise about what this “in-trinsic” failure means. Standard goodness-of-fit methods test a point-null hypothesis : the fitted model is treated as an exact description of the data. Since the fitted model differs from the truth with probability one, rejection at large Ntest is guaranteed by construction. However, this is not the question of scientific interest. The relevant question is whether the data are compatible with the generative model within its statistical uncertainty , the spread of fits that would result from different training samples and different runs of the optimization. This is a composite-null hypothesis . A test that ignores this uncertainty conflates genuine mismodeling with the un-avoidable statistical fluctuations of the model fit, and its outcome is driven by the relation between Ntest and 

Ntrain rather than by the quality of the model. On the other hand, the bias introduced by an incorrect model is one that uncertainties cannot solve, and needs to be addressed at the level of model design, e.g. by replacing parametric background functions that fail as the dataset grows with more flexible Gaussian processes [19]. The statistical uncertainty of the fit, however, can be prop-agated into the goodness-of-fit test itself, promoting the point-null to a composite one. With the uncertainties in-cluded, the median p-value remains close to its nominal value of 0.5 over the full range of test sizes we probe (blue markers in Fig. 2).

[24] provides one such parametrization, describing the uncertainty through the weights of a fixed ensemble of trained models. En-sembling can be performed for any architecture, but the resultant uncertainties span only the coefficients of the trained models. As a result, the uncertainties cover the true density only under the strong assumption that it lies within the span of the trained ensemble members. En-sembling is also used in experimental searches, where the spread of independently trained models provides the un-certainty of a generative background model in the final fit [25]. Our SIGMA approach follows a similar logic but spans the full parameter space of the generative model, weakening the well-specification assumption, at the price of requiring a tractable model likelihood. 

b. Data amplification and the statistical value of gen-erated samples. A closely related line of work asks whether samples drawn from a generative model carry statistical precision beyond that of the training sam-ple, quantified through an amplification factor [26–29]. Refs. [28, 29] quantify the uncertainty of the learned densities, but do not propagate any uncertainties to the point-null hypothesis. Ref. [10] establishes that the gen-erated data cannot contain more information than the data on which the model was trained, and Ref. [30] de-rives an information-theoretic bound on the achievable gain, posing a theoretical limit to amplification. We address a prerequisite for trustworthy amplifica-tion: whether the fitted density, together with its esti-mated uncertainty, is compatible with independent data from the true distribution. 

c. Validating generative models. Previous work typ-ically validates the learned density through a low-dimensional summary test statistic: quantile occu-pancies [26], projected Jensen–Shannon divergences of high-level observables [27], the per-bin coverage of Bayesian uncertainties [28], the Kolmogorov asymptotics of a one-dimensional statistic [29], or classifier-based scores [31, 32], including the full distribution of classifier weights [33]. Since all of these tests treat the fitted den-sity as exact, they eventually reject even a model that is as good as its training statistics allow, once Ntest is large enough to resolve the error of the fit (Sec. II). Two-sample tests for validating generative models have been compared in Refs. [34, 35]. The latter finds the Neyman–Pearson test to be overall the most robust, outperforming marginal-based comparisons, at a higher computational cost. The Neyman–Pearson test with a learned alternative hypothesis [12–14, 36] compares the hypotheses in the full feature space, assumes little about the position or shape of a deviation, and returns a p-5value that is calibrated under the assumption that the reference model is exact. While Refs. [37, 38] applied the Neyman–Pearson test to the validation of genera-tive models in high-dimensional settings, these studies did not account for their uncertainties. Ref. [15] extends the Neyman–Pearson test to include nuisance parameters in the standard LHC form [16]. We adopt this structure, but in our case the nuisance parameters are not hand-chosen systematic uncertainties, but the estimation un-certainty of the generative model. 

d. Gaussian mixture models. Gaussian mix-ture models are a classical tool of statistics [39]. Their maximum-likelihood fitting via expectation– maximization (EM) [40, 41], the computation of the observed information matrix [42], and the asymptotic theory of mixture likelihoods [43, 44] are all well estab-lished. So are the ways mixtures depart from textbook regularity: components can be degenerate or weakly identified, and the model is identifiable only up to relabeling [45]. These issues motivate the regularization choices in Sec. IV. We claim no novelty on this machin-ery. Our contribution is to use the tractable score and Fisher information of a fitted mixture to extend the point-null goodness-of-fit test to a composite one, so that the uncertainty in the fit enters the test itself. 

e. Goodness-of-fit with estimated parameters. Stan-dard goodness-of-fit tests such as Kolmogorov–Smirnov [46] assume a fully specified null. Testing a null hypoth-esis that was itself fitted to data is a classical problem in statistics [47, 48], and the standard remedy is the para-metric bootstrap [49–51]. Particle physics knows the same process as calibration with pseudo-experiments, or toy Monte Carlo [52, 53]. Our bootstrap prescription of Sec. IV D mirrors this classical procedure, combined with a composite-null test in which the fit uncertainty enters as an explicit nuisance parametrization.  

> IV. METHOD

The method, summarized in Fig. 3, has three parts: a generative model with an unconstrained parametriza-tion (Sec. IV A), a standardized description of its estima-tion uncertainty (Sec. IV B), and a goodness-of-fit test in which the model may move within that uncertainty (Sec. IV C). The calibration of the goodness-of-fit test is discussed in Sec. IV D.

To strike a balance between these consid-erations, we take σ to be set by the characteristic scale of the data (alternatively, tests at several widths can be combined [60]); see App. C for details.

> VI. CONCLUSION

All generative models trained on finite samples are in-exact approximations of their training distribution, and a sufficiently powerful goodness-of-fit test will eventually detect deviations. This is a consequence of finite informa-tion, not a failure of design. A density estimated from 

Ntrain samples carries an inherent estimation error, so even a perfectly specified model is rejected by a power-ful goodness-of-fit test. Validating a generative model against a point-null therefore measures the size of the test sample rather than the quality of the model, and no architectural or training improvement can change this. An approximate model can nonetheless be useful, pro-vided its estimation error is known. In this work, we make this statement quantitative. By linearizing a Gaus-sian mixture model around its best fit, we obtain the covariance of its parameters from its analytic score func-tions and Fisher information, and the goodness-of-fit test becomes a convex problem. We find that promoting the null from a point hypothesis to a composite hypothesis, together with a parametric bootstrap calibration pro-cedure, is able to calibrate the goodness-of-fit test on datasets an order of magnitude larger than the point-null equivalent. The success of SIGMA is in contrast with neural gen-erative models trained through stochastic gradient de-scent, which typically do not converge to a well-defined optimum and which have non-invertible information ma-trices due to parameter degeneracies. We find in App. B that even a parametrized ensemble of normalizing flows is only able to capture part of the uncertainty, and cannot match the performance of SIGMA in our low-dimensional case studies. Uncertainty quantification for generative models bears directly on how generated samples can be used. A gener-ative model cannot create information that was not in its training data [10]. What it can do is produce arbitrarily many samples whose deviation from the truth is bounded by stated uncertainties. Generated samples can therefore be used beyond the training statistics only if these uncer-tainties are carried along into the downstream analysis, e.g. by using a composite-null hypothesis in the context of a goodness-of-fit or hypothesis test. For statistical in-ference, the true figure of merit of a generative surrogate is therefore the precision to which it can be trusted.       

> composite-null prescriptions, as the bootstrap-null would re-quire retraining the whole ensemble of flows in each pseudo-experiment, which would be prohibitively expensive.

Within this framework, SIGMA still has clear limita-tions. Because the number of parameters of a GMM grows quickly with dimension, the method is suited to low-dimensional spaces rather than directly to high-dimensional ones. This further motivates the compres-sion of high-dimensional data into a small number of sum-mary statistics, such as through learned embeddings [61– 66]. Furthermore, SIGMA accounts for the statistical vari-ability of the fit due to the finite training sample and to the stochasticity of optimization, but it is still sub-ject to model bias: a model family that cannot represent the data will still be rejected by a sufficiently powerful test. The need to reduce model bias makes the choice of K a trade-off. Increasing the number of components reduces mismodeling but it also adds parameters, leading to larger uncertainties and potentially undermining the calibration procedures. The Gaussian calibration mani-festly requires the asymptotic regime where Ntrain ≫ P ,and the bootstrap calibration relies on this indirectly through the assumption that the estimator spread is sim-ilar for fits to draws from p0 and from pˆθ .We currently choose K, as well as the truncation hy-perparameter Λ max , empirically. These model selection choices, as well as verifying that the model can be trusted beyond its training statistics, require closure tests on held-out samples at least as large as the desired inference dataset. This is costly in exactly the simulation-scarce settings where generative models are most useful, and the necessity of the bootstrap for the most trustworthy calibration adds the cost of refitting the model for every pseudo-experiment. Each of these limitations offers promising avenues for progress. Both the restriction to low-dimensionality and the model bias considerations motivate exploring distinct model classes. In particular, the test of Sec. IV C is ag-nostic to the model class: it requires only tractable score vectors, which automatic differentiation supplies for any neural density estimator, and a faithful estimate of the uncertainty of the fit. Our tangent-space construction is then the exact, low-dimensional counterpart of the lin-earized Laplace approximations developed for deep net-works [71, 72], and that literature’s low-rank and sub-space curvature estimates offer a natural route to extend-ing the composite-null test to larger models; the open problem is promoting such uncertainties from predictive heuristics to calibrated ingredients of a goodness-of-fit test. On the other hand, the need for empirical tuning of 

K and Λ max could be eliminated with a data-driven pro-cedure for hyperparameter selection. Such a procedure could involve the use of cross-validation to check closure on subsets of held-out simulation data. Moreover, it would be interesting to consider appli-cations with systematic uncertainties. Because the fit uncertainty enters as a standard constrained nuisance vector, the Gaussian calibration prescription composes naturally with the existing treatment of systematic un-15 certainties [15, 73]: additional nuisance directions are profiled jointly, and a single composite test accounts for all sources of uncertainty on the reference model. In contrast, composing our nominal bootstrap calibra-tion prescription (which does not yield straightforwardly parametrized uncertainties) with additional nuisance pa-rameters may be more challenging. The immediate application of our method is back-ground estimation for anomaly detection in low-dimensional learned embeddings. There, a validated model could enter the final test in closed form, together with its uncertainties, as the composite-null hypothe-sis. More broadly, as generative models increasingly substitute for simulation across various scientific fields, uncertainty-aware validation is necessary for these sur-rogates to serve as instruments of trustworthy statistical inference. > Appendix B: Normalizing flows and the limits of ensemble uncertainties

Normalizing flows [55] are among the most widely used and flexible neural density estimators in high-energy physics, both as surrogates for expensive simu-lation [2, 31] and as background models [4–7, 25], in-cluding density estimation in learned embedding spaces for anomaly detection searches [66]. Unlike the Gaussian mixtures of the main text, a flow has no tractable param-eter covariance, so the score-based, full-parameter-space construction of Sec. IV is unavailable. In this appendix, we consider the same two numeri-cal experiments from Sec. V, a two-dimensional toy with a bimodal Gaussian and a skew-normal feature and a four-dimensional latent space for jet representations, and we ask the following two questions: (1) does the epis-temic uncertainty of a normalizing flow ensemble admit a parametrization that a likelihood-ratio test can profile over? and (2) with this uncertainty propagated in the null, does a normalizing flow ensemble pass a composite goodness-of-fit test such as the one presented in the main text? Ref. [74] provides such a parametrization through the maximum-likelihood wifi ensembling of Ref. [24], in which independently trained flows are frozen and com-bined into a weighted mixture whose weights carry the uncertainty. This appendix extends that work in two di-rections: to more complex target distributions, and to a goodness-of-fit test built on the same construction as the main text. We describe how we train the normalizing flow mod-els in Sec. B 1, how we estimate uncertainties with the 

wifi ensembling in Sec. B 2, and how we incorporate the uncertainties in the goodness-of-fit test in Sec. B 3. The results of our numerical experiments, presented in Sec. B 4, show that a normalizing-flow ensemble admits such a weight parametrization, and that propagating it restores closure up to and beyond Ntest ≃ Ntrain for the largest ensemble ( M = 128) in the two-dimensional toy, but not in the four-dimensional embedding, where the propagated weight uncertainty is negligible compared to the flow’s misspecification: the composite test collapses onto the point-null and the model is rejected well before 

Ntest = Ntrain .

Because the tilt is regularized (ridge and box) and its kernel dictionary is strongly collinear, neither statistic follows the asymptotic Wilks χ2, whose number of degrees of freedom would equal the number of tilt coefficients; the effective number of degrees of free-dom is much smaller. We therefore do not rely on Wilks’ theorem, but calibrate the statistics empirically from the pseudo-experiments, against which all p-values in Sec. B 4 are evaluated.  

> 4. Results

We apply the construction of Secs. B 1–B 3 to the two-dimensional toy of Sec. V A ( Ntrain = 10 5), for ensembles of M flows up to M = 128. The tests use the kernel dictionary of the main text ( J = 500, σ = 0 .4), ridge 

λ = 1 and box A = 3. Each null is calibrated on B = 100 pseudo-experiments and the p-value is estimated from 100 draws of Ntest events from the truth. We then repeat the construction on the four-dimensional jet embedding of Sec. V B, with the same Ntrain , ensemble sizes and test settings, and the four-dimensional kernel width of the main text, σ = 0 .53. 

a. Density quality. Figure 10 shows the fitted two-dimensional marginals for M = 1 , 32 , 128. A single flow (M = 1), which carries no parametrized uncertainty, re-produces the bulk of the target but visibly mismodels the peaks and the valley of the bimodal feature 1. As 

M increases the ensemble median tightens onto the tar-get (most visibly the bimodal valley of feature 1) and a weight-uncertainty band develops, narrow in the bulk and widening in the low-statistics tails. In the four-dimensional embedding (Fig. 11) the picture is different: the fitted marginals show structured deviations from the held-out data, most visibly around the sharp peaks, while the weight-uncertainty band remains negligibly small at every M . The propagated uncertainty therefore does not cover the discrepancy between the ensemble and the data, which anticipates the goodness-of-fit results below. 

b. Goodness-of-fit. Fig. 12 shows the median test p-value versus Ntest for M = 32 , 128. As expected, the 19 test sensitivity increases with Ntest : the p-value stays near nominal while the test sample is too small to re-solve the finite-Ntrain error of the fit, and falls once it does. The single flow degrades earliest, by Ntest ∼ 10 4,as anticipated in Sec. II: a flow’s large parameter count and imperfect inductive bias push the rejection onset to 

Ntest ≪ Ntrain . A single flow is therefore not a valid ref-erence model at these test sizes. Ensembling and prop-agating the weight uncertainty push this onset outward, and the benefit grows with M . A moderate ensemble (M = 32) delays the degradation to Ntest ∼ 2–5 ×10 4 but still reaches the floor 1 /(B + 1) by Ntest = Ntrain = 10 5

with little difference between the composite and point-null (median p ≈ 0.09 vs 0 .07 at Ntest = 5 × 10 4). This happens although the M = 32 ensemble closely repro-duces the two-dimensional marginals (Fig. 10): the test probes the joint density, where the residual error of the fit is resolved. Only at M = 128 does propagating the weight uncertainty make a clear difference: the composite test stays near nominal, p ≈ 0.43, whereas the point-null falls to p ≈ 0.16 at Ntest = 5 × 10 4 and to the floor by Ntest = Ntrain . With M = 128 the composite test gives p ≈ 0.43 out to Ntest = 2 × 10 5, i.e. it does not re-ject the model even in the genuine oversampling regime 

Ntest > N train .In four dimensions the picture changes (Fig. 13). The single flow degrades by Ntest ∼ 2×10 3 and the ensembles by Ntest ∼ 10 4. There is no improvement from prop-agating the weight uncertainty: the composite p-value is inseparable from the point-null within the pseudo-experiment variations at every M and every Ntest , with no separation even at M = 128. All constructions reach the floor 1 /(B + 1) by Ntest ∼ 2 × 10 4, well below 

Ntrain = 10 5, and even M = 128 is fully rejected. 

c. Discussion. Since every null is calibrated with pseudo-experiments drawn from the composite-null itself, the loss of p-value at large Ntest reflects a failure of the ensemble uncertainty to cover the error of the fit, not an artifact of the statistic. Ensembling the flows and prop-agating the weight uncertainty push this rejection out-ward in the 2D toy study, and with M = 128 the model survives up to Ntest ≃ Ntrain and into the oversampling regime Ntest > N train probed here, up to Ntest = 2 Ntrain .In the more complex 4D problem, the construction fails to close: ensembling still improves, but the weight co-variance Σ u spans only re-weightings P 

> i

δw ifi(x) of the frozen members, and in the embedding the resulting un-certainty is negligible compared to the discrepancy be-tween the ensemble and the data. With an uncertainty this small, the profiled weights can barely move, the com-posite null effectively reduces to the point-null, and the two statistics coincide: even an ensemble of M = 128 flows is rejected well before Ntest = Ntrain . This is the limitation anticipated in Sec. B 2: re-weighting frozen flows can make up for the error of a simple target, but not for that of a more complex one.

In other words, the test finds more discrepancy, on average, with the best-fit trained model in pseudo-experiments (where that model is guar-anteed to be well-specified) than in the observed data (where it is not), so this deviation from calibration is clearly spurious. We ascribe this to an interplay between the structure of the SIGMA model, which is a GMM, and the test. When trained on a finite training set without strong regulariza-tion, GMMs generally overfit by placing narrow compo-nents in regions of feature space with disproportionately many events. This overfitting is not pathological in our context, as it is part of the variance we aim to capture with uncertainties (as opposed to model bias, which we cannot capture; strong regularization would induce such bias). The fine-grained structure induced by overfitting is then generically present in our trained models but not in the data-generating process, so an alternative hypothesis comprised of small-length-scale Gaussian functions will systematically fit a dataset drawn from a trained SIGMA model better than it would observed data. As such, we do not recommend using SIGMA models in conjunction with a goodness-of-fit test sensitive to length scales sub-stantially smaller than the natural length scales of the data, hence our choice of σ in the main body to be com-parable to the data standard deviation. 25 1k 10k 100k
