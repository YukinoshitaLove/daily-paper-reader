# Abstract 

Autoregressive (AR) image generators are powerful in principle but inflexible in practice: training with a fixed raster-scan order hardcodes a single factorization of the image distribution into the model, leaving it unable to inpaint, outpaint, or edit at inference time. Existing solutions, including the modern discrete-diffusion family, train from scratch on a mixture of token orderings, paying roughly 3× the compute of standard raster-scan training; discrete diffusion further sacrifices com-patibility with the AR inference ecosystem (KV caching, vLLM) by construction. We argue that this cost is unnecessary. A pretrained raster-scan AR model has already learned the joint distribution over image tokens; what it lacks is not knowl-edge but the ability to express that knowledge through non-sequential conditional pathways. We propose a recipe for adapting pretrained raster-scan models into any-order generators using two components: future-aware positional embeddings 

that resolve a target-position identifiability problem at negligible overhead, and an ordering curriculum that traces a stable optimization path from the pretrained solution to a good any-order basin. Applied to four base models across two archi-tectures and two scales (LlamaGen-L/XL, RAR-L/XL), the recipe substantially outperforms MaskGIT on ImageNet inpainting (FID 6.21 vs. 10 .44 ) and outpaint-ing, is competitive with MAR-L, trades a 1.2–1.9 increase in class-conditional FID for this flexibility, and costs 13 –33% additional compute beyond pretraining, while leaving the standard AR inference stack untouched. Inpainting, outpaint-ing, and editing, our results suggest, need not be added to autoregressive image models. They can be unlocked from the ones that exist. Code is available at 

https://github.com/Ordered-AR/unlockedadaptation .

# 1 Introduction 

Autoregressive (AR) modeling has become a common paradigm for image generation [3, 1, 4, 5], inheriting the scalability and training stability [6, 7, 8, 9] that made it standard in language [10, 11, 12, 13]. By tokenizing images into discrete codes and predicting them sequentially [14, 1, 15, 16], AR models reach generation quality comparable to that of leading diffusion and bi-directional models, while sharing the architecture of their language counterparts. Standard AR image models, however, impose a fixed generation order, typically raster scan, proceeding left to right and top to bottom. This ordering is convenient, but it hardcodes a single factorization of the image distribution into the model, leaving it unable to condition on non-contiguous context at inference time. 

Any-order generation removes this restriction. Rather than committing to a single token ordering, an any-order AR model predicts any subset of image tokens conditioned on any other subset. This unlocks a range of capabilities from a single model: by treating observed tokens as context and sampling the remainder, the same weights perform inpainting, outpainting, and region-conditioned

Diffusion models support analogous tasks, but only through specialized samplers [19, 20] or auxiliary conditioning networks [21, 22] layered onto a fixed generation process. Any-order AR offers them as zero-shot capabilities of the native generation procedure: one model, one set of weights, one decoding loop. The obstacle is cost of training. Existing approaches to any-order image generation train from scratch on a mixture of token orderings, a joint optimization that takes roughly 3× the compute of raster-scan training (e.g., RandAR [18] requires 300 epochs vs. 100 for the corresponding raster-scan LlamaGen-XL [1]). The most common modern approach is discrete diffusion [2, 23, 24], which re-frames any-order generation as iterative mask-token denoising under a non-causal transformer. Discrete diffusion reaches competitive quality, but its bidirectional attention and iterative-refinement decoding place it outside the standard AR inference ecosystem: KV caching, speculative decoding, and AR-oriented serving frameworks such as vLLM [25] do not accelerate it. The result is not just a training cost gap but a recurring inference gap: practitioners with existing AR pretraining pipelines and AR serving infrastructure cannot adopt these methods without parallel investment in a separate stack. We argue that this cost reflects a conceptual conflation rather than a fundamental requirement. Any-order AR models need not be trained from scratch; they can be adapted from existing pretrained raster-scan models. A raster-scan AR model has already done the expensive work: it has learned an approximation of the joint distribution over image tokens, encoding texture statistics, spatial coherence, and semantic structure into its representations. The chain rule of probability guarantees that the true joint p(x1, . . . , x n) factorizes equivalently under any token ordering, so a model that is a good fit under one factorization is a candidate fit under all of them. We hypothesize that the representations learned under raster-scan training are sufficiently order-agnostic to support any-order generation with light adaptation, because they encode properties of images rather than properties of scan lines. What the model lacks is not distributional knowledge but the ability to express that knowledge through non-sequential conditional pathways, and acquiring that ability, starting from a good initialization, need not be expensive. To test this hypothesis, we propose a recipe for adapting a pretrained raster-scan AR model into an any-order generator. Two components are jointly necessary. First, we introduce future-aware positional embeddings that inject the identity of the next target position into each step of the generation sequence. This addresses an identifiability problem inherent to causal any-order generation: without target-position information, identical context must produce different outputs for different targets, generating conflicting gradient signals that prevent convergence. Our embeddings address this at negligible overhead, preserving the standard single-stream transformer architecture and the inference-time properties it confers. Second, we employ an ordering curriculum that gradually interpolates from raster-scan to fully random permutations over the course of adaptation. Without it, sudden exposure to random orderings produces large, high-variance gradients that move the model out of the pretrained basin before order-flexible routing has developed, eroding the representations we 2aim to reuse. The curriculum traces a stable optimization path from the pretrained solution to a good any-order basin. Both components are necessary: ablating either causes training to diverge or generation quality to drop noticeably (Section 4). The resulting model reaches any-order generation at roughly 2.4× lower end-to-end cost than training from scratch, requiring only 25% additional computation beyond the original raster-scan pretraining budget on LlamaGen-XL ( 13 –33% across base models); for practitioners who already have a pretrained AR model, the marginal adaptation cost is over an order of magnitude lower (12 ×) than training any-order from scratch. On LlamaGen-XL, FID moves from 2.63 to 3.91 while Inception Score, Precision, and Recall remain close to the pretrained values ( 252 .94 , 0.81 , 0.53 ), a measured quality-flexibility trade-off rather than a lossless conversion. We observe a similar pattern across LlamaGen-L and RAR-L/XL (Section 4). The adapted model also gains the any-order capabilities promised by the construction (inpainting, outpainting, and region-conditioned editing), where it substantially outperforms MaskGIT [2] on ImageNet inpainting (FID 6.21 vs. 10 .44 ) and outpainting (FID 5.84 vs. 12 .17 ) and is competitive with MAR [3], despite never having been trained on either task (Section 4). Because the recipe leaves the causal transformer architecture untouched, the adapted model retains KV caching and parallel decoding and can be served with vLLM [25] through a custom model registration, whereas discrete-diffusion any-order approaches cannot reuse the KV cache, by construction. Flexible image generation (inpainting, outpainting, editing) need not be added to autoregressive models. It is, in our results, already accessible from the ones that exist. 

# 2 Related Work 

Autoregressive image generation. Autoregressive image models, long standard in language mod-eling, have recently reached generation quality on par with leading diffusion methods on standard benchmarks [3, 1, 4, 26]. They tokenize images via a quantized autoencoder [14, 1, 4, 5] and predict discrete tokens with a transformer, inheriting the inference ecosystem developed for LLMs (KV caching, paged attention, tensor/pipeline parallelism) [25] and integrating naturally into unified multimodal stacks [16, 27]. Standard AR image models commit to a fixed raster-scan order; this work relaxes that constraint while preserving the inference-time properties that make AR attractive. 

Any-order generation. Permutation language modeling [28] showed that a single causal model can generate under arbitrary orderings if a target-position identifiability problem is resolved, originally via two-stream attention. Image-domain follow-ups [18, 29, 30] apply the same recipe to discrete image tokens but pay similar costs: any-order capability obtained from scratch, with architectural overhead or training budgets several times that of standard raster-scan AR. MAR [3] instead predicts continuous tokens with a diffusion head, also from scratch. The current dominant alternative is 

discrete diffusion [2, 23, 31], which casts any-order generation as iterative mask-token denoising under bidirectional attention but, by construction, sits outside the AR inference ecosystem. We instead retain causal AR, address identifiability with a lightweight additive embedding, and obtain any-order capability through adaptation rather than from-scratch training. 

Adaptation of pretrained models. Adapting pretrained models to new objectives is standard practice in language and vision [32, 33, 34, 35]. Most prior work targets new tasks or domains ;we instead target a new factorization of the same data distribution. Task-specific methods such as ControlNet [36] are complementary: they add a branch for one fixed control signal, whereas we support arbitrary observed-token subsets. Whether representations learned under one factorization transfer to others is an empirical question; our results suggest that they do, with light adaptation.

The question we address is whether a model pretrained under raster scan can be adapted, by continued training under Equation 1, into one that supports the full family of factorizations. We argue that this is possible, but only if two distinct issues are addressed. 

The identifiability problem. Under raster-scan, the prediction target at step t is implicitly σ(t +1) = t + 1 and the model never needs to be told which position to predict. Under varying σ, that contract breaks: the same observed context xσ(<t ) corresponds to different prediction targets across permutations. Concretely, predicting a sky token given the rest of an image is a different problem from predicting a face token given the same context: the visible information is identical, but the conditional distributions are not. A model that attempts to fit p(xj | xS ) and p(xk | xS ) for j̸ = k from the same input representation therefore receives conflicting gradients, and the objective in Equation 1 becomes ill-posed without target-position information. XLNet [28] resolved this in language with a two-stream attention mechanism that maintains a separate query stream for target-position information; this restores identifiability but doubles per-layer attention compute and breaks compatibility with KV caching. We propose an alternative based on input-level conditioning (Section 3.1). 

The optimization problem from a pretrained initialization. Even with identifiability resolved, naive any-order training from the pretrained parameters θ∗ fails. At θ∗, the per-permutation loss Lσ

is small for σ ≈ σraster but large for random σ, and an expectation over Uniform( Sn) is therefore dominated by high-loss random permutations. The resulting gradients are large in magnitude and inconsistent in direction across permutations; applied at θ∗, they push the model out of the pretrained basin before order-flexible routing has developed, a form of catastrophic forgetting that erodes the very representations we aim to reuse. We address this with an ordering curriculum (Section 3.2) that interpolates from raster-scan to uniform random over the course of adaptation, tracing a stable optimization path from θ∗ to a good any-order basin. Note that this is distinct from simply lowering the learning rate: a smaller step size leaves the gradient direction unchanged and continues to point away from the pretrained basin. The curriculum instead modifies the composition of the loss, weighting raster-like permutations heavily early on so that the gradient itself is dominated by directions consistent with the pretrained solution. Appendix A gives the full gradient-magnitude and gradient-variance argument and frames it as a continuation method. The two mechanisms address orthogonal aspects of the problem and are jointly necessary: identifiability makes the loss well-posed; the curriculum makes the well-posed loss reachable from the pretrained initialization. Section 4.4 confirms both halves empirically. 

3.1 Future-Aware Positional Embeddings 

We introduce a learned embedding table E ∈ Rn×d, where d is the model’s hidden dimension and row Ej encodes the identity of position j as a target. Under permutation σ, the input representation at sequence position t is augmented with the embedding of the next target position: 

˜ht = ht + Eσ(t+1) , (2) where ht = e(xσ(t)) is the standard input embedding of the token at sequence position t (for RAR, including its absolute position embedding at σ(t)). The final token has no successor; we use a learned null embedding E∅. The embedding E is shared across sequence positions and permutations: the same row Ej is added at any sequence position whose successor under σ is the spatial position j.The addition is at the input layer only; we considered AdaLN-style injection at every layer [37] but found a single input-level addition sufficient, consistent with the view that the embedding’s role is to disambiguate the prediction target rather than to participate in feature computation throughout the network. 

Position handling under permutation. The two base-model families we adapt encode position differently (LlamaGen via rotary position embeddings (RoPE) [38] and RAR via additive learned absolute position tables at the input layer), but the principle for any-order generation is the same in both: at sequence step t, the position used for rotation (LlamaGen) or table look-up (RAR) is the spatial index σ(t), not the sequence step. With this choice, attention or input addition between tokens at sequence steps t and t′ depends only on their spatial positions σ(t) and σ(t′), identical to what the 4model would have computed under raster scan with those tokens at the same spatial locations. The model thus inherits the spatial-relational priors learned during pretraining unchanged; only the order in which sequence steps consume those positions varies. Compared to two-stream attention [28], the additive embedding adds only (n + 1) · d parameters and an elementwise sum, leaves the standard single-stream transformer architecture untouched, and preserves KV caching and parallel decoding.

3.4 Inference 

At inference, the user (or upstream task) specifies a generation order σ: raster scan recovers the pre-trained model’s behavior; uniform random shuffles the order; task-induced placements put observed tokens (an inpainting mask, an outpainting boundary, a partial edit) first in σ and sample the rest in any chosen order. Generation proceeds sequentially under σ with the future-aware embedding 

Eσ(t+1) added to each input. Because the architecture is a standard single-stream transformer, KV caching applies unchanged, supporting inpainting, outpainting, and region-conditioned editing as direct instances of conditional generation without task-specific training, sampler modification, or auxiliary networks (Section 4). 

# 4 Results 

We evaluate our adaptation recipe along four axes: (i) generation quality relative to standard gen-erative baselines and the pretrained raster-scan models we adapt (Section 4.2); (ii) the any-order capabilities the unlocked model acquires, demonstrated on inpainting, outpainting, and region com-pletion (Section 4.3); (iii) the necessity of each recipe component, validating the diagnoses in Section 3 (Section 4.4); and (iv) training and inference cost relative to existing any-order approaches (Section 4.5). 

4.1 Experimental Setup Datasets. All experiments use ImageNet-1k (ILSVRC-2012) [39], on which all base models and baselines were pretrained. Adaptation uses the 1.28 M-image training split, pre-tokenized with each model’s VQ tokenizer. Class-conditional metrics are computed against the standard ImageNet 

256 × 256 reference statistics [40], inpainting and outpainting are evaluated on the 50 ,000 -image 5validation split, and region completion on 5,000 held-out images, with masks shared across methods. We use no other datasets. 

Base models. We apply our recipe to two families of class-conditional autoregressive image generators trained on ImageNet: LlamaGen [1] (sizes L and XL) and RAR [5] (sizes L and XL). For each base model we start from the publicly released pretrained checkpoint and apply the recipe described in Section 3. Image tokens come from the corresponding pretrained VQ tokenizer; we do not modify the tokenizer. LlamaGen-XL generates at 384 × 384 following its released configuration and is resized to 256 × 256 for evaluation. 

Adaptation. We adapt each pretrained checkpoint for Tft epochs (set per base model; see Ap-pendix B) with the curriculum coefficient α scheduled linearly from 0 to 1 over the first Sramp epochs and held at 1 for the remainder. Future-aware embeddings are zero-initialized (loaded from the checkpoint for RAR) and trained at 10 × the base learning rate; we ablate this choice in Section 4.4. All other hyperparameters match the corresponding pretraining configuration. 

Evaluation. We report Fréchet Inception Distance (FID) [41], Inception Score (IS) [42], and Precision/Recall on 50 ,000 generated samples following the standard ImageNet protocol. All samples use the same number of decoding steps as the corresponding pretrained model and the published sampling configuration unless otherwise specified.

4.4 Ablations 

The motivation in Section 3 makes two falsifiable predictions: future-aware positional embeddings are necessary to make the any-order objective well-posed, and the ordering curriculum is necessary to make it reachable from a pretrained initialization. We test each prediction directly. 

Future-aware embeddings are necessary. Removing future-aware embeddings (Figure 4) prevents the loss from descending below a substantial floor: as the curriculum ramps in random orderings, the same observed context must explain progressively more diverse target distributions, and the loss increases rather than decreases. This is the gradient-conflict failure mode predicted by the identifiability argument in Section 3, observed empirically. The corresponding generation quality is poor across all evaluation orders. 

The curriculum is necessary. With α = 1 from step zero (i.e., applying the well-posed any-order objective to the pretrained checkpoint without any schedule), at convergence the no-curriculum model trails our full recipe across all fidelity metrics on LlamaGen-L (Table 5), while Recall is essentially unchanged, a fidelity collapse rather than a coverage collapse. The pretrained representations were eroded before order-flexible routing could form. 

Embedding learning rate. The future-aware embedding table E is zero-initialized, which means it must develop useful values from scratch even as the curriculum increases the model’s reliance on it. We find empirically that scaling the learning rate of E by 10 × relative to the rest of the network is necessary for stable convergence: at the base learning rate, the embedding develops too slowly to keep pace with the curriculum, and final any-order quality suffers. We use the 10 × multiplier in all main results. 

Alternative mitigation: teacher regularization. Adding a KL term ( λ = 1 .0) toward the frozen raster-scan teacher on raster-ordered examples raises LlamaGen-L FID from 4.24 to 9.62 , suggesting that matching raster-specific predictions conflicts with learning arbitrary-order conditionals. 8Table 4: Reference-based completion metrics. # 5 Conclusion 

We presented a recipe for converting a pretrained raster-scan autoregressive image generator into an any-order generator through targeted adaptation. The recipe combines a future-aware positional embedding that resolves the identifiability problem inherent to causal any-order generation, and an ordering curriculum that traces a stable optimization path from the pretrained solution to a good any-order basin. Across four base models spanning two architectures and two scales (LlamaGen-L/XL, RAR-L/XL), the adapted models trade a 1.17 –1.93 increase in class-conditional FID relative to the pretrained checkpoints for any-order capability, outperform MaskGIT on inpainting (FID 6.21 vs. 

10 .44 ) and outpainting (FID 5.84 vs. 12 .17 ) and are competitive with MAR-L despite never being trained on either task, and remain compatible with the standard AR inference ecosystem (KV caching, parallel decoding, vLLM via a custom model). For LlamaGen-XL, the marginal cost of adaptation is approximately 25% of the original pretraining budget, against the ≈ 3× required by from-scratch any-order training. The chain rule of probability guarantees that any model that fits the joint distribution under raster-scan order is, in principle, a fit under every other ordering as well. Our results suggest that this principle is also empirically actionable: the representations a pretrained raster-scan model has already learned are not specific to scan order, and unlocking access to non-sequential conditional pathways is a much smaller intervention than learning the joint from scratch. We do not claim that causal AR should replace diffusion or flow matching; our contribution is reusing existing causal checkpoints. 

Limitations. Our experiments are restricted to class-conditional image generation on ImageNet at 

256 × 256 ; whether the recipe extends to text-to-image AR models or higher resolutions remains to be tested. We adapt token-based AR models specifically, and our diagnosis of the identifiability problem applies to any causal any-order generator; non-token-based variants such as MAR (which predicts continuous tokens via a diffusion head) lie outside the scope of models we adapt. Our editing evaluation covers region completion only. Finally, our random-initialization control (Section 4.2) matches compute but not schedule.

Gradient magnitude. The gradient norms ∥∇L σ (θ∗)∥ inherit this imbalance: small for raster-like permutations, large for random ones. The expected gradient under Uniform( Sn) is therefore dominated by high-loss permutations. Even though most σ̸ = σraster are unfamiliar to the model, they collectively contribute the bulk of the gradient signal at θ∗. Early updates therefore optimize aggressively for random-permutation loss, and in doing so they rapidly modify the parameters that encode the raster-scan distribution, the very representations we wish to preserve. 13 Gradient variance. Different random permutations σ1, σ 2 induce conditionals with different dependency structures and therefore prescribe different parameter updates. The directions ∇L σ1 (θ∗)

and ∇L σ2 (θ∗) can disagree substantially. Stochastic gradients computed over batches of random permutations therefore have high variance, causing optimization to oscillate rather than converge and, combined with the magnitude problem, to oscillate in directions that destabilize the pretrained basin. The combined effect is catastrophic forgetting: the network’s order-flexible routing has not yet developed when its raster-scan competence is being eroded, leaving the model worse on raster scan and not yet good on anything else. 

Curriculum as a continuation method. The curriculum addresses both failure modes. At small α

in Equation 3, the gradient is dominated by raster-scan terms whose magnitude is small at θ∗, and the model stays close to the pretrained basin. The small contribution from random permutations is enough to begin shaping order-flexible routing without destabilizing the existing representations. As 

α increases, the model has already partially adapted, so the random-permutation loss at the current parameters is no longer catastrophically large; magnitude and variance across random permutations both decrease. This is a continuation method : rather than leaping from θ∗ to a good any-order solution in a single optimization, we trace a path through parameter space along which each successive objective is a small perturbation of the last. At every intermediate α, the previous solution is a good initialization for the current objective, and the model’s competence on prior orderings is preserved as a foundation for new ones. 

Design-choice asides. We considered injecting the future-aware embedding Eσ(t+1) at every transformer layer (analogous to AdaLN-style conditioning [37]), but found a single input-layer addition sufficient, consistent with the view that the embedding’s role is to disambiguate the prediction target rather than to participate in feature computation throughout the network.

# E Broader Impacts 

Our recipe extends the capabilities of existing pretrained class-conditional image generators rather than producing a new class of generative model. It inherits both their positive applications, such as image editing and content completion, and their misuse risks, such as misleading content generation. Because the base models are already publicly released and restricted to ImageNet classes, the recipe does not introduce new categories of risk beyond what those models already enable.
