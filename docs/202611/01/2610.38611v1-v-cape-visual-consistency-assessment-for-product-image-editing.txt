# V-CAPE: Visual Consistency Assessment for Product Image Editing

Abstract 

Recent advances in generative image editing enable complex transformations such as background replacement, object manipulation, and pose adjustment from natural language instructions. However, verifying that these transformations preserve vi-sual consistency , defined as the preservation of an object’s intrinsic visual attributes, including geometry, texture, color, and fine-grained details, remains a largely un-solved problem. This challenge is particularly pronounced at scale, where manual inspection is infeasible and automated quality assessment (AutoQA) could become essential. In this work, we introduce Visual Consistency Assessment for Product Image Editing (V-CAPE), a large-scale dataset for evaluating visual consistency in generative image editing. The dataset consists of 40 K unique source–target product image pairs, each annotated with binary accept/reject labels and structured failure modes capturing fine-grained inconsistencies such as geometric distortions, pose errors, and identity mismatches. V-CAPE combines real-world edited images with a synthetic subset constructed from 3D assets, enabling both realistic evaluation and controlled analysis. Vision–language models (VLMs) across all evaluated size categories on AutoQA achieve accuracy below 65%, indicating that the dataset poses a substantial challenge regardless of model scale. Our results highlight fundamental limitations of current models in verifying visual consistency under transformation, positioning V-CAPE as a benchmark for advancing fine-grained image editing evaluation. 

1 Introduction 

Modern generative image editing models can perform complex transformations such as background replacement, object removal, relighting, virtual try-on, and pose manipulation from natural language instructions [1–8]. Despite this progress, ensuring that such transformations preserve the visual consistency , i.e., the original object’s geometry, structure, and fine-grained visual attributes, remains a significant challenge. Even subtle inconsistencies, such as slight geometric distortions, incorrect pose, or missing details, can degrade the semantic fidelity of the edited image. This issue could become particularly acute at scale, where large volumes of generated content could require systematic verification. We formalize this setting as AutoQA: given a source image and its edited counterpart, determine whether the transformation preserves visual consistency with respect to the original object. Manual annotation is impractical at scale, motivating the need for reliable automated methods. VLMs provide a natural framework for addressing this problem. Existing datasets and benchmarks have important limitations for evaluating this task. A large body of prior work focuses on the generation process itself, providing supervision for producing edits (e.g., InstructPix2Pix [1], Emu Edit [2], HQ-Edit [3], and DiTailed [16]) but not for assessing their cor-rectness. In parallel, recent benchmarks targeting fine-grained perception and multi-image reasoning (e.g., SEED-Bench [10, 17, 18], MMBench [11], POPE [12]) expose important weaknesses of current models, yet do not capture the specific challenge of evaluating consistency under transformation. In particular, these benchmarks lack (i) explicit source–output relationships induced by editing, (ii) annotations grounded in human judgments of visual fidelity, and (iii) structured descriptions of failure modes. We address these gaps by introducing V-CAPE (cf. Fig. 1), a large-scale dataset for evaluating visual consistency in generative image editing. The dataset is constructed to directly support the AutoQA setting by providing source images, edited outputs, and human annotations indicating whether the transformation preserves object fidelity. A key characteristic of V-CAPE is its emphasis on fine-grained consistency: beyond binary labels, each example includes structured annotations describing specific failure modes, such as incorrect pose, geometric distortion, or identity mismatch. This enables detailed analysis of model behavior and supports evaluation beyond coarse classification. V-CAPE comprises two complementary subsets. The V-CAPE-R subset captures real-world edited images, reflecting the types of artifacts encountered in practice. The V-CAPE-S subset provides a controlled setting in which target images are generated from 3D assets, allowing precise manipulation of pose, identity, and appearance. This combination balances realism with controllability and enables systematic analysis under both natural and synthetic conditions. In total, the dataset contains 40 Ksource–target pairs, each human–annotated by multiple trained annotators to ensure label reliability. Automatic quality assesment on V-CAPE is challenging. Our evaluation of state-of-the-art VLMs, both open and proprietary models, indicates that even the strongest models achieve only around 

60% on this task, highlighting a substantial gap between current capabilities and the requirements of reliable AutoQA. These failures are concentrated in fine-grained visual reasoning: models struggle to verify object identity under pose variation and detect subtle geometric inconsistencies, capabilities that could be essential for reliable automated quality assurance. 

2 Related Work 

Image editing datasets. Prior work on text-guided image editing primarily focuses on generating 

edits rather than verifying fidelity of the outputs. Early benchmarks such as EditBench [19] and MagicBrush [20] introduce instruction-based editing with paired source images, prompts, and targets. Subsequent datasets [1, 3, 21–25] expand scale, diversity, and annotation quality. However, they emphasize edit operations (e.g., addition, removal, replacement, style transfer) and do not explicitly address whether edited images preserve fine-grained object identity, geometry, pose, and product-2specific attributes. In contrast, we formulate visual consistency as an AutoQA problem, providing accept/reject labels with structured failure modes, and targeting subtle fidelity violations that are challenging for current models. 

Benchmarks for fine-grained visual perception. Recent benchmarks highlight limitations of VLMs in fine-grained perception. TWIN [9] introduces image-pair queries for instance discrimination, alongside the FGVQA suite across domains. While related in spirit, TWIN operates on natural images , whereas we study post-edit quality assessment , verifying whether a generated image preserves the source object under transformation. Additional benchmarks such as SEED-Bench [10, 17, 18] and MMBench [11] show degradation in tasks requiring compositional reasoning and instance-level understanding, and prior work reports hallucinations, weak visual grounding and 3D spatial relationships [12–15, 26, 27]. Our dataset captures these limitations in the context of generative editing, providing both binary decisions and structured failure modes. 

Multi-image reasoning and AutoQA-oriented evaluation. A complementary line of work studies multi-image reasoning. Visual Haystacks [28] evaluates long-context retrieval across many images, while Common-O Bench [29] examines cross-image reasoning by identifying shared objects, showing that models often hallucinate even when single-image perception is strong. MMIU [30] includes a comprehensive evaluation of multi-image task, OMIBench [31] evaluates olympiad-level reasoning across a multi-image scenario, Milebench [32] assess the capabilities of MLLM in multimodal long contexts, MuirBench [33] proposed a dataset for multiple-choice questions, while MIBench [34] divide the evaluation into three dimension: multi-image instruction, multimodal knowledge-seeking and multimodal in-context learning. Unlike retrieval or shared-object reasoning, we focus on visual consistency under generation : verifying that an edited image remains faithful to the source. This requires fine-grained correspondence reasoning over identity, pose, geometry, and attributes. Our dataset complements prior work by introducing an AutoQA benchmark for generative image editing, combining real and synthetic samples with structured annotations. 

3 Dataset 

We consider the problem of visual consistency in generative image editing, formulated as an AutoQA task. Given a source image and its edited counterpart, the objective is to determine whether the transformation preserves the semantic identity and fine-grained visual properties of the original object. While modern generative models enable complex edits such as background replacement, decluttering, and pose manipulation, they often introduce subtle artifacts, including geometric distortions, missing parts, or altered attributes. Detecting such inconsistencies is challenging and may not scale with manual inspection, motivating research into automated validation methods. Prior work further suggests that inconsistencies in product imagery could negatively affect downstream tasks such as retrieval and ranking [35, 36]. To study this problem, we introduce V-CAPE, a dataset designed for evaluating visual consis-tency under transformation. The dataset focuses on a representative and practically challenging scenario: transforming lifestyle images, which depict objects in complex environments, into studio-quality images where the same object is isolated on a clean background with a well-defined pose (cf. Fig. 2). This setting provides a natural benchmark for assessing whether models can verify object fidelity under non-trivial transformations. In contrast to datasets designed for generation [20, 22, 37], V-CAPE provides supervision tailored to verification. Each sample includes (i) a source image, (ii) a ground-truth reference image, (iii) a binary accept/reject label reflecting human judgment, and (iv) a structured rejection reason from a predefined taxonomy. This design enables evaluation of fine-grained consistency beyond coarse semantic alignment. V-CAPE comprises two complementary subsets (cf. Fig. 2). The V-CAPE-R subset contains edited images produced by generative mod-els, capturing realistic artifacts and failure modes observed in practice. The V-CAPE-S subset is constructed from 3D assets rendered via the Blender [38] API, enabling precise control over object geometry, pose, and appearance. Together, these subsets combine realism and controllability, enabling systematic analysis of AutoQA under both natural and controlled conditions.

3.2 V-CAPE-S Dataset Construction. While V-CAPE-R focuses on real-world generative outputs within a single category (sofa), V-CAPE-S extends the dataset to a broader and more controlled setting (19 addi-tional categories) using the ABO dataset [39] 1. ABO provides high-quality 3D assets with detailed geometry, textures, and physically-based materials, enabling precise control over object appearance and rendering conditions. We apply a two-stage filtering process (cf. Section A.2) to ensure diversity and reliability. At the product level, we retain categories requiring meaningful visual transformations and exclude trivial or hard-to-render cases (e.g., transparent or highly reflective objects). At the image level, we select 

lifestyle images xsrc using a lightweight classifier trained on CLIP [40] embeddings to distinguish them from studio-style images. This classifier achieves 90% precision and 82% recall, ensuring sufficiently challenging inputs.

Negative Pair Generation. To support fine-grained evaluation, we construct controlled negative examples that violate different aspects of visual consistency. These include incorrect pose, category mismatch, and incorrect instance within the same category. Such perturbations require reasoning over geometry, semantics, and identity. The final V-CAPE-S dataset contains 20K source–target pairs balanced between valid and invalid transformations. 

3.3 Quality Assessment and Annotation 

Each sample in V-CAPE is annotated with human judgments of visual consistency (cf. Fig. H.1). Three independent annotators evaluate each generated image given the source, optional reference, and output. Final labels are obtained via majority voting. We perform multiple alignment rounds (five) with a specific trained pool of annotators, achieving a substantial inter-annotators agreement (the Fleiss Kappa is 0.77). See Section B of Supplementary material for Annotation protocol and ethics. 

Label Taxonomy. Beyond binary labels, annotations include structured rejection reasons. For V-CAPE-R, the taxonomy captures common failure modes such as geometric distortions, incorrect pose, irrelevant objects, background inconsistencies, and texture or color deviations. For V-CAPE-S, the taxonomy aligns with the controlled setup: incorrect pose, wrong category, and wrong instance. This design enables evaluation of both binary decisions and structured reasoning. In contrast to datasets with only accept/reject labels [9], the proposed taxonomy provides richer supervision for identifying fine-grained consistency violations. 

4 Experiments 

We present a comprehensive evaluation of V-CAPE with the goal of assessing its effectiveness as a benchmark for fine-grained visual consistency and AutoQA. Our experiments are designed to address two key questions: (i) how well current VLMs can perform AutoQA under realistic editing transformations, and (ii) to what extent these models capture fine-grained visual properties that are critical for verifying object fidelity. 

4.1 Main Results 

We begin by evaluating a diverse set of VLMs on the AutoQA task defined by V-CAPE. 

Baselines. We group performances and aggregated statistics (mean ± std) by model size, considering “small ” (4–8B), “ medium ” (14–32B), “ large ” (128B+), and “ proprietary (n/a)”: (i) Open-weight VLMs : Qwen3 (8B, 32B, and 235B) [41], Gemma 3 [42] and Gemma 4 (4B and 31B) [43], and Mistral 3 (8B and 14B) [44]; (ii) Proprietary models : Claude (Sonnet, Haiku, and Opus) [45, 46], Nova (Lite 2 and Pro) [47, 48]. These models span a wide range of architectures, scales, and training paradigms, providing a representative snapshot of the current landscape. All models are queried through their native multimodal or chat interfaces using identical image inputs and output parsing. 

Prompting strategy. Since AutoQA performance is highly sensitive to prompt design, we evaluate two regimes. In the baseline setting, models are provided with a concise prompt describing the task and listing the quality criteria. In the auto-prompt setting, this prompt is refined using Intent-based Prompt Calibration [49], which iteratively improves the prompt by leveraging a small set of annotated examples and synthesizing challenging boundary cases. This allows us to assess whether prompt optimization alone can significantly improve AutoQA performance. 4.2 Zero-Shot Orientation Classification 

The AutoQA task requires reasoning about several fine-grained visual properties, among which object orientation is particularly critical. To isolate this factor, we evaluate zero-shot orientation classification independently of the AutoQA setting. Specifically, we assess how well different classes of vision models can recognize object pose without task-specific training. This analysis provides a controlled view of orientation understanding, disentangled from higher-level decision-making in AutoQA. We cast orientation recognition as a classification problem. In V-CAPE-S, each image is labeled as left view , right view , or front view . In V-CAPE-R, we map the original labels ( LEFT ,

RIGHT , FRONT , CORNER , OTHER ) to a binary distinction between side view ( LEFT , RIGHT , FRONT )and frontal view ( CORNER , OTHER ), reflecting the coarser orientation granularity in this subset. We exclude unreliable samples by removing incorrect-orientation cases in V-CAPE-S and retaining only accepted samples in V-CAPE-R. We evaluate three model families: (i) contrastive vision–language models (CLIP [40], SigLIP2 [50], EVA-02-CLIP [51]), where classification is performed via cosine similarity between image embeddings and text prototypes constructed from prompt ensembles; (ii) self-supervised vision encoders (DINOv2 [52], EUPE [53]), using a nearest-centroid classifier built from a small labeled support set; and (iii) orientation-specific models (OrientAnything [54], OrientAnything V2 [55]), which predict continuous 3D orientation and are mapped to discrete classes via calibrated azimuth thresholds. Fig. 6 summarizes the results. Overall, zero-shot orientation classification remains challenging. Contrastive VLMs achieve accuracy in the range 33–47% on V-CAPE-S, with a strong bias toward predicting front view. Self-supervised encoders provide more balanced performance; notably, self-supervised encoders reach 56.8% accuracy on V-CAPE-S with relatively uniform per-class F1. Orientation-specific models perform best achieving 91.1% accuracy on V-CAPE-R and ∼59% on V-CAPE-S. However, this performance relies on dataset-specific calibration of azimuth-to-class mappings. 

4.3 Few-Shot Fine-Tuning on V-CAPE 

The zero-shot VLM results in Section 4.1 demonstrate that general-purpose VLMs cannot reliably perform domain-specific quality assessment without adaptation. We investigate whether parameter-efficient fine-tuning with extremely limited labeled data can bridge this gap. Specifically, we fine-tune Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct [41] using LoRA [56] under a stratified few-shot regime, varying the number of training examples per defect category. 

Task formulation. Each training example is formatted as a Qwen3-VL multi-turn chat sequence.The user turn contains the source and generated images alongside a detailed evaluation prompt that 8instructs the model to follow a five-step reasoning protocol: (1) identify the product category, (2) check material and color consistency, (3) verify structural component counts, (4) assess configuration and design features, and (5) detect prohibited elements. The assistant turn provides the ground-truth binary label (accept/reject) with a chain-of-thought justification. This consistency prompt approach ensures that the model learns a structured evaluation procedure rather than memorizing surface-level patterns. 

Training data sampling. We employ a stratified sampling strategy that ensures balanced repre-sentation across all defect categories. For each dataset split, we sample N samples per rejection reason category plus N accepted (non-defective) samples, where N ∈ {1, 5, 10 }. Since V-CAPE-R contains six rejection categories (cf. Table A.1) plus one accepted class, yielding 7N samples per split. V-CAPE-S contains three rejection categories plus one accepted class, yielding 4N samples. The combined training sets therefore contain 11 N examples: 11 samples for N =1, 55 for N =5, and 110 for N =10 . Sampling is performed with a fixed random seed for reproducibility, and the selected indices are recorded in a manifest to ensure strict exclusion from the evaluation set. We provide additional details on the training procedure and LoRA configuration in the Appendix (Section G).

All three LoRA-adapted variants out-perform the baseline, with accu-racy improvements of 0.6–1.4 per-centage points on V-CAPE-R. Simply scaling the number of training examples does not guarantee monotonic improvement, the task requires the model to internalize subtle, category-specific visual criteria that may conflict across defect types when more heterogeneous examples are introduced. This finding highlights that the benchmark poses a challenge even for adapted models, and that more sophisticated training strategies are necessary. Third, the consistent gap between V-CAPE-R and V-CAPE-S performance (with the latter being higher across all configurations) suggests that synthetic defects may exhibit more systematic visual patterns that are easier to learn from few examples, whereas real-world quality issues are more diverse and context-dependent. 

5 Discussion 

We introduce V-CAPE, a large-scale benchmark for evaluating visual consistency in generative image editing. The dataset comprises 40 K source–target image pairs annotated by multiple human annotators with binary accept/reject labels and a structured taxonomy of failure modes capturing fine-grained inconsistencies such as geometric distortions, pose errors, and identity mismatches. Our experiments reveal a substantial gap between current model capabilities and the requirements of reliable verification. While effective at high-level semantic reasoning, VLMs are not yet reliable for instance-level verification tasks. In particular, the inability to consistently detect subtle inconsistencies limits their applicability in settings where correctness depends on precise visual fidelity. Moreover, the limited gains from prompt optimization suggest that the bottleneck lies not only in instruction design, but in the underlying perceptual capabilities of the models. 

Scope and outlook. V-CAPE focuses on a representative class of editing transformations (e.g., lifestyle-to-studio conversion) and a set of product-centric scenarios, enabling controlled and real-9istic evaluation of visual consistency. While this focus allows for precise analysis, extending the benchmark to broader domains, object categories, and transformation types would further stress-test model generalization. Similarly, while human annotations and structured failure modes provide rich supervision, future extensions could incorporate more granular signals (e.g., region-level or correspondence-based annotations) to support even finer-grained evaluation. Overall, V-CAPE provides a benchmark for studying visual consistency under transformation and highlights fundamental limitations of current multimodal systems. We hope this resource will enable the development of methods that more reliably capture fine-grained visual properties and advance automated quality assessment in generative image editing.

A The Dataset Generation Pipeline 

In this section, we provide additional details on the dataset construction we briefly described in the main part of the paper. 

A.1 V-CAPE-R 

Our goal is to transform an input product image so that it complies with predefined visual consistency requirements. To achieve this, we first describe the image sourcing component, which leverages object pose and symmetry classifiers to select suitable candidate images (Section A.1.1). We then present the image generation and editing module, responsible for background normalization, clutter removal, and object pose alignment (Section A.1.2). 

A.1.1 Image Sourcing and Candidate Selection 

The image sourcing component implements the selection function I⋆ = S(I, c) from Eq. (3.1), identifying the most suitable candidate image from real e-commerce product images set I for a given consistency specification c. This step is critical: choosing a source image that is already well aligned with the target specification reduces the extent of downstream edits and lowers the risk of geometric distortions or hallucinated details. According to our task, we would like to have an object which is left-facing, therefore, we prioritize source images that either a) already face left, or b) face right but exhibit geometric symmetry. In the latter case, pose correction can be achieved through deterministic horizontal mirroring, avoiding generative pose manipulation altogether. This strategy deliberately excludes asymmetric right-facing sofas from pose transformation, as such cases frequently lead to artifacts and structural inconsistencies in generative outputs. To implement this selection strategy, we introduce two classifiers that provide interpretable signals for candidate ranking: an Object Pose Classifier and an Object Symmetry Classifier , followed by a rule-based ranking and selection procedure, described below. 

Object Pose Classifier . This classifier predicts the orientation of a sofa image, assigning it to one of five discrete categories: a) left-facing , b) right-facing , c) frontal , d) corner , and e) other . The corner 

class captures multi-directional geometries such as L-shaped, U-shaped, or sectional sofas that lack a single dominant orientation, while the other class includes images with partial object visibility, heavy infographic overlays, or ambiguous viewpoints that are unsuitable for editing. Zero-shot classification with CLIP [40] proved ineffective at distinguishing LEFT from RIGHT orientations, motivating a supervised approach. We train the pose classifier on 7,000 manually annotated sofa images. Visual representations are extracted using a frozen DINOv2 [52] backbone, which provides robust embeddings without task-specific fine-tuning. Then, a lightweight two-layer MLP head maps these embeddings to pose categories. 

Object Symmetry Classifier . This binary classifier distinguishes symmetric from asymmetric sofa designs. Symmetry serves as a key signal for candidate selection: symmetric right-facing sofas can be converted to left-facing views via deterministic mirroring, eliminating the need for generative pose adjustment. Specifically, a) If the sourced image is left-facing, the cleaned image is passed directly to the downstream QA block without further modification; b) If the image is front-facing or right-facing, the cleaned image is passed to an object rotation network to enforce the canonical left-facing orientation; c) For right-facing but symmetric sofas, we first apply a deterministic horizontal mirroring operation to the source image, followed by background normalization only. In this case, generative pose editing is skipped entirely. For generative pose alignment, we use the Qwen-Edit-2509-Multiple-angles model (QEM) [4]. The desired rotation angle is specified explicitly in the editing prompt: we apply a rotation of −20 ◦ for front-facing sofas and −40 ◦ for right-facing sofas. These values are determined empirically to balance pose correction and geometric fidelity. This conditional design ensures that generative pose manipulation is applied only when strictly necessary, improving robustness and reducing hallucinations. After conditional editing, the resulting image ˆI is forwarded directly to the downstream QA pipeline. This stage verifies compliance with image quality standards and visual consistency requirements, ensuring that only images satisfying predefined acceptance criteria meet the quality standards defined by the benchmark. 

Prompt Design for Visual Cleanup. The visual cleanup stage relies on a carefully designed, deter-ministic text prompt that enforces strict constraints on background appearance, object preservation, and artifact suppression. The prompt is applied to a single input image selected by the image sourcing module and is reproduced below:             

> Place the {object} on a CLEAN, UNIFORM PURE WHITE background (NO gradients, no texture, no banding). The background must be perfectly continuous and consistent across the entire image. Do NOT change any attributes of the {object}. Keep the {object}’s position and orientation from the input image intact. If multiple {object}s or seating elements are present, KEEP ONLY ONE – the most prominent/central {object}. REMOVE everything from the {object} except pillows and cushions. REMOVE lighting and shadows from the input image. REMOVE all people, animals, food, decor, other furniture, coffee tables, electronics, and any visual clutter. No models, persons, and people in laying, seating or sleeping pose. ELIMINATE all INFOGRAPHICS, TEXT, LOGOS, LABELS, WATERMARKS, GRAPHIC OVERLAYS, or ICONS – the image must contain ONLY the sofa with decor elements inside the {object}. Ensure the entire {object} is FULLY VISIBLE (not cropped), CENTERED, and COMPLETELY ISOLATED. Only ONE sofa in the output image. Add light shadows UNDER the {object}. Preserve the object’s ORIGINAL SHAPE, POSE, GEOMETRY, and FINE DETAILS (e.g., cupholders, sockets, phone holders) with clean edges and no missing parts. Final result should appear PHOTOREALISTIC and free from any residual artifacts.

Here, "object" denotes a textual description of the input product. While the system supports both short object names (e.g., "sofa") and more detailed attribute-rich descriptions derived from product 3metadata, all experiments reported in this work use short object names for improved robustness and prompt stability. The visual cleanup model operates on single-view images only, using one selected catalog image as input. This design choice simplifies integration and improves runtime reliability, but introduces a known limitation: when parts of the object are heavily occluded in the input image, the model may fail to faithfully reconstruct the true underlying geometry (cf. A.2 V-CAPE-S Filtering Procedure. We implement a two-stage automatic filtering pipeline consisting of a) Product-level filtering: To ensure high-quality ⟨xsrc , xtrg ⟩ pairs, we implement an automatic filtering pipeline. We first exclude: i) product types with minimal transformation requirements (e.g., phone cases); ii) product types not associated with 3D models (ABO [39] provides 3D models for more than 7,900 

products); iii) product types where rendering is challenging due to material properties (e.g., mirrors, transparent objects); b) Image-level filtering: We then discard source images xsrc lacking “ lifestyle ”context. 

Target Image Generation. To create corresponding target images xtrg , we leverage the 3D models associated with each product sample. Specifically, using the Blender Python API 2, we render high-quality RGB images of the products at 1024 × 1024 resolution. Notably, for each source image xsrc ,we generate three target images xtrg corresponding to front , left , and right views of the object. For each view, we define a valid range of camera angles and randomly sample both an azimuth angle and an elevation angle relative to the object’s front-facing coordinate system. The camera is then positioned accordingly, ensuring the object remains within the field of view. 

Prompt Generation. We now describe how to generate the prompt psrc →trg , which specifies the desired transformation from source to target image. We begin with the following static template:  

> Generate an image of the {OBJECT_DESC} on a pure white background. Ensure strict preservation of the {OBJECT_CATEGORY}’s precise geometry, edges, intersections, and proportions. Remove people, info-graphics, and text. The entire {OBJECT_CATEGORY} is fully visible (not cropped) and isolated, no other objects or text in the scene. Ensure a soft natural lighting with gentle shadows. {ROTATION_INFORMATION}. The rotation coordinate system is the front-view of the object.

The template is shared across all samples, which we populate with sample-specific information, i.e.: Object category and description : we employ a VLM to generate a detailed description of the object and extract its category from xtrg (the product is rendered on a white background). Including a fine-grained description enables the editing model to disambiguate the target object when multiple objects of the same category are present in the source image. A manual inspection of 100 randomly sampled triplets revealed only 4 minor counting inaccuracies (e.g., number of sofa seats or drawers); and Rotation data : we encode the ground-truth camera rotation from Blender as a formatted string. We promote output diversity by selecting different system prompts and instruction wording during prompt construction.

G Fine-tuning on V-CAPE: Implementation Details 

In Section 4.3, we fine-tune Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct that supports multi-image inputs and instruction following. We apply LoRA to the query and value projection matrices ( q_proj , v_proj ) of the attention layers, with rank r=16 , scaling factor α=32 (effective scaling α/r = 2), and dropout 

p=0.05 . No bias terms are adapted. This configuration introduces approximately 0.1% additional trainable parameters relative to the frozen base model, enabling efficient adaptation on a single GPU. 

Training procedure. We train for 5 epochs using the AdamW optimizer [60] with a peak learning rate of 2 × 10 −4, a warmup ratio of 3%, and cosine decay. The effective batch size is 4 (per-device batch size of 1 with 4 gradient accumulation steps). We apply gradient clipping with a maximum norm of 0.3 and use bfloat16 mixed precision throughout. Gradient checkpointing is enabled to reduce memory consumption. For configurations with more than two training samples, we hold out 20% of the data as a validation set and select the checkpoint with the lowest evaluation loss; otherwise, we use the final checkpoint. Fig. E.3 reports the training loss progression for the N =5 configuration (11 training examples, 15 gradient steps over 5 epochs). The loss decreases monotonically from 4.93 at step 1 to 0.5 at step 110, with mean token accuracy improving from 42.0% to 92.0%. 

H Societal Impact 

This work introduces a benchmark for automated quality assessment of generative image editing, with a focus on preserving fine-grained visual fidelity in e-commerce product imagery. We discuss both the potential benefits and risks associated with this line of research. 

Positive impacts. Reliable automated quality assessment can reduce the need for large-scale manual annotation, lowering costs and improving turnaround times for content moderation in e-commerce platforms. By detecting subtle visual inconsistencies, such as altered geometry, incorrect pose, or hallucinated elements, AutoQA systems could help maintain product representation accuracy, supporting informed purchasing decisions and reducing return rates driven by misleading imagery. More broadly, our benchmark highlights critical limitations of current vision-language models in fine-grained visual understanding, which may guide future model development toward more robust and trustworthy visual reasoning. 

Potential risks. The dataset and methods presented here could lower the barrier to deploying generative editing pipelines at scale, potentially enabling the creation of misleading or manipulated product imagery if quality controls are circumvented rather than enforced. While our work is designed to detect such failures, the same understanding of model blind spots could, in principle, be used to craft adversarial edits that evade automated checks. Additionally, increased automation of visual quality assessment may reduce demand for human annotators and content reviewers, with implications for workers currently employed in these roles. We note, however, that our results demonstrate that fully automated assessment remains far from reliable, suggesting that human oversight will remain essential for the foreseeable future. 

Data considerations. V-CAPE-R is constructed from e-commerce catalog imagery and does not contain personally identifiable information. V-CAPE-S is derived from the publicly available Amazon Berkeley Objects dataset [39], which consists of 3D product assets. All human annotations were collected through structured annotation tasks with trained annotators.
