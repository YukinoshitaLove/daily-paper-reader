## ABSTRACT 

A user history can admit multiple plausible next interactions, yet conventional sequential recommenders produce a single deterministic ranking. While diffu-sion models offer a natural way to sample alternative continuations, we show that existing target-item diffusion recommenders often suffer from severe sampling collapse, producing nearly identical outputs across independent draws. We iden-tify two ubiquitous design choices that promote this collapse: joint learning of the latent space and denoiser, and in-trajectory supervision via discrete losses. To address these failure modes, we introduce DARTS (Diverse and Accurate 

Recommendation Through Sampling), a three-stage latent diffusion framework that explicitly decouples representation learning, generative modeling, and dis-crete ranking by learning and freezing the latent space before diffusion training and postponing ranking supervision to the final decoding stage. We further char-acterize sampling diversity using complementary measures of variation before and after decoding, and use posterior-predictive aggregation to exploit this diversity at inference. Across three Amazon Reviews 2023 categories, DARTS preserves substantially greater sampling diversity while outperforming strong sequential, generative-retrieval, and diffusion baselines by 7–12% on standard ranking met-rics. Aggregating multiple samples further improves ranking accuracy at a fixed slate size. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Sequential recommendation aims to predict the conditional distribution of users’ future interactions given their past behavior. Most commonly, these systems are trained to predict the next item given the preceding interactions. Given the diversity of user behaviors and the large size of the item space, these conditional distributions can be difficult to model accurately. The dominant approach relies on Transformer-based recommenders, from SASRec (Kang & McAuley, 2018) and BERT4Rec (Sun et al., 2019) to their extensions to industrial-scale cata-logues (Zhai et al., 2024) and semantic identifiers (Rajput et al., 2023). Despite architectural differ-ences, they share one training paradigm: the history is encoded into a single representation, scored against the catalogue, and optimised with a catalogue cross-entropy or ranking loss. Although ef-fective, such discriminative models may struggle to capture the diversity of user behaviour. Their output distribution can in principle be sampled to obtain varied predictions, but it does not faithfully reflect that diversity, and sampling from it degrades accuracy (Figure 4). These models are therefore used deterministically, returning the same ranking for the same history. Diffusion models (Ho et al., 2020; Song et al., 2021) provide an alternative to discriminative models for learning complex distributions, and a growing body of work has recently incorporated them into recommender systems (Wang et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2024; Li et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2025; Hu et al., 2025). Because generation starts from random noise, two samples conditioned on the same user history can, in principle, lead to different items. This gives diffusion-based recommenders the potential to preserve multiple plausible continuations rather than collapsing them into a single prediction (Li et al., 2024; Zolghadr et al., 2024). In practice, however, 1arXiv:2610.36954v1 [cs.IR] 27 Oct 2026 Under review as a conference paper at ICLR 2027 we find that this diversity largely fails to materialize. In most settings, repeatedly sampling existing diffusion recommenders for the same user history yields nearly identical rankings and only exhibit a weak diversity (Section 4.3), so that the resulting sampler behaves almost deterministically. We attribute this behavior to two design choices. First, item embeddings are learned jointly with the denoiser, allowing the latent space to reshape itself until little uncertainty remains for the denoising process to model. Second, the denoiser’s intermediate outputs are supervised using catalogue-level cross-entropy or ranking losses, which encourage different denoising trajectories to converge to-wards the same high-scoring items. Said otherwise, relevance based recommenders naturally pro-duce homogeneous and redundant lists. In this setting, we observe a tension between diversity and recommendation performance, as measured by accuracy metrics such as Hit Rate and NDCG: in-creasing diversity typically comes at the expense of recommendation accuracy, and vice versa. This is known as the diversity-accuracy dilemma Kenny et al. (2024). Successful continuous generative models avoid these design choices. Latent diffusion models for images fix the representation before training the generative model (Rombach et al., 2022), while recent continuous language models such as ELF learn the mapping back to discrete tokens only at the final stage (Hu et al., 2026). We carry these two principles over to sequential recommendation and introduce DARTS (Diverse and Accurate Recommendation Through Sampling), a generative recommendation model that can simultaneously improve prediction diversity and recommendation quality relative to the discrimi-native and diffusion-based baselines considered in our experiments. To the best of our knowledge, DARTS is among the first generative sequential recommenders to improve both sampling diversity and ranking performance, suggesting that generative modeling can capture multiple plausible user continuations without sacrificing recommendation quality. DARTS proceeds in three stages. We first pretrain a sequence autoencoder and freeze its latent space. We then train a denoiser in this fixed space using only a regression loss, without catalogue-level supervision. Finally, we fine-tune the decoder with a ranking objective. Sampling thus takes place entirely in the continuous latent space, allowing repeated draws to remain diverse. Our contributions are the following. • DARTS, a three-stage latent diffusion model for sequential recommendation that separates representation learning, generative training, and catalogue decoding (Section 3). • Sampling diagnostics, complementary to ranking metrics, that measure the diversity of re-peated draws in latent and item space. With them, we show that existing diffusion recom-menders collapse to near-deterministic sampling, and we trace this collapse to joint latent training and in-trajectory catalogue supervision. • Experiments on three categories of Amazon Reviews 2023 (Hou et al., 2026), where DARTS improves over discriminative, generative-retrieval, and diffusion baselines by 6.8– 12.0% on standard ranking metrics. Aggregating several posterior samples into a single slate of fixed size gives further gains. 

## 2 RELATED WORK 

Sequential recommendation. GRU4Rec (Hidasi et al., 2016) applied recurrent networks to in-teraction sequences, and later models moved to transformer encoders. SASRec (Kang & McAuley, 2018) is trained with causal next-item prediction, BERT4Rec (Sun et al., 2019) with masked item modelling, and S 3-Rec (Zhou et al., 2020) adds further self-supervised objectives. More recent work scales this recipe to industrial catalogues (Zhai et al., 2024), or retrieves items by generating their semantic identifiers autoregressively (Rajput et al., 2023; Zheng et al., 2025). Semantic-identifier models are generative too, but over discrete item codes. In contrast, DARTS samples continuous next-item representations, so the same history can produce different rankings. 

Diffusion models for recommendation. Diffusion recommenders aim at generating conditional recommendation distribution. They fall into two families. DiffRec (Wang et al., 2023) and DDRM (Zhao et al., 2024) diffuse a user’s interaction vector, while the second family, which in-cludes DARTS, diffuses a representation of the target item conditioned on the history. In this sec-ond family, DiffuRec (Li et al., 2024) denoises the target embedding with a transformer, Dream-Rec (Yang et al., 2023) adds classifier-free guidance, and ADRec (Chen et al., 2025) warm-starts its 2Under review as a conference paper at ICLR 2027 denoiser from collaborative embeddings. Zolghadr et al. (2024) study the effect of offset noise and cross-attention. Other methods change the training signal or the corruption process. PreferDiff (Liu et al., 2025) derives a ranking loss compatible with diffusion, and PreferGrow (Hu et al., 2025) diffuses discrete preference ratios. Most target-item methods learn the item representation together with the denoiser, and many add item-level losses during denoising. DARTS freezes a pretrained latent space, trains the denoiser by latent regression only, and applies ranking supervision only to the final decoder. 

Latent and continuous generative modelling. Latent diffusion (Rombach et al., 2022) separates representation learning from generation by training and freezing an autoencoder first, then fitting the diffusion model in its latent space. In language, Diffusion-LM (Li et al., 2022) diffuses in embedding space but repeatedly projects back onto tokens. ELF (Hu et al., 2026) instead stays continuous until a single final discretisation, and shows that this improves both generation quality and efficiency. We bring both ideas, a fixed latent space and one final discretisation, to recommendation, and test whether they keep sampling stochastic when tokens are replaced by catalogue items. 

## 3 METHOD 

3.1 OVERVIEW 

DARTS is composed by three architectural components: an encoder, a decoder, and a denoising diffusion model. The training of these components is realized in three separated stages that are key for a diffusion model for recommendation : the representation learning of the catalogue item in a latent space, the latent generation, and the item ranking. Each stage updates a different set of parameters of the model. 1. Sequence autoencoder pretraining. The encoder and decoder are trained with masked item modelling (Devlin et al., 2019; Sun et al., 2019), which defines a latent space tied to the item catalogue. 2. Latent diffusion. With the encoder and decoder frozen, a conditional denoiser learns to recover the latent of the next item from its noisy version, using the user history as context. 3. Decoder finetuning. With the encoder and denoiser frozen, the decoder is finetuned on sampled latents to rank the true next item first, which corrects the shift between the latents it was pretrained on and those generated at inference. Catalogue cross-entropy supervision should not be used inside the diffusion process. ## 5 DISCUSSION 

Existing diffusion recommenders are generative by construction, yet their repeated samples yield nearly identical recommendation lists. We traced this collapse to two design choices, learning the representation jointly with the denoiser and supervising the reverse process with catalogue losses, and showed by ablation that each one sharply reduces latent variation and degrades ranking. Re-moving both keeps stochastic variation through decoding while the model leads every comparison in Table 1, and averaging several draws further improves all four ranking metrics (Figure 6), a gain that injecting noise into a discriminative model does not provide (Appendix E). 

Limitations and future work. Leave-one-out evaluation observes a single next interaction, so ranking metrics cannot tell whether alternative samples are plausible futures. ## G INFERENCE COST 

Section 4.4 shows that aggregating multiple independently sampled predictions can further improve ranking performance. This comes at an additional inference cost, since each draw requires a com-plete reverse diffusion process. We therefore measure the serving cost of DARTS and the two baselines that use the same item representations. 

Protocol. We report throughput as the number of complete predictions processed per second. A prediction includes history encoding, reverse diffusion, decoding of the final position, and catalogue scoring. All models use a latent width of 64 , a catalogue of 25 ,848 items, and left-padded histories of length 50 . Measurements are performed at batch size 1024 on a single NVIDIA V100 (32 GB). DARTS uses 200 reverse diffusion steps and DiffuRec uses 32 steps (as stated in the paper), match-ing the configurations used for the results in Table 1. For M > 1, one complete prediction comprises 

M independently sampled reverse chains and their aggregation using Equation 12 with p = 1 .

Comparison with DiffuRec. At a single draw ( M = 1 ), DARTS achieves a throughput of 2,102 

predictions per second, compared with 590 for DiffuRec+T5, while achieving an 11 .1% higher NDCG@10 as reported in Table 1. Thus, relative to DiffuRec+T5, DARTS is both faster and more accurate at the single-sample operating point. This result is due to the substantially lower cost of each reverse step in our denoiser. DARTS cross-attends from a single latent query token to the encoded history, resulting in a per-step latency of 

2.4 ms at this batch size. In contrast, DiffuRec re-encodes the complete item sequence with a four-block transformer at every diffusion step, requiring 54 ms per step. The lower per-step cost therefore more than compensates for the 6.25 × larger number of reverse steps in DARTS.
