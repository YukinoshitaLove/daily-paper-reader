Freeze What Is Settled, Cache What Is Smooth:
Spatial Freezing Keeps Step Caching in Its
Low-Error Regime

Abstract—Instruction-based image editors built on diffusion
transformers run tens of denoising steps. Training-free acceler-
ation either reuses features across steps (step caching) or stops
computing the regions an edit leaves untouched (region freezing),
and the two have been combined before. We ask how a high
speed-up target should be split between them. On Qwen-Image-
2.1, freezing does more than remove tokens: it lowers the cost of
every step, so the step cache can reach the same overall speed-
up with a conservative threshold that keeps its error small. A
per-step cost model fitted on development runs predicts held-
out latency with a median error of 4.0% and reproduces the
speed ceilings of the region methods we re-implemented (2.73×
predicted vs. 2.70× measured for RegionE). Driving frozen tokens
to the model’s one-step x0 estimate beats returning them to the
reference image on every development sample, and in 90% of
measured cases the composed error stays below the sum of the
two individual errors. The resulting method, FreezePlan, keeps
five full steps and gives the rest of the budget to TaylorSeer.
On a pre-registered test split it runs 5.05× faster end to end at
29.8 dB PSNR to the original output, with no significant drop
in a VLM judge score; TaylorSeer at 5.02× reaches 23.5 dB.
The curve extends to 5.99× at 28.0 dB. Swapping in the region
selectors of prior methods under our schedule lowers median
PSNR by only 0.5–0.6 dB, which places the gain in the schedule
and budget split rather than in the selector.
Index Terms—image editing, diffusion transformers, inference
acceleration, feature caching, token freezing
I. INTRODUCTION
Diffusion-transformer editors such as FLUX.1 Kontext [1],
Qwen-Image [2] and Qwen-Image-2.1 [3] follow free-form
instructions with high fidelity, but every edit runs tens of de-
noising steps. On one RTX 4090, a 1024-pixel edit with Qwen-
Image-2.1 takes 24.5 s over 40 steps, and a full transformer
step costs about 0.58 s. Training-free acceleration follows two
routes. Step caching [4]–[14] reuses or extrapolates features
across steps. Region freezing [15], [16] exploits the fact that
an edit usually changes a small part of the image and stops
computing the rest.
Editing has both spatial and temporal redundancy, and prior
work already exploits the two together. EEdit [17] combines
spatial locality with step skipping for inversion-based editing,
RegionE [16] couples region partition, region K/V caching
and velocity caching, and SpotEdit [15] stacks its region
skipping with TeaCache and TaylorSeer. Budget-constrained
scheduling has precedents as well: BudCache [18] searches
step-level caching policies under a fixed number of function
evaluations, and ProCache [19] searches constrained caching
schedules with selective token computation. None of these
works explains how the errors and costs of freezing and
caching interact, and that interaction decides how a high
speed-up target should be split between them.
On Qwen-Image-2.1 both routes fall short beyond about
4× (Table II). TaylorSeer drops from 32.8 dB at 4.1× to
23.5 dB at 5.0× (SSIM 0.97→0.71), and the region methods
we re-implemented with their original schedules stop at 2.70×
(RegionE) and 3.90× (SpotEdit) even with TaylorSeer added.
Other works report higher speed-ups on other models, e.g.,
6.24× for HyCa [13] on Qwen-Image and up to 10× for
SpecEdit [20], under different quality protocols or acceleration
mechanisms.
We treat high-speed-up editing as a computation-budget
split under a fidelity constraint and make three observations.
(i) Under Euler sampling, a frozen token driven toward a fixed
target reaches it exactly (Prop. 1); its error is set by the target,
and the model’s one-step x0 estimate is a better target than
the reference image. (ii) A linear per-step cost model explains
real latency; the number of full steps sets the speed ceiling,
and freezing lowers the cost of every other step. (iii) For
the same overall speed-up, the step cache can therefore use
a conservative threshold, and in 90% of measured cases the
composed error stays below the sum of the individual errors.
Our contributions are:
• A per-step cost model, validated out of sample, that
explains the speed ceilings of the region methods we re-
implemented and motivates spending few full steps and
a conservative cache (Sec. III-C).
• Measurements of frozen-token targets and composed er-
rors (Secs. III-B, III-D).
• FreezePlan (Fig. 1), which computes only active tokens
on the native prefix KV cache of Qwen-Image-2.1 and
outperforms every tested method that reaches 5–6× on a
pre-registered test split. A schedule-matched comparison
attributes the gain to the schedule rather than to the region

II. RELATED WORK
Step caching. DeepCache [4], FORA [5] and ∆-DiT [6]
reuse features of neighbouring steps; TeaCache [7] and DB-
Cache [14] decide adaptively when to reuse; ToCa [8] and
DuCa [9] select tokens; TaylorSeer [10], FoCa [11] and Fre-
qCa [12] forecast instead of copying; HyCa [13] assigns dif-
ferent solvers to different feature dimensions. These methods
improve the cache operator. We leave the operator unchanged
and reduce the share of the speed-up it must deliver, so
stronger operators are complementary.
Budget-constrained schedules. BudCache [18] and Pro-
Cache [19] allocate a budget along the step axis. We study
how a second axis, spatial freezing, moves the operating point
of the step axis.
Region-aware editing. SpotEdit [15] selects regions with a
perceptual score, returns frozen regions to the reference and re-
decides at fixed steps. RegionE [16] partitions with a one-step
x0 estimate, drives frozen regions to that estimate, keeps their
K/V and caches velocities of the edited region. EEdit [17]
targets inversion-based editing; SpecEdit [20] uses dynamic
resolution. Our frozen-token target and K/V retention follow
RegionE; we differ in the analysis and in the schedule it leads
to. RAS [21], HSA [22] and HetCache [23] likewise keep K/V
of inactive tokens in generation.
Fewer steps and distillation. Fewer steps or step distil-
lation [24]–[26] change the sampling trajectory and hence
the output; we compare against fewer steps and discuss the
difference in Sec. IV-D.

C. A per-step cost model
We model the end-to-end time of one edit as
T = Tfix + nfull C + ncomp (¯ρ C + o) + ncache c,
(1)
where nfull, ncomp and ncache count full steps, compact steps
over active tokens and cached steps, ¯ρ is the mean active
fraction, and Tfix covers text encoding and VAE. A least-
squares fit on 968 development runs (22 configurations ×
44 samples) gives Tfix = 1.48 s, C = 0.575 s, o = 0.005 s
and c = 0.012 s (R2 = 0.997). Without refitting, the model
predicts the 1056 runs of the test split, including configurations
absent from the development set (fewer steps, SpotEdit+TS,
RegionE+TS), with a median absolute time error of 4.0% (90th
percentile 7.9%).
Setting every non-full step to zero cost gives a speed ceiling
that depends only on nfull (Table I), in the spirit of Amdahl’s
law [29]. RegionE’s schedule (9 warm-up, 1 forced refresh,
3 smoothing steps) has 13 full steps and a predicted ceiling
of 2.73×; with TaylorSeer we measure 2.70×. SpotEdit keeps
7 full steps and also decodes for its perceptual selector; its
ceiling is 4.45× and we measure 3.90×. The limit comes from
the original schedules rather than from region freezing itself;
under our schedule the same selectors exceed 5× (Sec. IV-C).

IV. EXPERIMENTS
A. Setup
We use Qwen-Image-2.1 [3] (40 steps, CFG=1, 1024 px,
bf16) on one RTX 4090 and the English part of GEdit-
Bench [30] (11 edit types). Within each type, samples are
ordered by the SHA-1 of their key and fixed positions form
disjoint splits of 44 samples: development (all design choices),
test A and test B (main). Configurations and pass criteria of
each test split were written to a hashed contract before any
output was generated; one earlier test run with a different
selection rule was stopped at 12/43 samples because its con-
figuration could not reach 5× (Appendix H). We report PSNR
and SSIM [31] to the 40-step output (also on truly changed
tokens), the GEdit VIEScore prompts [30], [32] judged by
Qwen3-VL-8B [33] as a paired difference to the dense out-
put with bootstrap 95% CIs (∆VLM), and the median real
end-to-end speed-up measured with CUDA events. Baselines
are TaylorSeer and DBCache with official cache-dit settings
(δ = 0.3–1.0), fewer steps (8/7/6), and SpotEdit and RegionE
ported from their official code with TaylorSeer as the step
cache; every baseline is pushed to the highest speed it reaches.
Each sample is generated once with a fixed seed (20261023),
shared by all methods. All runs use one RTX 4090 (48 GB);
the test split takes about 2.3 GPU-hours for 24 configurations
and 1.4 more for VLM judging.

significant drop for FreezePlan (−0.06, CI [−0.15, +0.01]);
this is not a proof of equivalence. DBCache and fewer steps
give the same picture in PSNR. Neither region method reaches

C. Schedule-matched selectors and ablations
To separate the schedule from the region selector, we kept
the schedule and cache of FreezePlan fixed and swapped
only the selector (Table III, top; a post-hoc comparison, not
pre-registered). All three selectors exceed 5×. Their median
PSNR is 0.5–0.6 dB below that of FreezePlan, and the paired
per-sample difference is 0.26 dB (95% CI 0.08–0.46) against
RegionE and 0.29 dB (0.08–0.73) against SpotEdit. RegionE’s
partition freezes more tokens and runs faster, so it lies close
to the same speed–fidelity curve. ∆VLM differences between
selectors are not significant. The gain of FreezePlan therefore
comes mainly from the schedule and budget split, not from
the selector. Removing one component at a time (Table III,
bottom) shows that the x0 anchor matters most; the refresh is
a speed–fidelity knob rather than a requirement: dropping it
trades 0.21 dB for 9.5% more speed.
D. Relation to fewer steps
Six steps reach 5.17× at 22.2 dB but lose only 0.23 VLM
points: a shorter trajectory yields a different yet plausible
image. We target reproducing the output the original model


produces for a given input and seed, on which FreezePlan is
7.4 dB better; it is also slightly better in ∆VLM (+0.17, CI
[+0.05, +0.30]). The two approaches are orthogonal and can
be combined.
E. Qualitative results
Fig. 5 shows an edit on an original anime illustration
that is not part of GEdit-Bench. We generated five such
illustrations with Qwen-Image-2.1 and edited them with the
configurations of split B; the figure shows the one with the
smallest gain of FreezePlan over the speed-matched baselines.
Flat colour regions make step-caching artefacts easy to see:
at 5×, TaylorSeer and DBCache turn the cake into noise,
while FreezePlan matches the dense output. Appendix K
shows all five illustrations and split-B samples chosen at fixed
percentiles of the PSNR gain.
V. LIMITATIONS
(1) The fallback fired on 11/44 test samples (25%), where
FreezePlan is slightly below TaylorSeer at the same threshold
(details and a fix in Appendix I). (2) Newly added small
objects occasionally show faint ghosting near the frozen
boundary; we have not quantified it. (3) All results are on
Qwen-Image-2.1 and rely on its prefix KV cache; the cost
coefficients are specific to one GPU. (4) The x0 anchor lets
unedited regions follow the dense model’s slight re-rendering
instead of copying the input. (5) Each test split has 44 samples
generated with one seed, the VLM judge is a local 8B model
rather than a commercial one, and the selector comparison of
Sec. IV-C was added after the test results were known.

APPENDIX B
SCOPE OF PROPOSITION 1
For a token frozen at step k0 with a fixed anchor c,
xk −c = (σk/σk0)(xk0 −c) for all k ≥k0. The frozen
trajectory is therefore fixed by its start, its anchor and the
noise schedule; computation and caching of active tokens
do not enter it. The token reaches c only if the anchor is
held until σK = 0. At a refresh step r the token is still at
c + (σr/σk0)(xk0 −c); the full step there computes a new
anchor ˆx0(σr), which FreezePlan holds to the end. That anchor
is computed on a trajectory shaped by the earlier cached steps,
so the final frozen latent depends on the cache before r. Frozen
K/V also enter the attention of active tokens, and the VAE
decoder mixes neighbouring latents, so pixel errors are not
strictly separable. Sec. III-B claims only that between refreshes
the frozen latents do not depend on the concurrent cache.

APPENDIX E
REFRESH AS A SPEED–FIDELITY KNOB
On split B, FreezePlan with a refresh reaches 5.05× at
29.77 dB and without it 5.53× at 29.56 dB; both lie on the
speed–fidelity front. On the development set the refresh adds
2.1 dB when no step cache is used (35.1 vs. 33.0 dB), so its
benefit shrinks once the cache error dominates.
APPENDIX F
ALTERNATIVES THAT DID NOT WORK [DEV]
• Per-band anchor mixing: the optimal weights on the x0
anchor are 1.02–1.16 in every frequency band, and the
gain over the plain x0 anchor is at most 0.7 dB.
• Extrapolating ˆx0(σ) to σ = 0 with polynomials over
the warm-up steps is 4–56 dB worse than the reference
anchor; the x0 trajectory drifts before it converges.
• Partial refresh: the error of the x0 anchor is spread out
(the worst 10% of tokens carry 17% of it), and decision-
time predictors correlate weakly with it (Spearman ≤
0.38). Refreshing 30% of tokens recovers 1.0 dB versus
3.8 dB for a full refresh.
APPENDIX G
WHY THE RE-IMPLEMENTED REGION METHODS STAY
BELOW 5×
RegionE. Its schedule keeps 13 full steps (9 warm-up, 1
forced update, 3 smoothing steps; rescaled from its 28-step
setting). The cost model caps it at 2.73×; we measure 2.70×
with TaylorSeer. With its own AVDCache, raising the cache
threshold from 0.03 to 0.1 or 0.2 produced identical outputs:
the official rule recomputes every step at which the decay ratio
γ(1 −∆σ) is at least 1, independently of the threshold.
SpotEdit. Its schedule keeps 7 full steps (4 initial steps and
resets at 10, 18, 25) and decodes at each decision for its LPIPS
selector; the ceiling is 4.45× and we measure 3.90×.
Schedule-matched comparison [A6]. Under the sched-
ule and cache of FreezePlan, both selectors exceed 5×
(Table III). Their ∆VLM to the dense output is +0.05
[−0.17, +0.41] (RegionE) and −0.16 [−0.32, −0.02] (SpotE-
dit), against −0.06 [−0.15, +0.01] for FreezePlan; the paired
differences to FreezePlan are −0.11 [−0.46, +0.09] and +0.09
[−0.01, +0.22], neither significant. A reference anchor under
the same schedule costs 1.9 dB (Table III, bottom).

APPENDIX L
RELATION TO HYCA, BUDCACHE AND PROCACHE
HyCa (ICLR 2026) assigns different extrapolation solvers
to different feature dimensions and reports 6.24× on Qwen-
Image and Qwen-Image-Edit. It improves the step-cache op-
erator and could replace TaylorSeer inside FreezePlan. We
did not port it to the prefix KV cache of Qwen-Image-2.1;
its reported numbers use other models, quality criteria and
samples and are not directly comparable to ours. BudCache
(ICML 2026) searches step-level caching policies under a fixed
number of function evaluations (up to about 3.7× on FLUX.1
and Wan2.1), and ProCache (AAAI 2026) searches constrained
caching schedules with selective token computation (up to
about 2.9×). Both allocate the budget along the step axis.
We study how spatial freezing shifts the operating point of
that axis, so these schedulers could serve as the temporal
component of FreezePlan.
