Abstract
One-step distillation turns pretrained diffusion models into fast generators, but strong
results often require substantial training machinery and compute. Pure adversarial
distillation offers a simpler route, yet has struggled at modern text-to-image scale. To
address this limitation, we propose ROAD (Representation-grounded One-step Adversarial
Distillation), a one-stage framework that distills text-to-image models with one adversarial
objective and a lightweight representation-space critic. First, we replace the classical
convolutional head with a Lipschitz-controlled Transformer head with token-level text
conditioning, which matches the semantic tokens of modern visual encoders. Second,
we observed a fixed random feature mixing leads to long-horizon degradation. We
attribute it to generator–critic co-adaptation around one realization-specific view, and
periodically re-draw the mixing to keep the randomization effective. Third, for our dense
and vector-valued logits, we introduce Jacobian R1 which penalizes the full input–logit
Jacobian, overcoming pooled R1’s limitation of neglecting most of logit sensitivities. A
single adversarial objective then suffices: in one step, ROAD reaches 11.03 FID on SDXL
and 13.73 on SD3.5-M on COCO-10K, surpassing the multi-step teachers at a fraction of
distribution matching’s training compute.

1 Introduction
Diffusion and flow models have achieved remarkable quality in text-to-image generation, but
their iterative sampling remains expensive at inference time. Step distillation therefore aims
to compress a many-step generator into one or a few evaluations. Paradoxically, however, the
strongest one-step methods often simplify inference by making training substantially more
complicated: distribution matching introduces an online score network at base-model scale [2–5],
while competitive systems commonly rely on additional objectives, auxiliary models, or multiple
stages (Tab. 2, Sec. D). Thus, one-step inference has become simple, but one-step training has not.
Adversarial distillation offers a strikingly simpler alternative. Rather than matching scores or
trajectories, it can train a one-step generator directly against real data with a single adversarial
objective, without an online score network or repeated teacher queries. Yet this simplicity has not
carried over to modern text-to-image models. Pure adversarial training at this scale has struggled
with stability and prompt alignment [1, 6]. As a result, adversarial losses are typically used only to
assist a heavier distillation objective [6, 7]. When adversarial training becomes the main objective,
existing methods often recover performance by introducing other sources of complexity, such as
teacher-generated supervision, previously distilled generators, or large trainable critics [8–10].
This leaves open a more fundamental question: is pure adversarial distillation itself unable to scale,
or is the critic used to train it the actual bottleneck?
We investigate the latter possibility. The critic used in adversarial distillation is largely inherited
from projected GANs [11, 12], while the representation space it operates on has changed sub-
stantially. Modern visual encoders such as DINOv3 and Perception Encoder already map images
into contextual, semantically organized token representations [13, 14]. Yet these representations
are still read through lightweight convolutional heads; the random feature transform is sam-
pled once and held fixed throughout training; and gradient regularization acts only on a scalar
obtained by pooling the dense critic outputs. We find that these seemingly innocuous design
choices become increasingly restrictive at modern model scale. They limit how the critic interprets

semantic representations, allow its judgments to become increasingly tied to a single feature view
during prolonged optimization, and leave individual logit sensitivities insufficiently controlled.
This diagnosis leads us to revisit pure adversarial distillation with a modern critic tailored to
today’s representations and optimization dynamics, making its simple training recipe effective
at text-to-image scale. We instantiate this idea in ROAD, Representation-grounded One-step
Adversarial Distillation.
Reading modern representations with modern ViT heads. The inherited convolutional
readout builds a visual pyramid over fixed neighborhoods, whereas modern token representations
call for a head that selects and relates semantic evidence by content. We therefore use ViT heads
made viable for adversarial training through Lipschitz controls, while cross-attention to individual
prompt tokens improves text alignment. The resulting critic clearly outperforms similar-sized
convolutional heads in fidelity and alignment (Sec. 3.1).
Keeping the random mixing random. Projected critics read the frozen features through a
random mixing, drawn once and held fixed so that the critic does not rely on a few dominant
feature directions [11]. This protection, however, does not last over long horizons: training
under a fixed draw converges quickly, then oscillates and slowly deteriorates. We attribute this
to generator–critic co-adaptation around one persistent view of the representation: directions
measured weakly by that view can remain poorly corrected. ROAD periodically redraws the mixing
while carrying forward the generator, heads, and optimizer states. This changes the feature view
without restarting learning, limits prolonged co-adaptation, and sustains improvement where
fixed-mixing runs deteriorate (Sec. 3.2).
Adapting R1 to dense, vector-valued critics. The classical R1 penalty regularizes a scalar
critic through its input gradient on real images and is a staple of stable GAN training [15–17].
ROAD’s critic, however, is dense and vector-valued, and the adversarial loss acts on every logit,
whereas the customary extension pools the logits into a scalar before applying R1 [18–20]. Pooling
keeps only one direction of the critic’s output space, so the input–logit Jacobian’s sensitivities in
every other direction, however large, never enter the penalty. The proposed Jacobian R1 (JR1)
instead penalizes every logit’s input gradient, i.e., the squared Frobenius norm of the input–logit
Jacobian. A single unbiased Hutchinson probe makes it practical with one vector–Jacobian product
(Sec. 3.3).
ROAD proves to be a simple, low-cost, and strong one-step distillation method: with one loss
and one stage, it reaches 11.03 FID on SDXL and 13.73 on SD3.5-M on COCO-10K. On SDXL, it
reaches the best FID of ODE-initialized DMD2 using 1/9.25 of the training GPU-hours (Fig. 1).

3 Scaling Pure Adversarial Distillation with a Representation-Grounded Critic
ROAD trains the one-step student with the dense adversarial losses and the JR1 penalty against
a projected critic assembled from three parts: the frozen encoder, the random mixings, which
ROAD periodically re-draws, and trainable Transformer heads that attend to the prompt’s text
tokens and emit dense per-position logits (Fig. 2). The teacher appears only as the student’s
initialization.
3.1 A Transformer Head for Semantic Token Representations
A natural starting point is to retain the convolutional discriminator design of Projected GAN [11].
In our one-step distillation setting, this convolutional (Conv) readout remains trainable but
underperforms in both image quality and prompt adherence, and simply increasing its capacity
(Deep Conv) further degrades performance, as shown in rows 1 and 2 of Tab. 1, respectively.
We attribute this to a change in what the trainable head is for. A modern ViT backbone such as
DINOv3 already maps an image into a contextual, semantic representation space, and every tap
returns the same number of tokens. The head need not rebuild the visual pyramid that projected

GAN’s convolutional heads construct; it has only to decide which tokens matter and how they
relate. Convolution aggregates over predetermined neighborhoods with fixed, translation-shared
weights, an inductive bias suited to building a hierarchy from raw pixels rather than to reading
backbone features: two tokens are combined because they are adjacent, not because their content
is related. Self-attention decides instead from the tokens themselves which of them are combined
and how strongly, which makes a Transformer head the more promising choice.
Lipschitz control, however, is necessary to make this replacement viable in the adversarial
game [22, 23]. It limits how strongly the critic can amplify changes in its input and thereby
constrains abrupt variation in the signal returned to the generator. A naive Transformer head
collapses in our setting (Tab. 1, row 3). This failure is consistent with a known difficulty: standard
dot-product softmax attention is not globally Lipschitz in its inputs, even when the learned linear
maps have bounded spectral norm [24].
A Lipschitz-controlled ViT head is therefore introduced, which constrains the gain of every
trainable component through three complementary mechanisms. First, spectral normalization
[23] is applied to every learned linear map in attention, the FFN, and the logit readout, so that its
spectral norm is no more than 1. Second, cosine attention with a clamped temperature normalizes

remaining scale explicitly. Third, each residual branch uses a learnable per-channel scale initialized

With the controls in place, the ViT head trains successfully and substantially improves the quality
of generated images (Tab. 1, row 5). ViTGAN [26] provides an alternative ViT discriminator
design, but remains inferior to our head under the same protocol (Tab. 1, row 4). The weak prompt
adherence long associated with adversarial distillation, however, persists [1, 6]. Comparing text-
conditioning schemes, we attribute this shortfall to the pooled prompt vector: a single pooled
embedding cannot express fine-grained linguistic structure.
Token-level text conditioning via cross-attention. The Transformer head admits token-level
cross-attention naturally, and cross-attention provides a direct mechanism for image tokens at
different spatial positions to interact differently with individual text tokens. We insert cosine
cross-attention into the first block of every head, with subsequent blocks refining the conditioned
representation through self-attention and FFN layer. Each level keeps its own text key/value
projections, allowing different semantic depths to read the same prompt differently. Under the
unified protocol, Miyato-style pooled-text projection provides only a minor improvement over
the unconditional critic, whereas token-level cross-attention yields a much stronger gain in CLIP
score (Tab. 1, rows 6-8).
Multi-branch random mixing. Projected GAN’s random mixing is tailored to a convolutional
pyramid, where CCM is a 1×1 convolution within a level and CSM adds 3×3 convolutions between
levels of differing resolution. A ViT backbone has no such pyramid, since all taps share one uniform
patch-token grid, so both maps reduce to a plain 1×1 convolution that mixes channels alone.
We therefore replace each single-kernel mixing by an average of three size-preserving random
convolutions over the patch tokens, with kernels 1×1, 3×3, and 5×5; the summary token is
transformed separately by a random linear map. The wider branches let a token’s mixed value
draw on its spatial neighbors, so evidence is spread across positions as well as channels and depths.
Sec. 4.3 shows the advantage of this design. These frozen multi-branch maps constitute the
mixing M that Sec. 3.2 re-draws, and Sec. B.1 gives its matrix form and analyzes its anisotropy.
3.2 Periodic Mixing Refresh Prevents Critic Co-adaptation
With the Transformer head of Sec. 3.1, training converges quickly to good performance, then
begins to oscillate and slowly deteriorate (Fig. 3a). The symptom resembles discriminator
overfitting, yet standard augmentation remedies such as DiffAugment [27] and ADA [28] do
not materially alleviate this late-stage degradation under fixed mixing (Sec. 4.3). We therefore
examine other possible causes and finally focus on the random mixing, held fixed throughout
training in the classical design. We argue that its anisotropy persistently biases long-horizon
optimization toward the same feature directions. The generator and the critic then form a
realization-specific co-adaptation around this single view, which eventually erodes generation
quality.
A persistent critic state. We diagnose the critic with two measurements, Pre-projection Feature
Utilization Coverage (PFUC) and the logit margin (Sec. B.3). PFUC is the first-order sensitivity of
the critic’s mean logit to each backbone channel before mixing, expressed in backbone coordinates.

Periodic mixing refresh suppresses co-adaptation-induced degradation. Given a refresh
interval τ, at the start of the k-th period we re-draw the mixing M (k) from its initialization law.
The generator, the critic heads, and the optimizer states remain unchanged, and no learnable
parameter is reset or added. Each re-draw replaces the draw-specific gain landscape, so a
residual direction that is weakly measured under M (k) may receive a higher gain under M (k+1).

co-adaptation and can re-expose weak residual directions to adversarial correction. Consistent
with this mechanism, the refreshed run re-enters an improving trajectory after re-draws and
maintains a more stable and lower FID over long-horizon training (Fig. 4a).

4.1 Main Results
Does a single GAN loss reach state-of-the-art one-step quality? On SDXL, we compare with strong
baselines in Tab. 2. Typically, most of these methods consist of multiple training losses and require
trajectory (traj.) distillation, score distillation (ScoreD), GAN, and reward learning, along with
multiple stages such as ODE regression training followed by ScoreD+GAN. Compared to them,
ROAD is much simpler, with a pure GAN loss and a single training stage. Nevertheless, it achieves
11.03 FID, ahead of all the baselines, and is also competitive on CLIP score, aesthetic score, and
preference scores, suggesting that ROAD does not trade preference or alignment for fidelity. The
comparison on SD3.5-M, a flow-based MMDiT generator, is given in Tab. 3. To the best of our
knowledge, no one-step generators exist for it yet, so we compare with few-step models. Again,
one-step ROAD achieves strong results on FID, much better than the competing methods as well
as the teacher. Its CLIP score is among the best, indicating promising text-image alignment, while
its preference scores are slightly inferior to those of the compared methods. We note that all these
SOTA methods use 4 steps against our single step and are therefore 4 times slower than ours,
while most adopt three training losses against our single GAN loss.

4.2 Ablation Study
Across this section, the one-step SDXL generator is used for ablation, in which the refresh interval
τ is 5k and the regularization is JR1 with γJ = 7.5 × 10−3 unless otherwise specified.
Transformer head. Tab. 1 (Sec. 3.1) isolates the critic head design and the text conditioning
under fixed mixing and pooled R1. Increasing the capacity of the convolutional head does not
improve performance. Our Lipschitz-controlled ViT head instead yields a substantial gain in both
fidelity and alignment, and it also outperforms the ViTGAN alternative. For conditioning, token-
level cross-attention provides a clearly stronger alignment gain than Miyato-style pooled-text
projection.
Component decomposition. Tab. 4(a) presents a breakdown of ROAD’s three components.
Lipschitz control first makes the ViT head trainable, indicating its importance for stabilizing
GAN. Periodic refresh then improves FID from 14.54 to 12.08 and CLIP from 0.331 to 0.335,
demonstrating its benefit of refresh mixing over no refresh (i.e. fixed mixing). Finally, three
components together reduces FID to 11.03 while improving CLIP to 0.338. Further, trajectory-
level comparison (Fig. 6) shows JR1’s benefit persists across training rather than appearing only
at the selected checkpoint.
Frozen backbone. Tab. 4(b) contrasts feature extractors from several different foundation models.
Among DINOv3 ViTs of different capacities, ViT-B is the strongest, while both the smaller ViT-S
and the larger ViT-L underperform it. This suggests that a stronger representation does not
necessarily translate into better GAN performance. PE-Spatial ViT-B and SigLIP2 ViT-B are close
behind DINOv3 ViT-B, so ROAD is not tied to one feature extractor.
Regularizer. Tab. 4(c) compares JR1 with pooled R1, feature R1 that moves R1 penalty from
image space to the frozen feature taps [6, 47], and Approx. R1 (APT) that approximates the image-
space penalty without a double backward pass [10]. JR1 is the strongest of the four. It improves
on pooled R1 from 12.08 to 11.03 FID, whereas both the feature-space and the approximate
penalty fall behind pooled R1. Sec. 4.3 further shows that JR1 consistently outperforms pooled
R1 across a broad range of γJ.

Trajectory of Refresh interval. Fig. 5 analyzes the effect of periodic refresh at τ ∈{2k, 5k, 10k},
without gradient regularization to isolate the refresh effect. Fixed mixing oscillates strongly and
deteriorates late in training, whereas all refreshed variants remain markedly more stable and
achieve better FID. More frequent refresh yields smoother trajectories, consistent with periodic
re-drawing interrupting co-adaptation to a single random view. The best FIDs nevertheless remain
close across intervals, indicating low sensitivity to τ. We use τ = 5k by default.
Trajectory of Jacobian R1. Fig. 6 contrasts no gradient regularization, pooled R1, and JR1
with refresh interval τ =5k. Pooled R1 yields a smoother FID trajectory than the unregularized
run, and JR1 maintains a consistently lower FID with smaller fluctuations across training. This
suggests that gradient regularization is complementary to our refresh mechanism; besides, the
optimization benefits more from JR1 which regularizes the full logit sensitivities.

5 Conclusion and limitations
We modernize Projected GAN for one-step diffusion distillation with a Lipschitz-controlled Trans-
former head, a periodic refresh of the random mixing, and a Jacobian R1 penalty. The resulting
method, ROAD, converges fast and attains the best FID on both SDXL and SD3.5-M with a single ad-
versarial objective and one training stage, whereas competing methods combine several objectives
and stages. To our knowledge, ROAD is among the first to obtain a competitive one-step SD3.5-M
generator. Its preference scores, however, still trail those of few-step competitors (Tab. 3). Text
reaches the critic only through cross-attention, so an explicit text–image alignment term is a natu-
ral next step, though it would mean replacing DINOv3 with a contrastively trained encoder such as
SigLIP2 (Tab. 4(b)). Scaling the frozen encoder does not help by itself either, since within DINOv3
ViT-B beats ViT-S and ViT-L (Tab. 4(b)). We attribute this to the two-player game’s balance, which
our refresh mechanism improves but does not settle, and addressing it directly is future work.

B.4 Longitudinal probes on a fixed-mixing run
The fixed-run measurements in Sec. B.3 establish a persistent critic state, but they do not determine
whether its separation is specific to the training realization M (0). We therefore apply two post-hoc
diagnostics to frozen checkpoints of the same run.
Fresh-draw transfer. At every 5k-step checkpoint from 5k to 30k, we hold the checkpoint,
critic head, and matched real and generated batches fixed, and replace only M (0) by eight
independently drawn mixings. The margin under M (0) remains 5.1–5.3 through 25k (4.5 at 30k),
whereas the margins under fresh draws are approximately zero at every checkpoint. Because only
the mixing changes, this collapse shows that the fixed head’s real–generated separation does not
transfer to a fresh measurement basis. This result directly supports its coupling to M (0).
Linear probe. We also fit a separate linear classifier to the frozen mixed features at each level to
distinguish real from generated samples. This classifier is used only as a diagnostic and does not
update the generator, backbone, mixing, or critic. Beginning at approximately 2.5k steps, real
and generated samples are harder for a linear classifier to distinguish under M (0) than under
fresh draws, particularly for the shallow-level features.
Over the same fixed-mixing run, FID oscillates and later worsens, whereas the margin under the
fixed training mixing M (0) remains large and nearly stationary. The fresh-draw and linear-probe
diagnostics show that this separation is specific to the pairing Dϕ ◦M (0).
B.5 Scope of the account
The account makes two claims at different strengths. First, Prop. B.1 shows in a linear surrogate
why weak-direction residuals persist under a fixed draw and why independent draws remove a
permanently privileged direction in expectation. The chain-rule bounds of Sec. 3.2 tie the visibility
and correction of a direction to its gain. Second, the realization-specific co-adaptation of Sec. 3.2,
in which prolonged optimization under the fixed gain landscape hardens both players around it
until quality degrades, is a mechanistic reading of the ongoing nonlinear game. It is supported
jointly by PFUC, the margin, the fresh-draw probes of Sec. B.4, and the fixed-versus-refreshed
intervention, but none of these alone establishes it.

D Related Work
Few-step distillation of text-to-image diffusion models. Multi-objective or multi-stage train-
ing. The existing methods mostly use multiple training objectives and often consist of multiple
training stages. Hyper-SD employs a six-phase hybrid training pipeline: four stages of trajectory-
segmented consistency distillation, followed by human-feedback optimization and DMD-based
one-step score distillation [39]. For the one-step SDXL model, DMD2 uses a two-stage pipeline
27


ROAD: Modern Adversarial Training for One-Step Distillation
consisting of brief regression pretraining followed by joint distribution-matching and adversarial
training stabilized by a two-timescale update rule [1, Sec. F.4]; DMDX is also a two-stage pipeline,
consisting of adversarial distillation pretraining on teacher-generated ODE pairs with hybrid
latent- and pixel-space discriminators, along with adversarial distribution-matching fine-tuning
[38]. SiDA jointly trains a one-step generator with score-identity and adversarial losses, reusing
the fake score network’s encoder as a parameter-free discriminator [7]. ADD (SDXL-Turbo) uses
single-stage joint training with teacher-based score distillation and a DINOv2-feature adversarial
loss [6]. SDXL-Lightning employs a five-stage progressive distillation pipeline: one MSE-based
trajectory-distillation stage followed by four adversarial stages [43]. NitroFusion uses dynamic
adversarial refinement with a pool of conditional and unconditional global–local discriminators,
relying on a DMD2 one-step student and four-step teacher [9], so separate score-based pretrain-
ing is a prerequisite. SenseFlow combines score-based distribution matching, trajectory-based
intra-segment guidance, and timestep-aware VFM adversarial supervision [44]. SwD produces a
progressive few-step generator that increases latent resolution across denoising steps, combining
patch-level MMD with score-based distribution matching and adversarial loss [42]. Adversarial
objective as a single loss. Both APT [10] and LADD [8] perform latent-space adversarial training
and derive multilevel discriminator features from pretrained diffusion models. LADD freezes the
diffusion teacher, attaches discriminator heads to its intermediate features, and uses teacher-
generated samples as real targets. APT initializes a separate discriminator from the pretrained
diffusion model, adds multilevel output heads, and fine-tunes it against real data. D2O [18]
converts a pretrained diffusion U-Net into a one-step generator through pure adversarial fine-
tuning; it is evaluated only on low-resolution unconditional and class-conditional benchmarks and
does not address text-to-image synthesis. Unlike APT and LADD, both of which use diffusion-
based critics—trainable in APT and frozen in LADD—ROAD uses a frozen foundation model (e.g.,
DINOv3) with lightweight, prompt-conditioned Transformer heads. Compared with D2O, we
propose Transformer heads, refreshed mixing, and JR1, thereby scaling pure adversarial training
to large-scale text-to-image generation.
Projected GAN and adjacent critic architectures. Convolutional heads over frozen encoders.
Projected GAN trains shallow convolutional heads over a frozen pretrained encoder, whose
features are scattered by fixed random cross-channel and cross-scale mixings [11]; StyleGAN-XL
scales it to ImageNet [55], inheriting Projected GAN’s heads, and StyleGAN-T to text-to-image
with 1-D convolutional heads over frozen ViT tokens [12]. Every head in this line is convolutional,
the only reported head comparison being 1-D versus 2-D convolution [12], and positions are
scored independently, so the critic is vector-valued, a property ROAD keeps and regularizes. As
far as we know, no work in this line attaches a Transformer head to frozen features. Transformer
discriminators from scratch. ViTGAN scores a ViT on raw pixels from the class token, stabilized
by L2 attention with tied query and key projections, and applies spectral normalization only to
initialize the linear weights [26]. It reports that the vanilla ViT discriminator trains markedly
worse than the stabilized one. TransGAN’s convolution-free discriminator ingests patches of
several sizes [56]. Other work pairs Transformer generators with convolutional discriminators or
embeds attention inside a CNN [57–60]. Our Lipschitz controls build on analyses of attention
[24, 61], scaled cosine attention [25], and spectral normalization [23]; their combination into the
projected critic’s ViT head with an explicit bound (Lemma A.1) and its conditioning on individual
prompt tokens are new.
Random feature mixing and time-varying discriminators. Fixed random mixing. Projected
GAN samples the mixing once at initialization and holds it fixed for the whole run [11], and every
method in that line inherits the choice; the consequence of one draw persisting through long
28


ROAD: Modern Adversarial Training for One-Step Distillation
training, a critic that specializes to it, is, to our knowledge, unexamined. Previously, multiple-
random-projection GANs train simultaneous discriminators on a fixed ensemble of input projec-
tions [62], as do random-projection ensembles for classification [63]; in both, the set of views
is fixed in advance. Renewing trainable parameters. The closest precedents do alter the dis-
criminator over time. NitroFusion keeps a pool of trainable heads, samples a subset per iteration,
and re-initializes a small fraction of it, and SD3.5-Flash follows with stochastic head renewal
[9, 64]; SDXL-Lightning resets its discriminator between progressive-distillation stages rather
than within one [43]. Outside GANs, re-initializing trainable parameters is the standard remedy
for lost plasticity, through last-layer resets, recycling of dormant neurons, shrink-and-perturb,
and continual backpropagation [65–68]. Our ROAD instead re-draws the frozen measurement
basis, the random mixings, at deterministic intervals while every trainable parameter persists,
so it targets not lost plasticity in the head but the head’s specialization to a single persistent
realization.
Discriminator gradient regularization. Zero-centered penalties in GANs. Zero-centered
discriminator-gradient penalties stabilize GAN training and admit local convergence analyses
under suitable assumptions [15, 69], and R3GAN combines R1 and R2 [70]. D2O applies pooled
R1 to the averaged logit [18], ADD and P2D regularize in feature space [6, 47], and APT uses a
finite-difference approximation [10]. Each constrains a scalar critic output, or an aggregate of
a vector-valued one, which is the assumption our ROAD removes. Jacobian regularization in
classification. Novak et al. [29] study the empirical relationship between input–output Jacobian
sensitivity and generalization, whereas Jakubovitz and Giryes [71] explicitly regularize the logit
Jacobian to improve adversarial robustness, and Varga et al. [30], Hoffman et al. [32] develop
efficient randomized Jacobian regularization. We apply a squared Jacobian-norm penalty to real
images in a dense adversarial game, motivated specifically by the limitation of pooled R1, which
aggregates the vector-valued logit (Sec. 3.3). Through Rademacher Hutchinson estimation [31]
we recover the Jacobian energy in expectation with one input VJP per probe.

Transformer block. A block follows the pre-norm Transformer design. It contains a self-attention
branch followed by an FFN branch, and the first block of every head contains a cross-attention
branch between the two. The block departs from the standard design in four ways. First,
RMSNorm replaces LayerNorm. Second, queries and keys have width d/2, where d is the token
width. Third, the FFN uses GELU with an expansion ratio of 2 instead of 4. Fourth, self- and
cross-attention use cosine attention with a clamped temperature. All learned linear maps of the
head are spectrally normalized (Sec. 3.1), and token positions are encoded with rotary position
embeddings (Tab. 7). Sec. A analyzes the resulting head.
Cross-attention. The cross-attention branch has the same cosine form. Its queries are the patch
tokens of the level, and its keys and values are computed from the prompt. The summary token is
excluded from the queries, so conditioning acts on the patch tokens only. The token sequences of
all text encoders are concatenated along the channel axis into a single sequence and normalized
without affine parameters. Two spectrally normalized linear maps then project this sequence to
the widths of the visual queries and values. Each level has its own pair of text projections, so
different levels can read the same prompt differently.
Attention temperature. Each attention head h has a learnable temperature sh. It is stored
in the log domain, initialized at log sinit, and clamped at log smax before exponentiation, with
sinit = 10 and smax = 100. This parametrization guarantees 0 < sh ≤smax. Queries and keys are
ℓ2-normalized, the normalized queries are multiplied by sh, and the result is passed to scaled
dot-product attention. Normalization, scaling, and attention are computed in single precision and
cast back to the training precision. This keeps the clamp exact under mixed-precision training.

E.2 Dataset and evaluation protocols
Real-image training set. We use the fixed dataset of 500K real image–caption pairs constructed
by DMD2 [1] from LAION-Aesthetics 5.5+ [34] for both SDXL and SD3.5-M. The dataset discards
30


ROAD: Modern Adversarial Training for One-Step Distillation
unsafe images and images whose width or height is below 1024 pixels. Model-specific decoding,
resizing, and feature extraction are applied only after sampling from this common pool.
COCO evaluation sets. For both models, we follow the DMD2 SDXL protocol without resampling
its prompts or reference images. We use the fixed draw of 10K distinct images from the COCO-2014
validation set and one associated caption per image, as stored in the coco10k/all_prompts.pkl
file and paired coco10k/subset directory. We generate one 1024 × 1024 image per prompt. The
prompt order and sampling seeds are fixed and shared by all methods.
FID and CLIP score. We compute FID with the DMD2 implementation of clean-fid in its clean
mode. Before Inception feature extraction, the longer spatial dimension is center-cropped to
form a square and the image is resized to 512 × 512 with Lanczos interpolation. The resulting
FID compares 10K generated images with their corresponding 10K real images. We compute
CLIP score with the DMD2 evaluator for both models. It center-crops each generated image,
resizes it to 256 × 256, and applies the OpenCLIP ViT-g/14 model pretrained on LAION-2B
(laion2b_s12b_b42k). The text encoder receives the original caption prefixed by “A photo depicts”.
We report the mean cosine similarity over all generated image–caption pairs.
Auxiliary metrics. All auxiliary COCO metrics use the same generated images and the un-
modified captions used by the corresponding FID evaluation. We report the mean raw score
of LAION-Aesthetics Predictor V2, the official HPSv2 score [37], and the score produced by
yuvalkirstain/PickScore_v1 [36]. HPSv2 and PickScore are shown on the ×100 scale used in the
tables. ImageReward is the mean raw score from ImageReward-v1.0 [40]. GenEval [41], reported
only for SD3.5-M, is evaluated separately because it defines its own prompt suite. We use the
released 553 prompts, generate four images per prompt, run the official Mask2Former/OpenCLIP
evaluator with its default thresholds, and report the unweighted mean accuracy over its six
compositional tasks.
Checkpoint selection. We evaluate ROAD checkpoints every 500 training steps and select the
checkpoint with the lowest FID. Every other metric in a row is computed from that same checkpoint;
we never select a separate checkpoint for alignment, aesthetics, human preference, or GenEval.
Public baselines are evaluated with the same prompt files, reference images, preprocessing, metric
implementations, and reporting scales as the corresponding ROAD model.
