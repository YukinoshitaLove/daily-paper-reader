## Abstract 

While instruction-guided editors built on diffusion trans-formers can turn a personal photo into a convincing forgery with a single sentence, protective perturbations aim to make such edits fail before the photo is ever shared. The threat is most acute for the photos people actually share, which have 10 megapixels or more and which current editors first downscale by a factor that the owner does not know. Exist-ing protections are computed at one resolution or averaged over random scales, and they lose most of their effect once the photo is downscaled. They implicitly assume that the editor sees the scale for which they were optimized, or that protecting the average scale protects every scale. We pro-pose SRIM, a protection that remains effective at every scale an editor may use, without knowing its preprocessing and at the cost of standard expectation over transformation. SRIM covers the range of scales implied by the photo size with an-chor scales and spends each optimization step on the scale where the protection is currently weakest. A spectral analy-sis shows that this worst-case protection degrades only log-arithmically with the width of the range, a value that aver-aging over scales cannot reach. Extensive experiments on full-resolution photos and popular instruction-guided edi-tors validate that SRIM gives the strongest protection, out-performing existing methods even with quater of their ℓ∞arXiv:2610.07464v2 [cs.CV] 7 Oct 2026 budget, remaining the strongest under input purification. 

## 1. Introduction 

While instruction-guided editors built on diffusion and flow-matching transformers [12, 17], such as FLUX.1 Kon-text [8], Qwen-Image-Edit [29], and FLUX.2, have made photo editing accessible to everyone, they also let anyone turn a downloaded personal photo into a convincing forgery with a single sentence of text. A growing line of work there-fore asks the owner to immunize a photo before sharing it: a small adversarial perturbation is added so that later edits of the image fail [3, 6, 10, 11, 14, 22, 24, 27, 31, 32]. In this paper, we focus on immunization in the setting in which it is used. The owner shares the photo at its native resolution, typically 10 to 30 megapixels, while the editor resizes its input to between 0.25 and 4 megapixels before encoding it. The editor therefore sees the protected photo only after an antialiased downscaling by a factor s that the owner does not know. A protection that fails after this resize leaves the owner unprotected exactly in the situation that immunization is meant to cover. In general, existing protections can be roughly divided into two categories. Protections of the first category are computed at one resolution: either the editor’s working res-olution, typically 512 × 512 pixels for Stable Diffusion edi-tors and about one megapixel for diffusion transformers, or the native resolution of the photo. Protections of the second category average the perturbation over random transforma-tions with expectation over transformation (EOT) [1, 34]. Despite their effectiveness at the editor’s working resolu-tion, both categories lose most of their effect at the scales that real editors use: a single 0.5× resize already removes most of the protection of existing methods [25]. As shown in Figure 1, once the photo is downscaled, the strongest published protection with EOT no longer stops the edit. We argue that this limitation stems from a latent assumption of existing protections: that the editor sees the protected im-age at the scale for which the protection was optimized, or, with EOT, that protecting the average scale protects every scale. Viewed as a frequency-selective channel, the resize discards most of a native-resolution perturbation, keeps a fixed-resolution perturbation effective only in a narrow win-dow of scales, and leaves the ends of the range uncovered when the protection is averaged over scales (Sec. 4). An ideal protection should therefore satisfy three re-quirements. (i) It should remain effective at every scale in the range that an unknown editor may use, which fixed-resolution protections fail to meet because they cover only a window of scales. (ii) It should protect the weakest scale of this range rather than the average one, which EOT fails to meet because it trades the ends of the range for its middle. (iii) It should be computable on a 10 to 30 megapixel photo at the memory and time of existing protections, without knowing the editor’s preprocessing, which native-resolution application fails to meet because it exceeds the memory of a 48 GB GPU. Meeting (ii) without sacrificing (iii) is the central difficulty, since evaluating every scale at every step multiplies the cost by the number of scales. To this end, we propose SRIM (Scale-Robust IMmuniza-tion), whose key idea is to treat the unknown resize as a worst case over a range of scales, in the spirit of group distributionally robust optimization [21], rather than as one more random transformation. To meet (i), SRIM places an-chor scales on a grid that covers the whole range implied by the photo size, with random jitter and resampling ker-nels. To meet (ii), it draws one anchor per step from a dis-tribution that tracks the scale at which the protection is cur-rently weakest. To meet (iii), each step encodes a single downscaled copy, so SRIM costs the same as EOT and does not need the editor’s preprocessing. We also provide the-oretical analysis showing that, under a linearized spectral model, the best worst-case protection degrades only loga-rithmically with the width of the scale range, whereas aver-aging over scales does not attain it (Sec. 5.1). In summary, the main contributions of this paper are as follows: • We revisit image immunization under the resize that edi-tors apply to shared photos, and reveal the root cause of its failure: the protection is optimized for a scale, or an average of scales, that the editor does not use. • Based on our analysis, we propose SRIM, which covers the whole range of scales with anchors and spends each step on the currently weakest one, at the cost of EOT. • We theoretically show that the best worst-case protection under an unknown resize degrades only logarithmically with the scale range, and that averaging over scales does not attain it. • Experiments on full-resolution photos and four editors, including FLUX.2-dev, show that SRIM raises the worst-case disruption of edits to 2.3 to 2.4 times that of the strongest published protection on the FLUX.2 editors. 

## 2. Related Work 

Image immunization against malicious editing Photo-Guard [22] introduced protective perturbations against la-tent diffusion editing, either by pushing the encoder la-tent toward a target or by attacking the full diffusion pro-cess. AdvDM [11] and Mist [10] maximize the denoising loss and add a textural latent loss, and score distillation makes these attacks cheaper [31]. Guo et al . [6] showed that attacking only the posterior of the variational autoen-coder (VAE) already disrupts latent diffusion [20] editing and transfers across models that share the autoencoder. Dif-fusionGuard [3] targets inpainting with mask augmentation, FaceLock [27] optimizes against face recognition features, and DeContext [24] weakens the attention pathways that carry context in diffusion transformer editors. Related pro-tections target personalization [5, 7, 23, 26, 33] and cross-model transfer [35]; recent surveys cover the area [36]. All of these methods compute the perturbation at the resolution at which the editor will see the image. Our analysis con-cerns the step they leave out: the resize between the shared photo and the editor’s input. 

Robustness of protections Protections are known to be fragile under compression, purification, and resizing [16, 25, 38]. The standard remedy is expectation over trans-formation (EOT) [1], and EOLT learns the transformation distribution instead of sampling it uniformly [34]. An-tiPure adds frequency guidance to resist diffusion purifica-tion [33]. We show that for an unknown resize the relevant quantity is the worst case over scales rather than the aver-age, derive its optimal value, and give a method that targets it at the cost of EOT. 

Resampling and adversarial perturbations Image-scaling attacks exploit downsampling in the opposite direc-tion: they hide a target image that appears only after the victim resizes the input [18, 30]. Low-frequency adversar-ial perturbations are known to transfer better and to survive defenses that remove high frequencies [4]. Our transfer-function analysis explains why low-frequency content mat-ters for protection against editors in particular: the editor discards the top of its own frequency band, so energy placed there is wasted even before any resize.

4.1. The Limitations of Native-Resolution Protec-tion 

Computing the protection at the native resolution relies on the latent assumption that a perturbation which moves the latent of the full-resolution photo also moves the latent the editor computes. However, sign-gradient steps [9, 13, 15] make such a perturbation close to white noise, and the resize discards most of it. 

Theorem 1 (Decay) . If ˆδ is flat on the native band, then 

ρs(δ) = ¯ g2/(g⋆2s2) in two dimensions, where ¯g2 is the mean of g2 over the working band. 

Two effects compound. The resize keeps only a fraction 

1/s 2 of the energy. What remains is spread over the whole working band, although the editor responds mostly to part of it, so ρ1 = ¯ g2/g ⋆2 < 1 already without resizing. In line with Theorem 1, on development photos of 2 megapixels, a native-resolution perturbation that moves the latent strongly changes the edit by only 0.157 LPIPS at s = 1 , and by 0.077 and 0.044 at s = 2 and 3.

Protecting at a fixed working resolution is therefore op-timal at one scale and decays on both sides of it. For a low-pass g, Theorem 3 predicts that a protection computed at one megapixel is strongest at the smallest downscaling, where its content sits at low working-grid frequencies, and decays as s grows. The published protections applied at one megapixel behave in this way: for PCA@1MP, the edit LPIPS on FLUX.2-klein is 0.189, 0.163, 0.129, 0.094, and 0.077 at s = 2 , 3, 4, 6, 8 (Table S1 of the supplementary material). 

4.3. The Limitations of Averaging over Scales 

The standard remedy, EOT [1], draws the scale at random and maximizes the average protection. It relies on the latent assumption that protecting the average scale protects every scale. 

Theorem 4 (EOT is not minimax) . The maximizer of the average of ρs over log-uniform s concentrates its en-ergy at a single native frequency ω†. Its worst case is 

min s g(sω †)2/g ⋆2, which tends to 0 when ln S exceeds the log-width of the support of g, where S = smax /s min .

EOT therefore sacrifices the scales at the ends of the range. With our objective, EOT is flat in the middle of the range on FLUX.2-klein (0.473 to 0.518 for s ≤ 6) but drops to 0.382 at s = 8 , the largest downscaling. The above analyses reveal that the root cause is the same in all three cases: each protection is optimized for a scale, or for an average of scales, that does not represent the scale the editor uses. This motivates us to optimize the protection for the scale at which it is currently weakest, rather than for one expected scale or for the average.

Relation to prior work Similar techniques have been ex-plored in EOT [1] and EOLT [34], which sample or learn a distribution over transformations; the key difference is that both maximize the average over that distribution, whereas SRIM targets the weakest scale. SRIM borrows the expo-nentiated group weights of group distributionally robust op-timization [21]; here the groups are anchor scales derived from the photo size, and each weight is updated from the single copy drawn at that step.

Cost On one L40S GPU, protecting a test photo (17 megapixels on average) takes 176 s with SRIM, 134 to 155 s for the EOT variants, and 59 s for the methods applied at one megapixel. At the native resolution, a baseline ran out of memory for most photos, and the one run that fit took 57 minutes. 

## 7. Conclusion 

While protective perturbations are computed for the image an editor sees, the photos that people share are first down-scaled by a factor that the owner does not know. We showed that this resize decides whether a protection works: native-resolution protection decays with the downscaling factor, fixed-resolution protection covers only a window of scales, and averaging over scales leaves the ends of the range ex-posed. SRIM instead protects the weakest scale at the cost of EOT and gives the strongest worst-case protection on four editors. We hope that protections will be evaluated un-der the preprocessing that editors apply. References
