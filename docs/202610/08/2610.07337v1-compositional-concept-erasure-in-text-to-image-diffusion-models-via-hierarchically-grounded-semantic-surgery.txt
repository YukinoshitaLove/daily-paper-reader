Abstract 

Removing copyrighted, unsafe, or user-specified concepts from a deployed text-to-image diffusion model is now a practical requirement. Weight-editing methods can sup-press fixed targets, but they require per-target retraining and modify the model check-point. Training-free methods, on the other hand, are deployment-friendly, but they suffer from text-side routing failures on compositional prompts. In such prompts, the erase target may be invoked through a related class rather than its lexical name, and its modi-fiers may migrate onto preserved objects. This paper proposes Hierarchically Grounded Semantic Surgery (HGSS), a training-free framework for compositional concept erasure. The framework lifts both the routing signal and the edit operator used by text-side era-sure. First, hierarchical span grounding resolves erase-target spans through lexical, taxo-nomic, and semantic evidence, while guarding against broad-hypernym and compound-head false positives. Second, dynamic attribute binding refines the text conditioning dur-ing early denoising via a counterfactual reference and a preserve-aware cross-attention objective, keeping surviving attribute–noun bindings intact. # 1 Introduction 

Removing copyrighted, unsafe, or user-specified concepts from a deployed text-to-image dif-fusion model [27] is now a practical requirement. Real prompts, however, are compositional. A target may be invoked through hierarchical or implicit reference, and modifiers often re-main bound to objects that the erasure procedure is trying to remove. The requirement is especially sharp for training-free erasure, where the deployed U-Net is left untouched and the intervention happens only through the text-conditioning stream. Existing methods occupy two deployment regimes. Weight-editing methods—ESD [8], UCE [9], MACE [20], and related approaches (Sec. 2)—can be strong on fixed targets. How-ever, they modify the model or attach learned modules, and each edit incurs a training, stor-age, and distribution-shift cost. Training-free methods—SLD [29], SAFREE [37], and Se-mantic Surgery (SS) [35]—are lighter: the same checkpoint can serve many erase targets on demand. Their bottleneck is that the edit must be computed from the prompt itself. Any routing error in the text side therefore becomes an erasure failure. SS [35], a strong training-free representative, detects erase-related prompt terms via a similarity-based concept-presence kernel and subtracts a weighted direction from the condi-tioning hidden states. This handles the literal safety–quality slice of I2P [29]. Two failure modes, however, become apparent on the compositional prompts of the recent SEE bench-mark [28], and neither is reliably addressed by tuning edit strength: 1. Hierarchical evasion. When the prompt does not lexicalize the erase target, similarity-based selection misses descendants of the target. For example, the prompt “a poodle DAI ET AL.: COMPOSITIONAL CONCEPT ERASURE VIA HGSS 3

running in the park” under target “mammal” still produces a mammal, because “poo-dle” is not lexically matched. The parent-to-child case is ambiguous in the opposite direction: “flower” may instantiate the fine-grained target “rose”, and routing the edit through the parent risks suppressing non-target flowers. On the SEE evasion axis, SS remained vulnerable, with a 29 .54 average despite strong neighboring erasure. 2. Attribute leakage. Cross-attention text-side subtraction projects an aggregate direc-tion and cannot determine whether a modifier such as “blue” belongs to the target noun or to a preserved one. As a result, attributes of the target can migrate onto preserved objects: “a blue car next to a dog” becomes a blue dog . On the SEE attribute-leakage axis, SS’s pairwise CLIP Attr E exceeded twice that of weight-edit baselines (Attr P

was roughly 1 .4× theirs). These two failures, visualised in Figure 1, share a structural cause. SS treats the prompt as a set of candidate terms and routes a single concept direction through lexical–CLIP simi-larity. This primitive represents neither the hierarchy that relates a target to its descendants nor the binding that ties an attribute to its noun. A larger SS subtraction can sharpen the edit on lexicalized targets. It cannot, however, recover an unselected descendant token or decide which noun a modifier should remain bound to. The remedy must therefore replace the structural primitives rather than re-tune the existing scalars. 

Contributions. This paper proposes Hierarchically Grounded Semantic Surgery (HGSS), a training-free framework for compositional concept erasure. Its contributions are: 1. A structural framing for compositional erasure. Existing training-free erasure methods treat prompt terms as loosely related routing cues. HGSS instead views con-cept erasure as the inverse objective to compositional generation. The framework keeps the SS coarse edit interface, but replaces its lexical routing and static condition-ing primitives. The two replacements remain training-free: neither introduces learned parameters nor per-concept hand-coded rules. 2. Hierarchical span grounding. To decide what to edit, HGSS introduces a resolver for erase-target spans (Sec. 3.3). The resolver combines lexical matches, WordNet-backed taxonomic evidence, and semantic fallback. Broad-hypernym and compound-head guards reduce false positives on preserved objects. 3. Dynamic attribute binding. To decide how the edit evolves, HGSS introduces a per-step conditioning update for early denoising (Sec. 3.4). A counterfactual reference defines the target-removed state. An asymmetric attention objective protects preserve spans and suppresses leakage from erase-target spans. 4. Evaluation across complementary axes. The experiments evaluate the two structural mechanisms on compositional side effects, style/object unlearning, and direct lexical safety compatibility. Across the three axes, the resolver and dynamic binding reduce compositional side effects while broadly preserving image quality and the safety ca-pability of the inherited coarse edit. Ablations separate the resolver from dynamic binding (Sec. 4). Taken together, the two structural mechanisms view erasure as an inverse objective to com-positional generation . Rather than binding every prompt entity correctly, the framework first routes the erase target and its descendants into the edit set, then stabilises the surviving 4 DAI ET AL.: COMPOSITIONAL CONCEPT ERASURE VIA HGSS 

preserve-side bindings during denoising. The grounding cascade and the dynamic-binding update are implemented as a plug-in module for training-free, text-side concept erasure. 

# 2 Related Work 

Parameter-modifying concept erasure. A large body of work edits the diffusion model itself to remove a target concept. Fine-tuning objectives steer the generator away from the target distribution. ESD [8] fine-tunes U-Net attention with a negative-guidance loss, Concept Ablation [16] matches a surrogate concept, and AdvUnlearn [40] optimises ad-versarial robustness during erasure. Closed-form and low-rank weight edits act directly on cross-attention projections without full fine-tuning. UCE [9] and RECE [10] apply closed-form edits, MACE [20] targets many concepts jointly, and SPM [21] uses one-dimensional adapters. Sparsity- and saliency-guided edits localise the intervention to a subset of param-eters. SalUn [6] uses gradient-based weight saliency, SAeUron [5] edits sparse-autoencoder features, and ConceptPrune [2] prunes skilled neurons. Locality-oriented and representation-space methods reduce collateral damage by constraining where the edit applies. CPE [18] introduces residual attention gates, GLoCE [17] gates a training-free low-rank adaptation, and AdaVD [32] edits the value space along orthogonal complements. A further line re-fines target geometry : AGE [1] identifies which concepts to push erased samples toward, while CoGFD [24] explicitly handles combinations of erase targets rather than single nouns. These methods exploit additional parameter capacity to retain preserve-side fidelity, but re-quire per-target training and modified checkpoints. 

Training-free and inference-time erasure. Training-free erasure preserves the base gen-erator and intervenes only at sampling time. SLD [29] modifies classifier-free guidance using a safety prompt; SAFREE [37] projects text embeddings away from unsafe direc-tions before they enter the U-Net. TraSCE [15] steers the denoising trajectory away from harmful regions via a localised loss-based guidance. Semantic Surgery (SS) [35] subtracts a similarity-weighted concept direction from the conditioning hidden states. These methods are deployment-friendly since the same checkpoint serves many erase targets on demand, but their edits are only as reliable as the text-side signals that select the edited span. When the target is reached through a related class, a semantic variant, or an entangled attribute phrase, a single conditioning-time subtraction can fail in two ways. It can miss the routing path entirely, or remove content beyond the intended span. 

Side effects and robustness. Early erasure evaluations emphasise literal unsafe-content removal and clean-set image quality, exemplified by I2P [29] with NudeNet-based scoring. UnlearnCanvas [39] expands this view to style and object unlearning across 60 styles and 20 object classes. SEE [28] factors side effects into neighboring impact , hierarchical eva-sion , and attribute leakage , each with its own protocol. A complementary line stress-tests erased models with adversarial prompts: Ring-A-Bell [31], UnlearnDiffAtk [41], MMA-Diffusion [36], and P4D [4]. 

Text-side compositional control. A separate line edits text conditioning states during sampling to repair compositional generation failures. Prompt-to-Prompt [11] swaps cross-attention maps between related prompts. Attend-and-Excite [3] strengthens the attention of DAI ET AL.: COMPOSITIONAL CONCEPT ERASURE VIA HGSS 5

subject tokens that risk being neglected. Structure Diffusion [7] incorporates linguistic struc-ture into the guidance computation, and DATE [23] updates the text embedding per denois-ing step to correct attribute–object misbinding. SynGen [26] introduces a positive–negative cross-attention loss that aligns each attribute’s attention with the noun it modifies. HGSS sits at the intersection of these lines by keeping the deployment advantage of training-free erasure but replacing the similarity-only routing used by prior inference-time methods with a hierarchical span grounding cascade. From text-side compositional control, HGSS reuses attribute–object binding primitives on the preserve side. HGSS inverts that framework’s objective by first routing target spans and descendants into the edit set, then re-applying binding control only to surviving spans, thereby treating concept erasure as the 

inverse objective to compositional generation , motivating the counterfactual reference and preserve-restricted attention pair in Sec. 3.4.

## 3.3 Hierarchical Span Grounding 

This module addresses hierarchical evasion : given prompt p and target Te, it returns the prompt spans that should be treated as erase-positive evidence, including spans that are hier-archical descendants of Te when Te itself does not appear lexically in p. SS solves this with CLIP similarity alone, which is unreliable when Te is not lexicalized. HGSS replaces this similarity-only test with a deterministic cascade that emits weighted (span, relation, confi-dence) tuples and removes false positives via guard rules so that the remaining tuples become the weighted erase set AE and, by complement, the preserve set AP.

Cascade. The cascade consults three categories of evidence in order of signal reliability: lexical, taxonomic, and semantic. Lexical evidence provides high precision. Taxonomic evidence expands recall when lexical matches fail. And, semantic fallback handles open-vocabulary concepts beyond the lexicon. These three categories are visualised as the three DAI ET AL.: COMPOSITIONAL CONCEPT ERASURE VIA HGSS 7

labelled icons in Box 2 of Figure 2 and are realised through four sources, consulted in priority order. The first source that emits a confident candidate determines the relation label. (1) Lex-ical evidence tests exact equality, substring containment, and tokenized head-noun match between prompt term tk and Te. Exact matches are labelled SELF . (2a) Taxonomic backoff 

uses WordNet [22] to supply non-lexical evidence when lexical matching fails. The resolver looks up synsets for tk and Te and runs a breadth-first traversal of the hypernym/hyponym directed acyclic graph (DAG) with max depth 12. The closest valid relation is classified as 

> DESCENDANT

/CHILD (force-include), PARENT /PARENT -LIKE , or SIBLING via shared hyper-nyms. A broad-hypernym guard suppresses generic ancestors ( entity , object , whole ,

artifact , organism , . . . ) so that “car” and “apple” do not become hierarchically related through the root of the WordNet DAG. (2b) Activity bridge (a taxonomic refinement) han-dles verb-style targets and gerund-derived identifiers by expanding each candidate through WordNet derivational links into verb, gerund, and nominal variants, then applying the same relation and CLIP checks. The expansion is generated per prompt rather than taken from a hand-curated activity lexicon. (3) Semantic fallback admits a candidate when its CLIP cosine similarity to Te exceeds a stage-specific threshold (distinct values for primary erase, derived modifiers, and activity targets; full settings are in App. B.1). This stage handles out-of-WordNet concepts (style names, brand names) without hand-curated lists. 

Guards. Two guard rules reduce the most common false-positive patterns in our develop-ment set: • Compound-head guard. For a compound noun tk = w1 w2 . . . wn, the derived head 

wn is only erased if the full compound tk has an erase-positive relation to Te. A relation between only wn and Te in isolation will not necessarily trigger erasure. This prevents “baseball bat” from triggering erasure under a generic “mammal” target: the derived head “bat” is a mammal in WordNet, but the full compound “baseball bat” does not bear a descendant relation to mammal .• Self-suppression. If the prompt lexicalizes a descendant of Te (the resolver returns a DESCENDANT /CHILD candidate), the SELF candidate corresponding to Te itself is suppressed to avoid double-counting when Te is also a prompt token. 

The edit plan. The resolver returns a weighted erase set AE = {(tk, ωk)}k and an analo-gously weighted preserve set AP. The weight ωk reflects the cascade’s confidence in can-didate tk: force-included relations ( SELF , DESCENDANT , CHILD ) contribute at a default full weight; threshold-admitted candidates contribute at their cascade-stage confidence score. All other candidates must clear a similarity-confidence threshold and a separate preserve-strength floor that protects against weakly related nouns. When the user provides an explicit preserve target Tp (as in SEE and UnlearnCanvas protocols), the resolver anchors Tp’s span in AP; otherwise AP is the complement of AE within the prompt. All threshold values were fixed once during development and not tuned on SEE or UnlearnCanvas; exact values are listed in App. B.1. The role separation between span grounding and dynamic binding is evaluated through module-level diagnostic ablations in Sec. 4.5.

## 3.4 Dynamic Attribute Binding 

Dynamic Attribute Binding is not intended to be a standalone erasure mechanism. It refines the live conditioning during early DDIM steps so that, after the resolver-grounded coarse edit, the modifier–noun bindings on spans that should survive the erasure remain intact. A small gradient correction is applied to the conditioning embeddings during early DDIM steps, when object layout and attribute binding are still being established. The update com-bines two parts: a per-token counterfactual reference and a per-step composite loss. The reference anchors what each token position should encode after Te is removed. The compos-ite loss pulls each preserved (modifier, head-noun) pair together while pushing preserve-side keys away from the residue left by the coarse edit. 

Design rationale. The four loss components in Eq. (3) (introduced below) are not inter-changeable. Each constrains a distinct level of the conditioning pipeline. The update con-centrates in early denoising, where object layout and cross-attention bindings are most mal-leable. Detailed motivation for each component (the symmetric-KL choices for the attention losses, the residual CLIP-space target penalty, and the ℓ2 prior anchor) is in App. A.2.

# 4 Experiments 

## 4.1 Experimental Setup 

Base model and inference. HGSS and the training-free baselines are evaluated as drop-in text-side modifiers of Stable Diffusion v1.4 [27] with the DDIM sampler at 50 inference steps, classifier-free guidance scale 7 .5, image resolution 512 ×512. No fine-tuning or dis-tillation is performed. For controlled comparison, all SS baselines are evaluated with visual feedback disabled (no-VF). HGSS does not use visual feedback, so the SS →HGSS com-parison isolates the two proposed modules under the same coarse text-side edit interface. All reported SS and HGSS rows use the same evaluation protocol and the same benchmark-specific coarse-edit operating point. For compositional erasure (SEE, UnlearnCanvas) the SS coarse-edit operating point is β =−0.12; for direct safety erasure (I2P) it is β =−0.06, matching the original SS safety operating point. In every setting HGSS introduces no new hyperparameter at this interface and runs at exactly the same β as the matched SS baseline .Per-step refinements use the window [0, 0.6T ] and interval ∆t=10. The step size is η=0.5. The loss weights are (λbind , λneg , λtgt , λprior )=( 0.4, 0.1, 1.0, 0.02 ) of Sec. 3.4.2; identical set-tings are used on every benchmark, and the update target is the live conditioning embedding ¯c. Under matched inference conditions, the measured per-image wall-clock cost is approxi-mately 1 .3× SS’s on a single L40S GPU at 512 ×512 resolution. This is consistent with the 

≈ 3 extra backward passes derived in Sec. 3.5. 

Benchmarks. Three complementary benchmarks were used. (i) SEE [28] served as the primary diagnostic benchmark. Its three side-effect axes isolate the compositional failures targeted by HGSS: neighboring impact (SEE Table 2), evasion (SEE Table 3), and attribute leakage (SEE Table 4). The benchmark spans 11 super-categories with 79 base objects and 5,056 compositional variants. The official CLIP-based scoring protocol was followed and metrics were reported in the paper direction ( ↓ for erase/evasion/leakage; ↑ for preserve). Neighbor E measures residual target evidence on erase-side prompts, while Neighbor P

measures preservation of related non-target concepts. Evasion measures target reappear-ance through hierarchical or related-class prompts. Attr E and Attr P measure pairwise at-tribute leakage on erase and preserve sides. (ii) UnlearnCanvas [39] measures style and object unlearning quality. The evaluation covers the 50 style targets and 20 object targets de-fined by the official benchmark protocol (selected from the 60-style UnlearnCanvas dataset). The reported metrics are the standard unlearning accuracy (UA), in-domain retention accu-racy (IRA), and cross-domain retention accuracy (CRA) triple, with the six-metric average across style and object axes. FID was computed using a regenerated reference set with five images per combination. The SS and HGSS rows are therefore strictly comparable. UA measures target removal. IRA and CRA measure retention on in-domain and cross-domain non-target generations. (iii) I2P [29] measures direct safety erasure. Per-class NudeNet detection counts are taken under the standard paper8 + threshold=0.6 protocol on all 4 ,703 I2P prompts across eight body-part classes. MS-COCO-30K FID and CLIP are reported as image-quality sanity checks.

# 5 Conclusion 

This paper introduced HGSS, a training-free correction to text-side concept erasure for text-to-image diffusion models. Compositional erasure needs both target routing and preserve-side binding control. Hierarchical span grounding (what to edit) and dynamic attribute bind-ing (how the edit evolves) together cast erasure as the inverse objective to compositional generation.
