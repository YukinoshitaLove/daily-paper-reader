Abstract
Open-ended image-editing requests, such as “decorate this room,” admit multiple
valid realizations. Direct sampling may produce ungrounded variation, whereas
explicit diversity prompting often produces superficial differences. We study how to
ground these requests without prematurely eliminating their legitimate ambiguity.
We introduce a two-stage framework that first identifies diverse, feasible edit
scopes and then realizes them as executable edit instructions. Across 300 open-
ended image-editing tasks, our approach achieves 24.6% higher mean semantic
dispersion and 17.1% higher mean groundedness than direct diversity prompting,
while producing more valid and distinct realizations. These results show that
structuring feasible edit directions before realization supports more useful grounded
exploration.

Introduction
Image generation and editing systems are often evaluated on requests with relatively specific targets
Xiang et al. [2025], Zhang et al. [2023], Sheynin et al. [2024], such as removing an object or changing
its color. Prior image-editing systems therefore typically optimize for a single best edit for a given
instruction Zhao et al. [2026], Xu et al. [2026]. Yet many real-world creative requests Collins et al.
[2026] are fundamentally more open-ended: users may instead ask for “ideas to make my bedroom
feel cozier” or “ways to improve this space.” Such requests do not specify a single desired outcome,
but admit many plausible realizations. Supporting them therefore requires more than producing a
valid edit—it requires exploring the space of possible solutions that are consistent with the user’s
intent. In practice, however, because each generation conditions on the same request and source
image, repeated sampling often remains concentrated around the same high-level interpretation.
For example, a model may respond to “make this room cozier” with several variations that all add
plants, while overlooking qualitatively different directions such as introducing rugs, wall art, warmer
lighting, shelving, or combinations of these changes. Conversely, explicitly prompting for diversity
can increase variation without ensuring that the alternatives correspond to meaningful or plausible
editing directions.
This exploration problem is especially challenging for image editing, where the solution space is
constrained by an existing source image. Unlike unconstrained image generation, a successful edit
must not only reflect the user’s underspecified intent, but also remain grounded in the visible objects,
spatial layout, scene structure, and implicit preservation constraints of the input. The model must
therefore infer where the request leaves room for change and where the source image constrains
it, while still exploring substantially different ways of satisfying the request. These objectives can
be in tension: staying too close to the most obvious interpretation leads to redundant alternatives,

To move beyond prior work of producing a single best edited image, we separate exploration from
visual execution. We designed a two-stage workflow: it first constructs an input-adaptive scaffold
that captures relevant visual constraints while preserving genuinely open decisions, and then uses
this scaffold to generate multiple semantically distinct, executable edit realizations. Across 300
open-ended image-editing tasks, our approach achieves 24.6% higher mean semantic dispersion and
17.1% higher mean groundedness than direct diversity prompting, while also producing more valid
and distinct realizations. These results suggest that effective exploration benefits from explicitly
structuring high-level edit directions before realization, rather than simply prompting for diversity.

Grounded Exploration for Open-Ended Image Editing
Given a source image and an underspecified request, our goal is to generate a set of K meaningfully
different candidate edits while remaining grounded in the user’s intent and the visible scene. Rather
than relying on repeated sampling from an image editing model to discover alternatives implicitly,
our method separates spatial exploration from concrete realization.
Stage 1: Exploring spatial edit scopes.
The first stage generates K distinct spatial scopes as
natural-language descriptions that are compatible with the image and request. Each scope specifies
the primary editable region, the permitted extent of change, and a preservation boundary for the rest
of the image. For example, a request to give a room a more designer look may be applied to the sofa
area, the fireplace, the workspace, or the entire seating area. At this stage, the model does not choose
a particular style, material, or object replacement. It determines only where an edit may occur and
how much of the visible scene it may affect.
Stage 2: Realizing concrete edits.
The second stage generates one natural-language edit plan
for each spatial scope. Each plan is a self-contained description of the specific objects, materials,
and visual treatments to modify while respecting the corresponding editable region and preservation
boundary. For example, a fireplace scope may be realized by refinishing the surround, while a
seating-area scope may coordinate changes to the sofa, rug, and coffee table. The resulting K plans
are passed directly to the same image editing model and executed independently.
3
Experiments
We evaluate whether explicitly structuring exploration produces candidate sets that are both more
diverse and more grounded than alternatives based on repeated sampling or generic diversity prompt-
ing. Our evaluation uses 300 open-ended image-editing tasks sampled from an internal evaluation set
2


collected during employee testing of an online design platform. Each user request generated eight
candidates.
3.1
Evaluation Metrics
Our primary evaluation is set-level, since the goal is to produce a useful collection of grounded
alternatives rather than identify a single best edit. We use GPT-5.5 as a VLM judge, providing the
source image, original user request, and eight generated candidates for each method. We report two
1–5 set-level ratings: Instruction Following, which measures how well the generated set satisfies
the requested editing intent and scope, and Groundedness, which measures how well the outputs
preserve the identity, structure, spatial relationships, and unrelated content of the source image. We
additionally report Valid@8, the number of candidates that both satisfy the request and remain
sufficiently grounded in the source.
After fixing candidate validity in the first judge call, we use two additional calls to characterize
diversity at different semantic levels. Direction Coverage@8 counts the valid high-level editing
directions represented in the set, where directions differ in target, function, layout, intervention
strategy, or interpretation. Differences only in color, material, texture, accessory choice, or minor
placement remain within the same direction. A separate call clusters valid candidates into concrete
realizations. Distinct Realizations@8 counts these clusters, allowing multiple realizations to belong
to the same broader direction. Finally, Dispersion averages pairwise semantic separation over all 28
candidate pairs: invalid or redundant pairs receive no credit, related realizations receive partial credit,
and different high-level directions receive full credit.
3.2
Experimental Setup
Our experiments study where and how to introduce exploration so that the resulting candidate set is
diverse while remaining grounded in the source image and user request. We ask two questions: (1)
whether grounded diversity is better obtained through prompt-level exploration or through stochastic
image-model sampling, and (2) how prompt-level exploration should be structured.
Image-model sampling.
We provide the same source image and original user request to an
image editing model and generate eight outputs by varying only the random seed.
We eval-
uate three image backends: Qwen/Qwen-Image-Edit-2509, gemini-2.5-flash-image, and
black-forest-labs/FLUX.2-klein-4B. This setting tests whether stochastic image generation
alone can produce meaningfully different edits without generating alternative edit instructions.
Instruction-space exploration. All prompt-level methods use Qwen/Qwen3.6-35B-A3B to generate
eight alternative edit instructions, which are then rendered using a fixed downstream backend,
Qwen/Qwen-Image-Edit-2509. We consider two unstructured baselines. Naive independently
samples eight instructions, whereas Prompted Diversity requests eight diverse instructions in a
single call. As an ablation, Realization only uses our Stage-2 prompt to jointly generate eight
concrete realizations without the preceding exploration scaffold. We additionally evaluate High-level
exploration, an alternative two-stage strategy that first identifies a small set of image-grounded
readings of the request, while using the same Stage-2 procedure.

4
Discussion
Our results suggest that open-ended generation should be viewed not only as producing diverse
samples, but as covering multiple valid modes of a constrained solution space. This is particularly
important for image editing, where outputs must remain grounded in a specific source image. Simply
increasing variation is therefore insufficient: repeated sampling may remain concentrated around
similar high-level realizations, while generic diversity prompting can introduce less grounded alter-
natives. The goal is instead to broaden semantic coverage while remaining within the set of edits
supported by the image and user request.
More broadly, this highlights a complementary perspective to the common goal of sharpening a
model distribution toward a preferred output. For inherently open-ended tasks, multiple distinct
modes may all be desirable, making the problem one of preserving and exposing valid multimodality
rather than collapsing toward a single solution. Our current approach addresses this at inference time
by structuring what can vary before generation; a longer-term question is how models can learn and
represent such distributions over valid solutions directly.
5
Conclusion
We study open-ended image editing as a grounded exploration problem, where a useful system should
expose multiple meaningfully different solutions without drifting from the source image or user
request. Our results show that generic diversity prompting is not sufficient, while structuring the space
of permissible variation can improve the balance between grounding and exploration. More broadly,
this suggests that open-ended multimodal tasks may benefit from generation strategies that preserve
and expose multiple valid modes rather than optimizing toward a single preferred realization.

B
Condensed Generation Prompts
All generation prompts receive the source image and original user request. We set all candidate
counts to eight. Repeated input and output boilerplate is omitted for brevity.
B.1
Realization Generation
Given the image, request, and optional Stage-1 scaffold, generate K concrete and
executable edit instructions.
Each instruction must:
- plausibly satisfy the request;
- specify which visible content changes and how;
- use an appropriate edit extent;
- preserve source geometry and spatial relationships;
- preserve unrelated content and explicit constraints.
The set should differ in dominant editing direction or composition, not merely in
wording or superficial attributes.
Return only a JSON array of K instruction strings.
B.2
Region-Level Exploration
Construct K pairwise-different spatial edit scopes for the request.
Vary the amount and distribution of editable content. For each scope, identify the
editable regions and a clear preservation boundary.
These are WHERE and EXTENT choices, not choices of style, material, object,
palette, or treatment. Do not broaden a narrowly targeted request to unrelated
regions.
C
Condensed Evaluation Prompts
We use three structured GPT-5.5 judge calls. The prompts below omit repeated image-indexing
instructions and diagnostic details while retaining the core evaluation criteria.
C.1
Validity and Set-Level Ratings
Evaluate each candidate against the source image and original user request. Do not
evaluate diversity.
A candidate is valid only if it:
- reasonably satisfies the requested intent and scope;
- remains an edit of the same source scene;
- preserves viewpoint, fixed structure, geometry, spatial relationships, and
unrelated content unless their modification is required by the request;
- remains physically plausible and free of severe artifacts.
Unsupported reconstruction, structural or layout changes, unrelated edits, wrong
targets, and required-edit no-ops are invalid.
Also rate the complete set:
Instruction Following:
5 = almost all outputs satisfy the request;
3 = mixed performance;
6


1 = most outputs fail.
Groundedness:
5 = source structure is preserved almost throughout;
3 = noticeable drift occurs in several outputs;
1 = most outputs depart from the source.
Return structured JSON containing both ratings and the validity decision for every
candidate.
C.2
High-Level Direction Coverage
Candidate validity has already been fixed. Partition all candidates into high-level
semantic editing directions.
Use different directions for differences in editing target, function, layout,
intervention strategy, or interpretation of the request.
Use the same direction when candidates differ only in color, material, texture,
accessory choice, minor placement, or other low-level realization details.
Each candidate must appear exactly once. A direction is valid if it contains at
least one valid candidate.
Return the direction partition as structured JSON.
C.3
Distinct Realizations and Dispersion
All provided candidates have already passed the validity and grounding checks.
First, cluster candidates into distinct realizations. Keep candidates together when
they express the same consequential edit decision and differ only in
incidental appearance or stochastic rendering.
Split candidates for meaningfully different targets, operations, functions,
layouts, furnishings, or decor solutions.
Then group the realization clusters into high-level directions using differences in
target, function, layout, strategy, or interpretation.
Return both clustering levels as structured JSON.
