ABSTRACT
Clean-image prediction (x-prediction) and noise prediction yield equivalent
population-optimal denoisers, yet can perform very differently in finite diffusion
networks. For noisy input Yt with noise standard deviation σt, a noise predictor
fε,t induces the clean-image estimate b
X1 = Yt −σtfε,t(Yt). This reveals a noise-
transmission burden: the predictor must preserve information needed to cancel
the input corruption. Under Gaussian corruption of d-dimensional data, a linear
input bottleneck of rank r < d imposes a denoising-error floor σ2
t (d −r) even
with unrestricted downstream computation, while the corresponding optimal risk
for image prediction remains bounded. EDM attenuates this bottleneck contribu-
tion at high noise; exact preconditioning cancels it at every noise level. Without
an input bottleneck, assuming exact affine recovery of the noisy patch from the
model representation, we characterize how the covariance spectrum of corrections
beyond linear denoising determines the width required above the patch dimen-
sion. ImageNet experiments with JiT show that measured denoising losses reflect
the predicted bottleneck floor and its attenuation or cancellation through precon-
ditioning. Further widening yields larger relative denoising gains at low noise,
where corrections beyond linear denoising spread across more directions, con-
sistent with our spectral characterization of the required model width. Together,
these results provide a denoising-based account of practical generation-quality
gaps across prediction targets, preconditioning choices, and model widths.

INTRODUCTION
Why should predicting the clean image be easier than predicting the noise added to it? At the
population optimum, image, noise, and velocity predictors are related by simple identities and yield
the same denoiser. Yet their performance can differ substantially in finite networks. JiT demonstrates
this gap in pixel-space diffusion Transformers: image prediction succeeds with coarse patches and
narrow input embeddings in settings where noise or velocity prediction performs poorly (Li & He,
2025). Understanding this mismatch matters for choosing prediction targets and allocating model
capacity. It raises a concrete architectural question: what information must each predictor retain
from its input for accurate denoising, and can it preserve that information when an input bottleneck
reduces the representation dimension below the patch dimension?
Prior work shows that both data geometry and architecture influence prediction-target preference
(Li & He, 2025; Jin & Wang, 2026; Gagneux et al., 2026), but does not quantify how architectural
constraints on retained input information affect denoising. Existing output-rank bounds establish
a loss floor when predictions are confined to a restricted output subspace (Zheng et al., 2025). A
complementary limitation arises when the architecture discards noisy-input information: even an
unrestricted decoder may then lack the information needed for accurate denoising. How does this
error depend on the input bottleneck and noise level, even with unrestricted downstream computa-
tion, and how does an explicit input path change it? Once input compression is removed, how much

We address these questions through the clean-image estimate induced by a noise predictor. For a

a noise-transmission burden. This viewpoint connects input compression, output preconditioning,
and hidden width through their effects on attainable denoising accuracy. Based on this viewpoint,
our theoretical analysis and ImageNet experiments with JiT yield three linked results.
1. An input bottleneck imposes a denoising-error floor for noise prediction that grows
quadratically with noise. Theorem 1 and Corollary 2 establish the floor σ2
t (d −r) when the
architecture imposes a linear input bottleneck of rank r < d, even with unrestricted downstream
computation. The result requires only a finite second moment for the clean data and no man-
ifold assumption; the corresponding optimal risk for image prediction remains bounded under
the same bottleneck. Experiments show denoising loss approaching the floor at high noise (Fig-
ure 2b), with affine noise-recovery probes below the ceiling (Figure 3; Table 3).
2. EDM alleviates the noise-transmission burden at high noise; exact preconditioning cancels
it. Theorem 3 and Corollary 4 characterize how an explicit input path changes the denoising-
error floor imposed by the input bottleneck. EDM suppresses it at high noise, while exact pre-
conditioning removes it at every noise level. Consistent with this analysis, EDM improves FID
from 412.11 for noise prediction to 52.28, and exact preconditioning reaches 9.15, close to im-
age prediction’s 9.13 (Figure 2a). Concurrently, EDM’s denoising loss approaches that of image
prediction at high noise, exact preconditioning tracks image prediction across noise levels (Fig-
ures 2b and 7), and learned skip coefficients closely track their exact-preconditioning values at
moderate and high noise (Figure 6a).
3. Without an input bottleneck, corrections beyond linear denoising determine how far model
width must exceed the patch dimension. Assuming the noisy input patch can be recovered
exactly by an affine readout of the model representation, Theorem 5 and Corollary 6 character-
ize the minimum representation width needed for a chosen level of denoising accuracy. Since
such a representation already supports linear denoising, the additional width needed depends on
the remaining nonlinear correction. Both the correction’s magnitude and the distribution of its
variance across directions matter. In our experiments, modest widening improves generation,
denoising, and affine noisy-input recovery (Figures 4a–b, 5, and 9). Further widening yields
larger relative denoising gains at low noise, where the required nonlinear corrections are spread
across more directions than at high noise (Figures 4b–c and 8b–c).

We call this requirement to carry the input information needed for noise cancellation a noise-
transmission burden for a network with limited capacity. Specifically, a Transformer’s model width
dh, the dimension of each token’s hidden representation, constrains its capacity to represent input
and denoising information. When this width is smaller than the patch dimension dp, or when the
patch passes through an intermediate embedding narrower than dp, the network has an input bot-
tleneck. This bottleneck discards directions of the noisy input, leaving their Gaussian components
inaccessible to subsequent network computation.
We ask three questions to better illuminate this noise-transmission burden. First, when an input
bottleneck is present, how much does it hurt noise recovery and the clean-image estimate induced
by it? Second, how much can output preconditioning, such as EDM and its flow-matching adaptation
(Karras et al., 2022; Kim et al., 2025), alleviate the effect of that bottleneck by supplying an explicit
input contribution? Finally, without such an input bottleneck, how much model width dh is needed
to recover the noisy input and approximate its population denoiser?

If the input embedding discards some directions of the noisy image, how accurately can any subse-

bottleneck patch embedding illustrates the second case: the first linear layer reduces a patch to at
most 128 independent coordinates, and expanding back to model width dh = 768 does not restore
the discarded information (Li & He, 2025, Section 4.2, Figure 4, and Table 9).

3.2
OUTPUT PRECONDITIONING CONTROLS THE NOISE-TRANSMISSION BURDEN
Output preconditioning controls the noise-transmission burden by supplying an explicit input
contribution. Exact preconditioning makes the network’s target a scaled clean image, while
EDM approaches this form at high noise.
Output preconditioning combines the network output with a scaled copy of the noisy input (Karras
et al., 2022; Song et al., 2023; Li & He, 2025), which can mitigate the noise-transmission burden.
To see this, consider the general target Tt = αtX1 + βtε introduced in Section 2. With an input
bottleneck A as in Section 3.1, a preconditioned estimate takes the form
bTt = cskipYt + coutfT,t(cinAYt).

THE RESIDUAL BEYOND LINEAR DENOISING DETERMINES MODEL WIDTH
The extra model width needed for denoising is determined by the residual beyond linear denoising
of the noisy patch, assuming the model representation allows affine recovery of that patch.

EXPERIMENTS
We test our theoretical predictions with experiments on ImageNet. Sections 3.1 and 3.2 show that an
input bottleneck, arising when model width dh is smaller than patch dimension dp or the input passes
through a narrow patch embedding, limits noise recovery (Theorem 1) and imposes a denoising-
error floor (Corollary 2). EDM-style preconditioning alleviates this discarded-noise contribution at
high noise, while exact preconditioning eliminates it (Theorem 3 and Corollary 4). To test these
predictions, we use JiT-B/16 (Li & He, 2025) on ImageNet256 with a 768 →128 →768 bottleneck
patch embedding, comparing prediction targets and output preconditioning through FID-10K, fixed-
time denoising loss, and noise-recovery probes.
Section 3.3 shows that when dh ≥dp, the additional model width needed to approximate the
population-optimal denoiser is determined by the covariance spectrum of its residual beyond the
best affine denoiser, under Assumption 1 (Theorem 5 and Corollary 6). This residual describes the
correction beyond linear denoising needed at each time t. To examine this width requirement, we
remove the bottleneck embedding and widen noise predictors from dh = dp to 2dp, using Ima-
geNet256 with dp = 768 and ImageNet128 with dp = 192. We also probe affine recovery of the
noisy input from the hidden representation to assess empirical support for Assumption 1. Finally,
we use a pretrained ADM denoiser (Dhariwal & Nichol, 2021) as a proxy for the population-optimal
denoiser to estimate the residual spectrum directly.
Both suites use Xt = tX1 + (1 −t)ε, so σt = (1 −t)/t and small t means high noise. Denois-
ing, probe, and residual-spectrum measurements use the ImageNet validation split, held out from
backbone training. Experimental details and additional results are provided in Appendix B.

5
CONCLUSION
Our analysis has several limitations. Our width characterization relies on Assumption 1: the noisy
patch must be exactly recoverable through an affine readout of the representation. This describes one
representation strategy rather than a necessary property of all successful noise predictors. Within our
unrestricted representation class, width 2dp already suffices to represent the population-optimal de-
noiser. Further improvements in generation from widening beyond 2dp are not characterized by our
current denoising-risk theory; explaining these gains is left for future work. Moreover, although our
experiments use networks shared across noise levels and evaluate both denoising and generation, the
theoretical analysis treats each noise level separately, leaving open how parameter sharing changes
the attainable tradeoffs. It also characterizes denoising accuracy without establishing how errors
propagate through sampling or translate into differences between generated and target distributions.

RELATED LITERATURE
Prediction targets and data geometry. JiT motivates image prediction through the manifold hy-
pothesis and demonstrates its advantage in coarse-patch Transformers, including models with nar-
row input embeddings (Li & He, 2025). Jin & Wang (2026) connect data geometry to the preferred
mixture of clean data and noise in the prediction target through an analysis of a linear predictor
shared across time, and propose k-Diff to learn this mixture jointly with the network. These works
establish the importance of data geometry when choosing a prediction target.
A further question is whether the architecture preserves the input information needed to predict that
target accurately. Even directions containing little clean-image information can carry noise that must
be canceled when forming a clean-image estimate from a noise predictor. Our analysis quantifies
the denoising error caused by losing this information: Section 3.1 establishes a rank-dependent er-
ror floor even with unrestricted downstream computation, requiring only Gaussian corruption and a
finite second moment for the clean data. Once input compression is removed, Section 3.3 character-
izes the additional width needed to represent corrections beyond linear denoising, assuming affine
recovery of the noisy patch.
Architecture and prediction-target differences. Gagneux et al. (2026) place prediction parameter-
izations in a common denoising formulation, separate the induced denoiser class from loss weight-
ing, and show that target preference depends strongly on architecture: small-patch Transformers
favor velocity prediction, while larger patches favor image prediction in their tested settings. Their
controlled-manifold experiments also show that intrinsic dimension interacts with the architecture.
These experiments establish that architectural locality can change which prediction target performs
best. Complementing this experimental evidence, RAE derives a sampled-velocity loss floor for a
network with linear input and output maps around a nonlinear body (Zheng et al., 2025, Theorem 1
and Appendix B.1). Its proof uses the restriction of every prediction to the column space of the final
output map. This identifies a limitation on the directions the output can represent.
These results leave open how information discarded at the input translates into unavoidable denois-
ing error, and how much representation width is needed to retain the noisy input while supporting
accurate denoising. Within the common denoising viewpoint, Theorem 1 characterizes information
unavailable from the input while allowing unrestricted decoder outputs, and Corollary 2 translates
the resulting noise-recovery floor into denoising error. Theorem 5 and Corollary 6 then character-
ize the width required for a chosen denoising accuracy, assuming exact affine recovery of the noisy
patch.
Linear denoising and affine input recovery. Wang & Vastola (2024) study Gaussian score approx-
imations based on the data mean and covariance, and provide theoretical and empirical evidence that
these linear approximations describe learned scores well at moderate to high noise. Li et al. (2024) fit
linear models to trained denoisers and find that, in the generalization regime, these approximations
closely resemble the optimal denoisers for a Gaussian distribution with the empirical data mean and
covariance. They also report near-identity behavior at low noise, distinguishing this behavior from
evidence of learned Gaussian structure (Li et al., 2024, Appendix D). These observations motivate
the representation strategy in Section 3.3: keeping the noisy patch affinely accessible provides the
features needed for linear denoising, while additional representation coordinates can encode the re-
maining correction. We formalize this strategy in Assumption 1, which requires exact recovery of

the noisy patch through an affine readout of the model representation. Such a representation supports
any affine denoiser of that patch, including the best affine estimate used as our linear-denoising base-
line. This connects the observed linear structure of learned denoisers to our analysis of the additional
width needed for corrections beyond that baseline.
Preconditioning and analytic input paths. EDM combines a scaled copy of the noisy input with
a learned output using noise-dependent coefficients (Karras et al., 2022); consistency models use a
related construction to enforce their boundary condition (Song et al., 2023). Related approaches use
analytic score components for subspace data (Chen et al., 2023) or project the noise target while
recovering the full velocity analytically (Chen et al., 2026). JiT reports that output preconditioning
improves over direct noise and velocity prediction but remains worse than image prediction, and
links the improvement to its high-noise approach to image prediction (Li & He, 2025, Appendix B.2
and Table 10). Section 3.2 quantifies this pattern through the input-rank-dependent contribution of
discarded noise, distinguishing EDM’s high-noise attenuation from exact cancellation at every noise
level. Theorem 7 also formalizes the raw target’s mean-square convergence to a scaled clean image.
Data preconditioning improves covariance conditioning and optimization in Gaussian and Gaussian-
mixture settings (Ahamed et al., 2026). This complements our output-preconditioning analysis of
the best attainable risk under architectural information constraints.
