LOCI: Localized Continuous Control for
Multi-Subject Image Editing
Sai Vidyaranya Nuthalapati1, Shlok Kumar Mishra1
1Meta AI
Recent instruction-following image editors such as Flux.1-Kontext enable text-driven editing but
lack fine-grained control over where and how much an edit is applied. Existing approaches provide
either continuous but global control or spatially localized editing at a fixed strength. To our knowledge,
none jointly supports explicit spatial localization, continuous per-region strength, and independent
simultaneous control of multiple subjects. We present LOCI (LOcalized Continuous control for multi-
subject Image editing). Given a multi-subject image with per-subject regions and instructions, LOCI
edits each subject at an independently chosen, continuously adjustable strength, for instance gradually
aging one person while adding a beard to another with minimal cross-region leakage. LOCI pairs
subject-level attention binding for spatial isolation with a Regional LoRA layer that replaces the
LoRA’s scalar coefficient with a per-token scaling vector aligned to the spatial layout.
A single
adapter is trained at one suppression point, and continuous edit strengths follow by rescaling its
regional residual at inference. Experiments on multi-subject scenes demonstrate that LOCI achieves
strong edit fidelity with significantly reduced cross-region interference compared to baselines, while
maintaining continuous control over edit strength.

Introduction
The rise of text-to-image (T2I) generative models (Ramesh et al., 2022; Saharia et al., 2022b; Podell et al.,
2023; Rombach et al., 2022) has revolutionized image editing, enabling creative expression at an unprecedented
scale through simple textual prompts. Ranging from Generative Adversarial Networks (Goodfellow et al.,
2014) to diffusion models (Sohl-Dickstein et al., 2015; Song et al., 2021; Dhariwal and Nichol, 2021), to flow-
based models (Lipman et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2023; Esser et al., 2024; Black Forest Labs, 2024), these models
have demonstrated the ability to generate high-quality images which adhere to the text prompts. Recently
diffusion and flow matching models have excelled at various tasks such as inpainting (Lugmayr et al., 2022),
outpainting (Saharia et al., 2022a), style transfer (Zhang et al., 2023), and instruction-based editing (Brooks
et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2024; Sheynin et al., 2024; Black Forest Labs, 2025) to name a few.
Existing editors fall into two families. Firstly, continuous editors expose an edit-strength control but reach a
subject only through the prompt (Zarei et al., 2025; Parihar et al., 2026). Language grounding is coarse: it
can express “replace the dog with a cat,” but not “replace the second dog from the left” when several same-
category instances co-occur. Because the control is never bound to an image region, it cannot designate which
subject to edit, cannot assign independently chosen intensities to several subjects in one pass (Gandikota
et al., 2023; Ye et al., 2025; Endo et al., 2026), and cannot confine the edit once it is applied without spilling
onto its neighbours where an edit intended for one subject spills onto its neighbours (Dahary et al., 2024;
Mun et al., 2025).
The second family binds edits to regions, through masks or attention, and so resolves both designation and
containment. These methods, however, apply the edit at a single fixed strength, offering no interpolation
between the source image and the fully applied edit (Chakrabarty et al., 2024; Zaccagnino et al., 2026; Cai
et al., 2026; Xiong et al., 2026).
The two capabilities therefore live in separate families. To our knowledge, no existing approach combines

We present LOCI, a single framework that resolves these limitations. Given a multi-subject image annotated
with per-subject regions and edit instructions, LOCI edits each designated subject at an independently
chosen, continuously adjustable strength while confining every edit to its own region. Each region is given as
either a bounding box or a segmentation mask, and it supplies the explicit spatial addressing that language
grounding lacks, so the user can specify exactly which subject to modify. Scaling each region’s contribution
independently gives every subject its own continuous strength within a single forward pass, and attention
masking isolates the regions to suppress cross-subject leakage.
Realizing per-region strength control is nontrivial. The conditioning paths in MMDiT backbones (Esser et al.,
2024; Black Forest Labs, 2024) (AdaLN modulation and attention) govern what features are computed, not
how intensely an edit is applied; modulating their parameters does not yield a linear interpolation between
unedited and edited outputs. Attention masking can partition which edit reaches which region (Zaccagnino
et al., 2026), but this isolation is binary and carries no notion of graded strength. On the other hand, the
low-rank update of LoRA (Hu et al., 2022), enters as an additive residual whose magnitude directly governs
edit intensity. Scaling it by zero recovers the base output, scaling by one applies the learned update in full,
and intermediate values interpolate continuously by construction (Zarei et al., 2025). However, scaling the
residual per region is not sufficient on its own because the learned edit direction is spatially entangled: the
residual is written only inside a region, but attention carries its effect outward, so assigning different scales
to different regions does not produce independent edits. Our key insight is that training the LoRA under
instance-aware attention masking yields region-local edit directions. Once each direction is local, per-region
scaling of the residual becomes meaningful, and each subject can be edited at an independently chosen
strength within a single forward pass, without modifying the base architecture.
We make the following
contributions.

1. We introduce LOCI, a framework for independently controllable multi-subject editing that represents an
edit as an instruction, a spatial support, and a continuous control coefficient. At its core is Regional LoRA,
a layer that scales the residual separately in each region instead of applying one strength to the whole
image, so every subject carries its own control and the background carries none. Multiple subject-specific
edits are then composed within one model while their strength and spatial influence stay independently
controlled.
2. We curate a multi-subject evaluation set comprising 128 scenes and 427 source–region–edit tuples spanning
people, animals, vehicles, furniture, and mixed compositions with 2–7 subjects per scene, and define
four evaluation protocols (single-target editing, independent multi-control sweeps, joint editing, and two-
dimensional Cartesian grid control) that together probe target fidelity, cross-subject leakage, continuity,
and slider independence.

Related Work
Instruction-following image editing.
Instruction-following editing has advanced rapidly through better train-
ing data and multi-task objectives (Brooks et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2024; Sheynin et al., 2024). We build
on Flux.1-Kontext (Black Forest Labs, 2025), a prominent recent architecture that represents the source
image as latent tokens concatenated into the transformer’s sequence, a property our construction relies on
(section 3.1).
Spatial control.
Attention manipulation localizes edits without training (Hertz et al., 2023; Cao et al., 2023),
and layout-guided generation injects boxes or keypoints as conditions (Li et al., 2023). For multi-instance
editing, Zaccagnino et al. (Zaccagnino et al., 2026) build disentanglement and harmonization masks from box
layouts to block cross-instance leakage, which we adopt as our spatial backbone. Masked inpainting (Rombach
et al., 2022) localizes an edit but needs a binary region and offers no graded strength. None of these vary
edit intensity smoothly.
Continuous edit-strength control.
Concept Sliders (Gandikota et al., 2023) learn attribute directions in
LoRA weight space. SliderEdit (Zarei et al., 2025) scales instruction-indexed token-selective adapters, and
Kontinuous Kontext (Parihar et al., 2026) maps a scalar strength to instruction-conditioned global modulation.
Both reach a subject only through language, so neither binds a strength control to a user-specified image
region.
Multi-subject and localized editing.
Mask-guided multi-object editing (Chakrabarty et al., 2024) and regional
LoRA composition (Dalva et al., 2025) edit several subjects at once but at a fixed intensity, as do patch-
weighted concept merging (Foteinopoulou et al., 2026) and region-aware adapters (Cai et al., 2026; Xiong
et al., 2026). Layered Diffusion Brushes (Gholami and Xiao, 2024) exposes a regeneration amount tied to
the noise injection timestep. Neither applies distinct per-subject edits, and neither isolates regions at the
attention level. Appendix A expands this comparison.
Positioning.
Existing work addresses continuous strength, spatial control, or multi-subject isolation sepa-
rately. LOCI unifies region-based addressing, per-token LoRA scaling, and instance-disentangled attention,
so several subjects can be edited at independently chosen strengths in a single forward pass.

3.4
Single-Region Suppression Training
Sections 3.2 and 3.3 fix how the adapter is applied and how attention is routed. Here we describe how it is
trained. The frozen editor supplies its own supervision. Run on a source image with a neutral instruction it
reproduces that image, and run with the real instruction it produces the edit. Following (Zarei et al., 2025),
we train a single low-rank adapter, one subject at a time, to turn the second prediction into the first, so that
at α = 1 it removes exactly the change the instruction causes. No edited image is therefore required: each
example is a source image I, an instruction e, and its region M. Training uses the same attention routing as
inference.
Routing-matched suppression.
For each training tuple, we encode I once. One copy of its latent serves as the
clean context latent Xsrc, while another is corrupted with noise by the standard rectified-flow interpolation to
form the denoising latent Xt at a logit-normally sampled noise level (Liu et al., 2023; Esser et al., 2024). We

3.5
Multi-Region Inference
Although training uses a single subject, inference may instantiate N > 1 controls on the token partition of
section 3.1. We assemble all regional coefficients into a single vector λ using equation (5) which modulates
the shared adapter at every denoising step. The controls may have different signs, allowing suppression and
extrapolation to be applied to different subjects in the same trajectory.
Inference applies the support pattern used during single-region training to each subject, so that its instruction
block Tn and the image tokens selected by Gn share the coefficient αn. On subject n’s tokens, λ is therefore
αn times its value during training.
Since no query outside subject n may read Tn under either regime
(section 3.3), adding subjects does not directly expose subject n to another subject’s text, and the same
adapter can be applied without retraining for a particular subject count.

4.2
Quantitative Evaluation
We call a method slider-capable when it exposes a continuous, per-subject edit control, and sweep every
slider-capable method over α ∈{1.0, 0.8, . . . , −0.8, −1.0}.
Compared methods.
1. SliderEdit. The released Selective Token LoRA checkpoint (Zarei et al., 2025), the multi-instruction variant,
which scales instruction text spans but is given no mask, box, or routing.
2. SliderEdit + IDAttn. The same checkpoint with IDAttn (Zaccagnino et al., 2026) added at inference and
each instruction block paired with its subject box, testing whether a naive combination works.
3. IDAttn only. Routing and local instruction blocks, but no LoRA, so there is no learned strength control.
4. Regional LoRA without IDAttn. The regional adapter trained with the masked suppression objective of
section 3.4, but under ordinary full attention.
5. LOCI (ours). The regional adapter and IDAttn routing, trained under the routing-matched objective of
section 3.4.
Two contrasts within this set carry the component analysis. Methods 3, 4 and 5 isolate the two components,
giving routing alone, the regional adapter alone, and both together, which shows what either contributes by
itself and whether the two compound. Methods 2 and 5 form the second contrast, testing whether routing
must be present while the regional edit direction is learned or whether composing the two post hoc suffices.
Evaluation protocols.
1. Protocol A: single-target editing. We supply one subject-level instruction and sweep its control over the full
range, with no instruction assigned to any other subject.
2. Protocol B: independent multi-control. Every subject now carries an instruction, but we move one control
and hold the rest at the neutral anchor α = 1. This measures whether one control can move without
dragging the others with it.
3. Protocol C: joint editing. Every subject carries an instruction, and we generate one image with all regional
controls at the nominal edit, α = 0, which we compare with the corresponding neutral output at α = 1.
This tests whether several requested edits remain effective when composed in the same image.
4. Protocol D: two-dimensional control. We consider two-subject scenes and sweep both controls over the full
grid αA, αB ∈{−1, −2

Protocols B and D require per-subject controls and are therefore run only on the slider-capable methods.
Metrics.
For Protocols A and B we adopt the extrapolation and disentanglement metrics of SliderEdit (Zarei
et al., 2025). Extrapolation is the maximum target similarity attained over the control trajectory, measured
with CLIP (Radford et al., 2021) and SigLIP (Zhai et al., 2023), and disentanglement is the LPIPS (Zhang
et al., 2018), DINOv2 (Oquab et al., 2024) and ArcFace (Deng et al., 2019) distance between each edited out-
put and the source image. We do not adopt their continuity score, which by construction cannot distinguish
a wide, well-ordered sweep from a narrow or noisy one; appendix B gives the argument. In its place we report
two diagnostics of the trajectory itself. Direction correlation is the Spearman rank correlation between α and
the target similarity. Mean range is the average gap between the highest and lowest similarity a trajectory
reaches, which reports how far the control actually moves the target. Target similarity for these diagnostics
is measured between the edit text and a crop of the edited image formed from the subject bounding box,
independently with CLIP and SigLIP.
Protocol C scores each region by the CLIP similarity between a crop of the subject and its edit description, and
measures how much that similarity improves from the neutral output to the nominal edit. Regional success
rate is the fraction of regions that show an edit, and worst-region ∆CLIP is the smallest improvement among
the subjects. Protocol D moves one control by a single step and records the size of each region’s change
per unit of control. Moving a subject’s own control should change that subject, and the average of those
changes is the intended response. Moving the other subject’s control should change nothing, so semantic
cross-talk reports that unintended change as a fraction of the intended one, averaged over the two subjects,
and perceptual cross-talk repeats this with regional LPIPS in place of the CLIP score. Appendix D gives the
formal definitions of every metric above.
Implementation details.
All five methods use FLUX.1-Kontext-dev (Black Forest Labs, 2025) as the frozen
base editor and generate at 1024 × 1024 resolution with 28 denoising steps, guidance scale 3.5, and seed 0.
Only prompt syntax and spatial inputs vary, following each method’s interface. The two trained methods
add a rank-16 LoRA to the frozen transformer and train it for 10,000 steps on 8×H200 GPUs over 22,482
examples derived from HQ-Edit (Hui et al., 2024), each pairing a source image with one subject mask and
one instruction; appendix F gives the full configuration.
Edit strength and preservation.
Across Protocols A and B, LOCI attains both the highest extrapolation and
the lowest disentanglement on every metric and both encoders (table 1). These two normally trade off, since
a method can preserve the source simply by editing less; here the strongest preservation coincides with the
strongest target alignment, so it does not come from weaker editing.
Continuous control.
LOCI also gives the most reliably ordered response and the widest semantic range,
under both protocols and both encoders.
Joint editing.
Under Protocol C, routing is what allows several requested edits to land at once (table 2).
IDAttn alone gives the best coverage but exposes no continuous control, and LOCI comes close to matching
it while retaining an independent slider per subject, so continuous control costs little coverage.
Two-dimensional control.
Protocol D separates the components most clearly and shows that neither substi-
tutes for the other. The regional adapter without routing produces a strong intended response but the worst
cross-talk in the table, worse even than unmodified SliderEdit: the control moves its target, but its effect is
not confined there, which is what a spatially entangled edit direction predicts. Post-hoc composition fails
in the opposite way, containing the edit well while responding weakly. Only training the adapter under the
routing it will be used with achieves both, giving the strongest intended response together with the lowest
cross-talk on both measures, though its perceptual margin over post-hoc composition remains small. Routing
supplies the spatial isolation; routing-aware training supplies the control.

Conclusion
We presented LOCI, a framework for region-aware continuous image editing that edits multiple subjects in
one image at independently chosen strengths within a single forward pass. By replacing the adapter’s scalar
coefficient with a per-token vector aligned to the token partition, and training that adapter under the same
instance-disentangled routing used at inference, we obtain region-local edit directions whose rescaling gives
each subject its own continuous control.

Appendix
A
Extended Related Work
This section expands the comparison summarized in section 2.
Instruction-following image editing.
InstructPix2Pix (Brooks et al., 2023) established the paradigm by train-
ing on synthetically generated edit triplets, MagicBrush (Zhang et al., 2024) improved data quality with
manually annotated pairs, and Emu Edit (Sheynin et al., 2024) decomposed editing into recognition and
generation sub-tasks. Flux.1-Kontext (Black Forest Labs, 2025) concatenates source and noise latents
inside an MMDiT backbone.
Spatial control.
Prompt-to-Prompt (Hertz et al., 2023) edits by swapping cross-attention maps between
prompts, and MasaCtrl (Cao et al., 2023) obtains consistent synthesis by sharing mutual self-attention keys
and values. GLIGEN (Li et al., 2023) injects bounding box or keypoint conditions through gated self-attention,
but targets generation rather than editing.
Continuous edit-strength control.
NumeriKontrol (Kang et al., 2025) conditions on explicit numeric descrip-
tors written into the prompt, which gives numeric control over an attribute but no spatial addressing.
Multi-subject and localized editing.
Differential Diffusion (Levin and Fried, 2023) assigns each pixel or region
its own strength through a change map. Because the map scales the per-pixel noising schedule under a single
global prompt, it modulates how much a region changes rather than applying distinct edits to different
subjects, and it provides no attention-level isolation. Layered Diffusion Brushes (Gholami and Xiao, 2024)
exposes a per-layer strength that scales the Gaussian noise injected into a user-drawn region. That control
is a regeneration amount coupled to the injection timestep, rather than a disentangled slider interpolating
a single edit from source to full application. Both rely on manually specified regions and allow regions to
interact without isolation.
Cross-subject interference.
Interference between subjects is well documented. Bounded Attention (Dahary
et al., 2024) attributes it to semantic leakage between subjects in the attention layers, and text-embedding
leakage has been shown to spill edits onto unintended regions (Mun et al., 2025). These studies motivate a
dedicated isolation mechanism rather than reliance on language alone.
B
Why We Do Not Report Semantic Continuity
SliderEdit (Zarei et al., 2025) summarizes the continuousness of a control trajectory with a continuity score.
Given the similarity sequence s1, . . . , sδ recorded over the α sweep, the interval [mini si, maxi si] is divided
into δ bins, the number of scores falling in each bin is counted, and the reported value is (χ2/dof)−1 against
the uniform expectation of one score per bin. Higher values indicate a more evenly traversed range.
Two normalizations in that construction discard the information a slider is meant to provide. Binning counts
is invariant to order, so a trajectory that oscillates scores exactly as well as one that rises monotonically.
Binning between the observed minimum and maximum is invariant to scale, so a trajectory whose similarity
moves by 0.005 is stretched to fill the same bins as one that moves by 0.30. A control that barely responds
therefore scores highly: measurement noise is close to uniformly distributed by construction, which is exactly
what the statistic rewards.
We therefore report the trajectory diagnostics of table 1 instead: Spearman correlation restores sensitivity
to order, and mean range restores sensitivity to scale.

Implementation Details
Training data.
Training examples are derived from HQ-Edit (Hui et al., 2024).
We decode all 98,675
source images together with their original edit instructions. Each instruction is passed to a vision–language
model (Bai et al., 2025), which assigns it one of four types, modify, add, remove or global, and names the
primary target noun; the instruction text itself is left unchanged. That noun prompts Grounding-DINO (Liu
et al., 2024), and the resulting box is passed to SAM2 (Ravi et al., 2024) to obtain a mask. We discard
examples for which no usable mask is produced, and those whose instruction requests a change of pose,
movement, size, rotation or camera viewpoint, as these do not correspond to a confined image region. The
resulting manifest holds 23,591 examples; removing instances whose mask covers less than 3% of the image
leaves 22,482 training examples spanning 21,962 distinct instructions. Rows are shuffled during training, but
instructions are used as written and are never resampled, recombined or synthesised.
The VAE, the T5 and CLIP text encoders, and the scheduler are frozen wherever applicable, and we train
only a regional adapter: a rank-16 LoRA without dropout wrapping the linear layers of the transformer,
stored in fp32 while the frozen backbone computes in bf16.
The per-region write gate projects each subject’s mask onto the transformer’s token grid. The VAE down-
samples by 8× and Flux.1-Kontext packs 2 × 2 latent neighbourhoods into one token, so each token cell
spans 16×16 pixels and a 1024×1024 input yields a 64×64 grid. The gate takes the max over each cell rather
than a coverage threshold, which keeps thin structures alive at the cost of dilating each gate by at most one
token. The resulting per-token scale is applied stream-aware: text entries are sliced into the dual-stream text
modules, image entries into the dual-stream image modules, and the full vector into single-stream blocks.
The text sequence uses a 512-token T5 encoding truncated to the first 200 positions per instruction block.
Combined with 4096 noise and 4096 source-image tokens, the joint sequence is 8,392 tokens for a single-region
training example.
Both adapters are trained for 10,000 steps on 8×H200 GPUs under data parallelism with one example per
rank (effective batch size 8), using AdamW (lr = 7 × 10−5, weight decay 10−5), a cosine schedule with 500
warmup steps, gradient clipping at 1.0, gradient checkpointing, logit-normal timestep sampling (µ = 0, σ = 1),
guidance embedding 3.5, and no SNR reweighting.
