Abstract
We present Scalable Pixel-anchored End-to-end Diffusion
(SpeeDiff), a latent diffusion method that jointly trains the
VAE and the diffusion model from scratch. In principle, joint
training allows the diffusion loss gradient to directly guide
the VAE encoder, encouraging the formation of a generation-
friendly latent space and potentially yielding faster con-
vergence than the conventional two-stage approach with a
pretrained frozen VAE. However, a naive end-to-end imple-
mentation severely degrades performance, as unrestricted
backpropagation of the diffusion loss leads to latent space
collapse. Our main technical contribution is a simple yet
effective Tweedie Pixel Reconstruction (TPR) loss, which
provides additional pixel-level feedback by decoding a pre-
dicted clean latent from an intermediate noisy state using
Tweedie’s formula, thereby alleviating collapse. Further-

1. Introduction
Latent diffusion models (LDMs) [41, 46, 54] have emerged
as a dominant paradigm for visual synthesis. These mod-
els perform the diffusion process in a compact latent space
produced by a visual tokenizer, typically a variational au-
toencoder (VAE) [28]. In the standard two-stage training
pipeline, the visual tokenizer is first trained to encode raw
visual data into a compressed latent space, after which a dif-
1
arXiv:2610.36419v1  [cs.CV]  26 Oct 2026


fusion model is learned to operate within that latent domain
while keeping the pretrained visual tokenizer frozen.
Recent advances in LDMs have largely focused on en-
hancing the VAEs by leveraging vision foundation models
(VFMs) [18, 40, 45, 50, 61]. Despite their success, VAEs
trained with reconstruction objectives primarily capture low-
level pixel statistics, resulting in latent spaces that do not
directly associate with sufficient semantic knowledge, pos-
ing challenges for the diffusion model to learn efficiently.
Motivated by semantically rich representations from self-
supervise pretrained VFMs, recent works either incorporate
representation alignment to a VFM [33, 58] or directly re-
place the VAE encoder with a VFM [3, 5, 49, 63]. These
approaches have achieved remarkable improvements in train-
ing efficiency and generative quality.
In contrast to improving the visual tokenizer through
VFMs, much less attention has been given to enhancing it
via end-to-end optimization with direct feedback from the
diffusion model training. This idea can in fact be traced back
to LSGM [54], where the VAE and diffusion model were
jointly trained. However, vanilla end-to-end training was
found to degrade overall performance, a phenomenon that
has also been revisited in recent studies [33]. We believe that
enabling truly end-to-end optimization holds the potential to
further push the performance limits of LDMs, yet existing
approaches still lack effective mechanisms to support that.
In this paper, we focus on the core idea of enhancing
the VAE through end-to-end direct gradient feedback from
diffusion training, and show that allowing the latent space to
evolve under generative supervision further improves both
training efficiency and synthesis quality. After carefully
analyzing the vanilla end-to-end training, we find that the
diffusion loss tends to collapse the latent space into a de-
generate representation. Specifically, we observe that the
latent statistics exhibit severely suppressed per-channel vari-
ances and large per-channel biass (see Fig. 3). We further
demonstrate that this latent collapse can be effectively alle-
viated with a simple Tweedie Pixel Reconstruction (TPR)
loss, which computes a pixel-level reconstruction loss by de-
coding the predicted clean latent from an intermediate noisy
state using Tweedie’s formula [9]. Extensive experiments
show that this additional pixel-level feedback anchors the
VAE and prevents latent degeneracy, resulting in robust and
well-converged end-to-end training.
Building upon the end-to-end training framework, we
further improve latent diffusion model through two enhance-
ments. We first refine the architecture by replacing the con-
ventional convolutional VAE (CNN-VAE) with a vision-
transformer-based variant (ViT-VAE) and modernizing the
diffusion backbone (DiT [37, 41]) with recent architectural
improvements. In addition, inspired by REPA [33, 60], we
introduce REPA++, a enhanced representation alignment
method in our end-to-end training that aligns both the latent
and the intermediate diffusion features with VFM representa-
tions (e.g., DINOv3 [50]) and is optimized end-to-end with
both diffusion and VAE.
Our final method, SpeeDiff, is a single-stage end-to-end
latent diffusion framework that jointly trains the VAE and
diffusion model from scratch without stop-gradient opera-
tions (see Fig. 1b), simplifying the conventional two-stage
pipeline while substantially accelerating diffusion conver-
gence (see Fig. 1c) and improving scaling effect (see Fig. 1d).
To assess the contribution of representation alignment, we
compare SpeeDiff variants with and without REPA++. The
model without alignment achieves an FID of 2.42, setting
a new state-of-the-art among methods that do not rely on
VFM alignment, whereas incorporating REPA++ further re-
duces the FID to 1.50. For ImageNet 512ˆ512, SpeeDiff-XL
model using a efficient 32ˆ compressed VAE achieves an
FID of 1.53 without guidance.

Variational autoencoder as tokenizer.
Visual tokenizers
employ either continuous [32, 46], discrete [10, 36], or uni-

tokenizers for visual generation, which are predominantly
implemented using variational autoencoders (VAEs) [28, 46].
Such VAE-based tokenizers have become a fundamental
component of modern text-to-image and video generation
systems [15, 29, 32, 43, 55].

models synthesize images by gradually transforming Gaus-

duced by pretrained tokenizer to reduce costs. In this work,
we adopt the LDM formulation under the unified perspective
of stochastic interpolants [1, 37].

Representation from VFMs.
Recent studies show that
incorporating semantic representations from self-supervise
pretrained vision foundation models (VFMs), such as
DINO [40, 50], CLIP [45] and MAE [18], can substan-
tially improve the convergence and generative quality of
diffusion models. Depending on how VFMs are utilized,
existing approaches fall into two main categories: represen-
tation alignment and direct representation utilization. The
representation alignment family [33, 58, 60] distills VFM
semantics by enforcing consistency between latent (or inter-
mediate diffusion features) and VFM representations. For
instance, REPA [60] employs a cosine-similarity objective,
whereas VA-VAE [58] adopts a more sophisticated simi-
larity loss. REPA-E [33] backpropagates a representation
alignment loss through from diffusion to VAE encoder. In
contrast, another line of work [3, 5, 49, 63] replaces the VAE
encoder with a pretrained VFM and directly leverages its
representation as the latent, with different design choices
such as freezing the tokenizer in RAE [63], adding a learn-
able residual module in SVG [49], or inserting a learnable
projection layer in VFM-VAE [3]. To better integrate VFMs
into our end-to-end training paradigm as a flexible plug-and-
play module, we adopt the representation alignment strategy
and introduce REPA++, an enhanced alignment mechanism
in our end-to-end training framework.
3. Our Approach
We now outline the key components of our approach. We
first analyze the vanilla end-to-end training (Sec. 3.1) and
identify latent collapse as the primary cause of failure. To ad-
dress this issue, we introduce a simple yet effective Tweedie
Pixel Reconstruction loss that provides additional pixel-level
feedback (Sec. 3.2). We then enhance the framework with
refined architectures and REPA++ (Sec. 3.3, Sec. 3.4) to
accelerate convergence and improve final performance.
3.1. Vanilla End-to-end Training
The vanilla end-to-end setup directly couples the VAE and
diffusion model by optimizing both objectives simultane-
ously from scratch:
 \label {eq:van il la - e2e} \m at h cal L_{\ text {Vanilla-E2E}}(\vphi , \vpsi , \vtheta ) = \mathcal L_{\text {VAE}}(\vphi , \vpsi ) + \mathcal L_{\text {Diff}}(\vphi , \vtheta ), 
(1)
where ϕ, ψ and θ refers to the parameters of VAE encoder,
VAE decoder and diffusion, respectively. Despite its concep-
tually simple formulation, backpropagating the diffusion loss
into the VAE encoder causes severe degradation of the latent
space, consistent with observations in prior work [33]. In
our experiments, the vanilla end-to-end setup attains an FID
of 33.95 on ImageNet 256ˆ256 after 80 epochs, which is
considerably worse than standard two-stage LDM baselines.
We refer this setting as Vanilla E2E.
Why does Vanilla E2E training fail?
We observe that
vanilla end-to-end training drives the latent space into a
highly degenerate representation. To diagnose this phe-
nomenon, we compute the Tweedie estimate of the clean
latent ˆz0 “ zt ´ tVθpzt, tq and compare the latent recon-
struction error }ˆz0 ´ z0}2 with the corresponding pixel re-
construction error }Dψpˆz0q´x0}2. As shown in Fig. 3a, the
latent error is nearly zero, whereas the pixel error remains
substantial. This indicates that the diffusion model fits its
latent-space objective very well, yet the resulting latent code
no longer preserves the information required for accurate
pixel reconstruction.
To further scrutinize this failure mode, we visualize the
latent distribution and find that it deviates significantly from
a standard Gaussian prior, forming multiple sharp peaks in
latent space (see Fig. 3b). A closer inspection reveals strong
per-channel biases and heavily suppressed per-channel vari-
ances (see Fig. 3c). In such a collapsed regime, most latent

diffusion loss in latent space while discarding the semantic
content needed for faithful image reconstruction.
3.2. Tweedie Pixel Reconstruction Loss
The latent collapse observed in vanilla end-to-end training
suggests that the diffusion model can exploit the latent space
for trivial solution when no pixel-level constraints are pro-
vided. To counter this, we strengthen the connection between
diffusion predictions and the underlying image content so
that the VAE is compelled to maintain a semantically mean-
ingful latent representation. This motivates the Tweedie
Pixel Reconstruction (TPR) loss, which decodes the Tweedie-
estimated latent back into pixel space and directly enforces
consistency with the clean image.
Given the stochastic interpolant zt “ p1 ´ tqz0 ` tϵ,
the diffusion model estimates the clean latent via Tweedie’s
formula ˆz0 “ zt ´tVθpzt, tq. We decode this estimate using
Dψ and penalize discrepancies with the ground-truth image:

3.3. Scaling with Refined Architecture
Building on our end-to-end framework, we adopt a vision-
transformer-based VAE (ViT-VAE) in place of the conven-
tional convolutional design (CNN-VAE). The encoder em-
ploys a patch-embedding layer followed by transformer
blocks, and the decoder mirrors this structure.
For the diffusion backbone, we refine DiT [41] with sev-
eral modern architectural components. Following Lighten-
ingDiT [58], we integrate RMSNorm, SwiGLU activations,
and 2D RoPE, and we replace block-specific modulation
layers with a shared global modulation mechanism supple-
mented by per-block learnable biases, similar to PixArt-α [6].
Unless otherwise specified, the patch size is fixed to 1 in
all experiments. We refer this as Refined-DiT. Additional
architectural details are provided in Sec. A.1.
Employing ViT-VAE and Refined-DiT and training them
end-to-end with the TPR loss, this variant reduces computa-
tion while improving performance, enabling improved scal-
ing behavior. Model configurations are summarized in Tab. 7
in Appendix. Each training step consists of two decoder
passes (for the VAE and the TPR objective) and one encoder
and diffusion pass. With these architectural refinements, the
total training cost decreases from 436.29 to 334.98 GFLOPs,
and the model achieves an improved FID of 3.66 on Ima-
geNet 256ˆ256 after 80 epochs. We refer to this setting as
SpeeDiff (w/o REPA++) to emphasize that it does not require

For class-conditioned generation, we adopt

NFEs by default. Guidance sampling uses AutoGuidance
(AG) [25] instead of Classifier-Free Guidance (CFG) [21],
as we observe that the benefit of CFG diminishes when the
diffusion model attains strong unconditional performance
with the null-class embedding. AG addresses this issue by
leveraging a weaker auxiliary model that does not rely on
null-class conditioning. Further implementation details and
rationale are discussed in Sec. A.3.1.

4.3. Impact of E2E Training for Diffusion
Accelerated convergence.
A primary advantage of SpeeD-
iff lies in its ability to substantially accelerate diffusion
model convergence. On ImageNet 256ˆ256, SpeeDiff-XL
(w/ REPA++) reaches a gFID of 7.36 without guidance
within 10 epochs, already surpassing the vanilla SiT baseline
trained for 1400 epochs. As shown in Tab. 2, SpeeDiff-
XL (w/o REPA++) attains a gFID of 3.66 without guidance
after 80 epochs, indicating that allowing diffusion loss gradi-
ents to directly shape the latent space significantly improves
convergence speed. The incorporation of REPA++ further
strengthens this effect, enabling SpeeDiff-XL to reach a
gFID of 1.69 without guidance after 80 epochs. SpeeDiff
could produce high visual fidelity without guidance, with
representative samples presented in Fig. 4.

proved scaling behavior in diffusion models. As shown in
Fig. 1 and Tabs. 2 and 3, SpeeDiff exhibits a clearer and
more monotonic scaling trend compared to prior state of the
art approaches such as EDM2 [26]. SpeeDiff adopts a fully
transformer based pipeline, which benefits from the strong
scaling properties and avoids the capacity limitations of con-

4.4. Impact of E2E Training for VAE Latent
Automatic semantic gain.
Analysis of the latent spaces of
SpeeDiff (w/o REPA++) and SpeeDiff (w/ REPA++) shows
that end-to-end training with diffusion loss gradients nat-
urally smooths the latent and strengthens its semantic or-
ganization, as illustrated in Fig. 5a, where independently
trained VAEs tend to overfit low level image statistics and
exhibit weak semantic separation. This phenomenon is fur-
ther supported by the quantitative results in Fig. 5b, where
latent linear probing accuracy on ImageNet improves by
9.45%, indicating that the diffusion objective induces a more
semantically aligned latent space.
Generation-friendly latent space.
We further examine
whether the trained VAE in SpeeDiff remains effective when
used separately from the diffusion model it was trained
with. We freeze the converged VAE from SpeeDiff-XL (w/
REPA++) and train a new diffusion model from scratch,
which converges at a similar speed to SpeeDiff (Tab. 4). This
suggests that SpeeDiff learns a generation-friendly latent
space. From this perspective, SpeeDiff can be interpreted
as performing latent pretraining, producing representations
inherently better suited for downstream generative modeling.

Latent channels.
The number of latent channels serves
as a key hyperparameter governing the trade-off between
reconstruction and generation. Increasing the latent channels
generally alleviates the information bottleneck and improves
reconstruction. However, excessively large latent channels
often lead to a less compact and less semantically structured
latent space, making the diffusion model more difficult to
train. Across a range of model sizes and settings, we ob-
serve that 32 channels consistently yield the best overall
performance, shown in Tab. 6.
5. Conclusion
In this work, we conduct a detailed analysis of why vanilla
end-to-end training of the VAE and diffusion model fails, and
identify latent space collapse as the primary cause. By intro-
ducing the Tweedie Pixel Reconstruction loss, we establish
a stable and effective optimization pathway that enables the
VAE to benefit directly from diffusion loss gradient supervi-
sion. Building on this foundation, architectural refinements
together with the proposed REPA++ further improve con-
vergence speed and generative quality. Overall, SpeeDiff
sets new state-of-the-art results for both non-alignment and
VFM-aligned latent diffusion models.

A. Detailed Implementation of SpeeDiff
This section provides comprehensive implementation details of SpeeDiff to ensure full reproducibility. We describe our
model architectures in Sec. A.1, training configurations in Sec. A.2, and sampling setup in Sec. A.3. A complete summary of
hyperparameters and model configurations is included in Tab. 14.
A.1. Model Details
A.1.1. ViT-VAE
We adopt a vision-transformer–based VAE (ViT-VAE) in place of the conventional convolutional VAE, as transformer
architectures have better scalibility and offer improved compute efficiency (see Tab. 7 for comparison).
Encoder.
The encoder begins with a patch-embedding layer that tokenizes each p ˆ p patch into a hidden vector. We use
p“16 for ImageNet 256ˆ256 and p“32 for 512ˆ512 generation. The embedded tokens are processed by a stack of standard
transformer blocks equipped with 2D RoPE [19] positional encoding and SwiGLU [48] feedforward layers. A final linear
projection maps the output tokens into a compact latent representation of dimension d“32.
Decoder.
The decoder mirrors the encoder. It first maps the latent tokens back to the hidden dimension through a linear
layer, followed by transformer blocks, and finally an unpatch-embedding layer that reconstructs pixel-space outputs. To further
refine visual details, we append a single convolutional layer initialized as an identity mapping. This lightweight refinement
consistently improves visual quality.
A.1.2. Refined-DiT
Our diffusion model, Refined-DiT, is based on recent architectural advances from Lightening DiT [58] and incorporates a
more parameter-efficient modulation mechanism. Due to spatial downsampling introduced by the VAE, we fix the diffusion
model’s patch size to 1, implemented as a simple linear projection into the transformer hidden dimension. The model then
applies a sequence of diffusion transformer blocks. Instead of per-block modulation, we adopt a global modulation layer
combined with learnable per-block biases, following the design strategy of PixArt-α [6]. This approach reduces parameters
and computational overhead while preserving generative performance.

Discriminator Loss.
We incorporate a PatchGAN-style discriminator to enhance perceptual fidelity. To ensure stable
adversarial training without relying on warm-up schedules or dynamically adjusted weights, we introduce two modifications:
• Replace BatchNorm with SpectralNorm [38] to improve Lipschitz continuity and reduce training instability.
• Apply LeCam regularization [53], which tracks an exponential moving average of real and fake logits and penalizes large
deviations, effectively smoothing discriminator updates.
These stabilizations allow us to adopt a fixed discriminator loss weight and enable adversarial supervision from the beginning
of training without the need for special scheduling.
Variational Objective (KL Loss).
The evidence lower bound (ELBO) [28] has an additional KL divergence loss between
the posterior pϕpz0|x0q and the standard Gaussian prior Np0, Iq. Interestingly, the KL weight strongly affects the trade-off
between reconstruction of VAE and generative of diffusion. As the KL weight increases, reconstruction performance decreases
while generative quality initially improves and then degrades. We select the turning point that yields the best generation
performance; details are provided in Sec. B.3.

A.3. Sampling Details
In this section, we describe our guidance sampling strategy based on AutoGuidance [25] and discuss how representation
alignment affects Classifier-Free Guidance (CFG) [21] in SpeeDiff. In particular, we analyze why the gains from CFG tend to
saturate when REPA++ is enabled.
A.3.1. AutoGuidance
For all SpeeDiff variants, we train an additional bad version of the diffusion model required by AutoGuidance. Concretely,
this bad model is architecturally smaller: it uses half the number of transformer blocks and half the number of attention heads
compared to the main model. To decouple it from end-to-end training, we pair this bad diffusion model with the frozen VAE
obtained from the 200-epoch checkpoint and train the bad diffusion model from scratch for only 20 epochs.
At sampling time, we apply AutoGuidance using this bad model in conjunction with the main model. The guidance scale
and guidance intervals are tuned per model size to achieve the best gFID score.
A.3.2. Analysis of Unconditional Generation and CFG
We compare three settings for guidance: (1) without guidance, (2) CFG, and (3) AutoGuidance (AG). For SpeeDiff (w/
REPA++), we observe that the improvement from CFG over the unguided model is noticeably saturated, whereas AG continues
to provide a substantial gain.
After careful analysis, we identify a key contributing factor: the unconditional generation of SpeeDiff (with REPA++) is
already surprisingly strong. In other words, the null-class (unconditional) predictions of the main model produce high-quality
samples. We illustrate unconditional samples in Fig. 6 and report their FID scores in Tab. 8. Due to the strong unconditional
sampling quality, the negative direction in CFG provides only limited additional guidance. AutoGuidance avoids this issue by
constructing the negative direction using the prediction of a deliberately degraded bad model under the conditional class. This
alternative direction is substantially more informative, and as a result, AutoGuidance delivers a much larger improvement in
FID compared to CFG.

B. Additional Results and Ablations
B.1. Joint Scaling of VAE and Diffusion
In this section, we examine how jointly scaling the VAE and the diffusion model affects performance. Unlike prior latent-
diffusion pipelines that pair a fixed-size VAE with diffusion models of various capacities, SpeeDiff benefits substantially from
joint scaling both components. A larger VAE not only produces a more expressive latent space but also provides stronger
feedback to the diffusion model during end-to-end training.
To understand how model capacity should be allocated, we systematically vary the number of transformer layers assigned to
the encoder, decoder, and diffusion model. As shown in Fig. 7, several trends emerge. First, the encoder and decoder perform
best when their depths are approximately balanced (around a 1:1 ratio). Second, dedicating roughly 40% of total layers to the
VAE yields a strong trade-off between reconstruction quality and downstream generative performance. Finally, we observe a
consistent and stable scaling trend across model sizes, both with and without REPA++, when following a 1:1:3 allocation rule
for encoder, decoder, and diffusion layers.
B.2. SpeeDiff with Conventional Architecture
We further evaluate the impact of our refined architectures by comparing ViT-VAE and Refined-DiT with conventional
CNN-VAE and DiT variants. As shown in Tab. 10, both refinements consistently improve performance across model scales.
Across both non-aligned and REPA++ settings, our refined architectures provide consistent improvements over conventional
CNN-VAE and DiT designs. Without alignment, replacing DiT with Refined-DiT already yields substantial gains (e.g., gFID
improves from 5.89 to 3.71 with CNN-VAE), and replacing CNN-VAE with ViT-VAE further enhances performance, reaching
3.66 at the XL scale on ImageNet 256ˆ256 generation after 80 epochs. With REPA++, the benefits become even more
pronounced: Refined-DiT-XL lowers gFID from 2.83 to 2.15, and jointly scaling the VAE achieves the best overall result
of 1.69 on ImageNet 256ˆ256 generation after 80 epochs. Together, these findings confirm the effectiveness of our refined
architectures.

B.5. Analysis of Practical Cost
We analyze the practical cost of SpeeDiff by measuring the time-level speedup attributable to end-to-end training. Specifically,
we compare SpeeDiff (w/o REPA++) against the two-stage baseline, which assumes a pretrained VAE with preprocessed latents
and thus requires only a single diffusion forward pass per training step. In contrast, our end-to-end approach additionally
involves one VAE encoder call and two decoder calls per step, resulting in an approximately 2ˆ slower per-step time (see
Tab. 13 for detailed per-step timing and Tab. 7 for model FLOPs).
Despite this per-step overhead, SpeeDiff converges dramatically faster in terms of training steps. Taking the baseline’s 7.93
FID without guidance at 4M steps as the reference point, SpeeDiff reaches the same FID in only 0.17M steps. This translates
to a 23.52ˆ step-level speedup and, after accounting for the per-step cost difference, a 12.14ˆ time-level speedup. Notably,
these numbers are conservative: the two-stage pipeline additionally requires VAE pretraining or finetuning as a prerequisite,
whereas SpeeDiff trains the VAE jointly from scratch, further widening the practical efficiency gap.
