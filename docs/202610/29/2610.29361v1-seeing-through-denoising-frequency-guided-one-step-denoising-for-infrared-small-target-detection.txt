Seeing through Denoising: Frequency-guided One-step Denoising for
Infrared Small Target Detection
Xuelin Qian, Zixuan Wang, Chang Xu, Dingwen Zhang and Junwei Han
Abstract—Infrared small target detection (IRSTD) is a chal-
lenging task due to the extremely small size of targets, low signal-
to-noise ratios, and heavy background clutter in infrared imagery.
While the progress of deep learning has advanced this field,
existing methods primarily adopt discriminative paradigms that
directly map images to target masks, overlooking the potential
of generative modeling. In this paper, we propose DiffIRSTD,
a novel generative framework that reformulates infrared small
target detection as a background reconstruction problem. Instead
of explicitly predicting target regions, we reinterpret small targets
as noise-like signals and leverage a diffusion-based model to
recover a clean, target-free background from a perturbed input
image. The residual between the reconstructed background and
the original image is then exploited to highlight candidate target
regions, providing explicit cues for subsequent detection. To
address the computational cost typically associated with diffusion
sampling, we introduce a one-step denoising strategy that enables
efficient background reconstruction while maintaining practical
inference speed. Furthermore, we present a frequency-guided
perception loss to preserve high-frequency background details
and focus more on the reconstruction quality of target regions.
More importantly, our DiffIRSTD operates as a plug-and-play
framework, compatible with existing IRSTD models without
increasing the computational complexity of detectors. Extensive
experiments on IRSTD-1K, NUAA-SIRST, and NUDT-SIRST
datasets demonstrate the superiority of our method, achieving
state-of-the-art performance across three benchmarks.
Index Terms—Infrared small target detection, one-step de-
noising, background reconstruction, frequency-guided perceptual
loss.
I. INTRODUCTION
I
NFRARED small target detection (IRSTD) aims to auto-
matically localize and segment small targets in infrared
imagery, where targets often occupy only a few pixels and
lack clear structural or texture cues [1]. Such targets typically
appear as dim and point-like targets, such as distant aircraft,
missiles, or drones, in complex and cluttered backgrounds.
IRSTD is critical in numerous military and civilian scenarios,
including early warning systems, long-range surveillance, and
autonomous sensing. However, the long imaging distance, low
signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), and strong background interfer-
ence often cause targets to be submerged in noise and clutter,
making robust and accurate detection highly challenging.
Recent advances in deep learning have significantly im-
proved IRSTD performance. Early approaches mainly rely on
U-Net architectures, which enhance small target representation
through multi-scale feature interaction [2]–[5]. Transformer-
based models are later introduced to capture long-range de-
pendencies and global contextual information, further boost-
ing detection accuracy [6]–[8]. More recently, large vision
foundation models, such as Mamba [9]–[11] and SAM [12]–
[14], have been explored to enrich feature representations and
improve generalization. Despite these developments, most ex-
isting methods remain fundamentally discriminative, focusing
on learning direct mappings from images to target masks,
while the potential of generative modeling in IRSTD remains
underexplored.
Diffusion models [15] have recently demonstrated remark-
able capability in generative modeling and have achieved
impressive success in image synthesis, restoration, and in-
painting. Existing diffusion-based studies primarily explore
two directions of either using diffusion models to generate
additional training samples to alleviate data scarcity [16], or
employing diffusion-based networks as iterative decoders to
progressively refine predicted masks [17]–[19]. Nevertheless,
data generation struggles to faithfully synthesize small infrared
targets due to their low intensity and ambiguous boundaries,
whereas iterative diffusion decoders may introduce additional
computational overhead, which limits deployment in resource-
constrained scenarios.
From an imaging perspective, infrared small targets typi-
cally exhibit intensity values that differ from their surround-
ing background, appearing as local “anomalies” against the
smooth background (see Fig. 1(a)). However, factors such as
blurry target boundaries, complex background textures, and
low SNR frequently obscure these “anomalies”. Motivated by
this analysis, we propose a novel generative paradigm for
infrared small target detection, termed DiffIRSTD. Instead
of directly predicting targets from raw images, we reinterpret
small targets as noise-like signals and leverage diffusion
models to denoise them from the image, as illustrated in
Fig. 1(b)-(c). By reconstructing a clean background image
and comparing it with the original input, the target regions
can be explicitly highlighted (Fig. 1(d)). This counterintuitive
perspective transforms the detection problem into a back-
ground reconstruction problem. Reconstructing the low-rank
background is expected to be easier than directly generating
small target masks, and this pipeline is not required to alter
the architecture or increase the complexity of the downstream
detector.
Specifically, our DiffIRSTD framework consists of a re-
construction network for generating target-free background
images and a detection network for localizing small targets.
Given an input infrared image, we first perturb it by adding
Gaussian noise, where the target regions are implicitly treated
as part of the noise. The noisy image is then processed by
a diffusion model to reconstruct a clean background image
in which the targets are removed. Diffusion models typically
require dozens or even hundreds of sampling steps to generate
high-quality images. Such heavy iterative sampling is compu-
tationally expensive and conflicts with the efficiency require-

complexity of the detection network, and it can be flexibly
adapted to different deployment constraints. In summary, our
contributions can be organized as follows,
• We propose a novel diffusion-based framework for in-

tive for denoising. We treat the small targets as a part of
noise perturbation and use the diffusion reverse process

• To improve the efficiency of diffusion sampling, we

introduce a frequency-guided perceptual loss. The con-

target area.
• Extensive experiments on three IRSTD benchmarks
demonstrate that our proposed method outperforms state-
of-the-art approaches, achieving superior detection per-
formance.
II. RELATED WORK
A. Infrared Small Target Detection
In the field of deep learning-based IRSTD methods, the
research utilizing CNNs has mainly focused on structural
fine-tuning and loss function optimization to break through
the bottleneck that small target features are prone to being
submerged. DNA-Net [20] proposes a dense nested interaction
module to avoid small target loss during deep downsampling.
UIU-Net [3] adopts a U-Net nested structure to strengthen
multi-scale information retention. ISNet [5] emphasizes the
crucial role of target shape information by designing an edge
block. MSHNet [4] proposes a scale and location-sensitive
loss to improve the model’s discriminative ability for targets
of different scales and spatial positions. Transformer-based
methods like SCTransNet [6] deploy spatial-channel cross
Transformer blocks at the skip connections of U-Net to
bridge the semantic gap between the encoder and decoder.
RKformer [8] introduces ViTs to balance local details and
global dependencies. DATransNet [7] proposes a DATrans to
accurately extract target edge gradients. Recently, research on
IRSTD has been extended to Mamba and SAM to enrich
feature representations. MiM-ISTD [9] proposes a ”Mamba-
in-Mamba” nested design, which achieves a leap in accu-
racy while maintaining linear computational overhead. IR-
Mamba [10] introduces PDMamba to enhance target contour
perception. In the SAM-based research, IRSAM [12] is the
first work to adapt SAM to the IRSTD task, utilizing WPMD
and a GAD to finely reconstruct masks. Meanwhile, SAM-
SPL [13] leverages the general semantic knowledge of SAM
to propose a unified self-prompt learning framework.
Different from the aforementioned architectures that focus
on the explicit learning of target features, some low-rank de-
composition studies [21]–[25] return to the physical essence of
infrared imaging and formulate infrared small target detection
as a background-target decoupling problem. Given an infrared
image (I), the scene is typically modeled as,
I = B + T + N
(1)
where B, T, and N denote the background, target, and noise
components, respectively. These methods estimate the back-
ground component via the deep model F(∗) and subsequently
detect targets through residual extraction,
ˆT = I −ˆB = I −F(I)
(2)
For example, RPCANet [24] unfolds the iterative solution of
Robust Principal Component Analysis (RPCA) into a network,
combining low-rank priors with deep features to improve inter-
pretability. LRRNet [25] adopts a compression-reconstruction-
subtraction paradigm, implicitly learning and generating low-
rank background representations with structure-aware capa-
bilities in a data-driven manner. Although LRRNet and our
work both involve background estimation, the motivations
and mechanisms are fundamentally different. LRRNet assumes
that targets in the infrared image are sparse components
over a low-rank background and performs target detection


3
through low-rank decomposition and residual extraction. In
contrast, our method formulates target removal as a generative
denoising problem, where diffusion models can be leveraged
to learn the underlying background distribution and generate
a target-free image. The generated background is not used
to directly locate targets but is instead provided as comple-
mentary contextual information to enhance target perception
within the detector. Briefly, LRRNet attempts to suppress the
background, whereas our method attempts to understand the
background. Furthermore, our generated background serves as
an auxiliary cue rather than a detection result, enabling the
proposed framework to be seamlessly integrated into various
existing detectors in a plug-and-play manner.
B. Diffusion-based Object Detection Models
As a class of generative models, Diffusion Models have
demonstrated powerful capabilities in the field of computer
vision. Their applications have transcended image genera-
tion and gradually expanded to discriminative tasks such as
detection and segmentation. The pioneering work that first
introduced diffusion models into object detection is Diffu-
sionDet [26], which regards the generation process of target
bounding boxes as a denoising process from a random noise
distribution to real target boxes. DiffusionAD [27] proposes
a noise normalization paradigm and a single-step denoising
mechanism for anomaly detection. It achieves unbiased re-
construction and accurate localization of abnormal regions
through a dual-branch network. Although both DiffusionAD
and our method employ diffusion-based reconstruction, their
underlying assumptions and objectives are fundamentally dif-
ferent. DiffusionAD assumes that anomalies lie outside the
normal data distribution and reconstructs normal samples by
removing semantic defects. In contrast, infrared small targets
are not semantic anomalies but extremely weak signals em-
bedded in cluttered backgrounds and sensor noise, often ex-
hibiting substantial distribution overlap with the background.
Consequently, our technical designs aim to efficiently purify
the backgrounds to enhance target-background contrast rather
than eliminating semantic abnormalities.
In recent years, diffusion models have been gradually in-
troduced into IRSTD tasks, and relevant research has mainly
developed along two paths: data augmentation and detection
architecture. In the data augmentation paradigm, aiming at
the lack of realism in traditional stitching methods, Diff-
Mosaic [28] designs a pixel coordination network combined
with latent diffusion priors for resampling. Gaussian Agnos-
tic Representation Learning [29] addresses the challenge of
data scarcity by proposing a Gaussian-driven non-uniform
quantization strategy and a two-stage diffusion generation
framework. In the detection and feature modeling paradigm,
DCFR-Net [30] innovatively embeds implicit neural represen-
tation into conditional diffusion models, learning continuous
high-resolution feature representations in a self-supervised
manner. ISTD-diff [31] models IRSTD as a conditional mask
generation task, directly outputting high-precision target masks
through an iterative denoising process. Unlike these methods
that use diffusion models to generate images with various
foreground and background, our method treats targets as noise-
like signals and utilizes diffusion models to remove them,
thereby generating target-free background images for the sub-
sequent detection. In addition, our reconstruction and detection
networks are not tightly coupled but are independently sepa-
rated. Therefore, it can be easily adapted to different detectors
according to the requirements of deployment scenarios.
III. METHODOLOGY
A. Overview
As aforesaid, the key idea of our DiffIRSTD is to enhance
the contrast of small targets in infrared images using generative
models, thereby improving the detector’s ability to perceive
them. Unlike previous approaches that use diffusion models to
either generate background-free target masks or as the detector
to localize targets, we take a different perspective of treating
small targets as noise-like perturbations in infrared images
(T ≈N). Based on this assumption, we thus exploit the
denoising property of diffusion models to learn the conditional
distribution of target-free backgrounds,
Bclean ∼p(B|I)
(3)
where Bclean denotes the generated target-free background
conditioned on the observed image (I). The generated back-
ground is not used for residual computation, but is instead
provided as contextual information to a detector (compared
with Eq. 2),
ˆT = D(I, Bclean)
(4)
where D(·) denotes the target detector. Consequently, existing
methods regard the background as interference to be sup-
pressed, whereas our method explicitly models the background
distribution and exploits the generated background as informa-
tive scene context. By comparing the original image with the
reconstructed target-free image, the detector can more easily
identify the location and precise boundaries of the target.

diffusion-based framework for infrared small target detection.
In the remainder of this section, we first explain the basic
principles of diffusion models, including the diffusion and
denoising processes (in Sec. III-B). Next, we describe how
diffusion models are utilized to denoise infrared images in
Sec. III-C, focusing on our one-step denoising strategy and
the proposed frequency-guided perceptual loss, which together
improve both the efficiency and quality of reconstructing
background images without targets. We then discuss how to
leverage the generated target-free infrared images to strengthen
the detector’s ability to perceive small targets (see Sec. III-D).
Subsequently, we present the workflow of obtaining synthetic
background images using morphological operations, which
serve as pseudo-labels for training the diffusion model (in
Sec. III-E). Finally, we detail the training and inference pro-
cedures of the proposed DiffIRSTD framework in Sec. III-F.

C. Reconstruction Network
Revisiting the imaging characteristics of infrared small
targets, we observe that although infrared targets are small and
lack distinct structural features, they often exhibit subtle local
intensity deviations from their surrounding background. These
deviations may be visually difficult to perceive due to complex
scenes, illumination conditions, and sensor noise. From a
statistical perspective, infrared small targets can therefore be
regarded as noise-like signals relative to the smooth back-
ground. Generating a target-free image is thus analogous to the
denoising process of a diffusion model, which progressively
removes noise (e.g., target) to recover a clean signal (e.g.,
background). Therefore, we adopt a diffusion model as the
reconstruction network to generate the background image.
Given an infrared image x ∈RH×W containing small
targets, we first apply Eq. 6 to gradually add Gaussian noise,
so that after t steps, the image becomes increasingly corrupted
and the targets are overwhelmed by noise. The resulting noisy
image is then fed into the reverse diffusion process, which iter-
atively denoises the observation according to Eq. 8. During this
reverse process, the model is encouraged to reconstruct a clean
background image while suppressing target-related “noise”. As
a result, we obtain a reconstructed image xB ∈RH×W that
approximates the scene without targets.
However, according to Eq. 8, the reverse diffusion process
progressively denoises the perturbed image from xt to x0

through multiple iterative steps. Each iteration corresponds
to a single denoising transition, and performing all t steps
during inference leads to substantial computational overhead.
This iterative sampling procedure significantly increases the
inference time and limits the practicality of diffusion models
in real-time infrared small target detection. Is it possible
to directly predict the background image x0 from a noisy
observation xt in a single step?
One-step Denoising Strategy. During the theoretical deriva-
tion of diffusion models [32], it has been proven that the clean
image x0 can be estimated directly from a noisy sample xt
by rearranging the forward formulation Eq. 8. However, this
direct estimation generally produces images of lower quality
compared to multi-step iterative sampling. The primary reason
is that when xt approaches pure Gaussian noise, the prediction
errors in ϵ may be amplified, leading to distortions and arti-
facts. Differently, our purpose is not to synthesize images from
random noise, but to remove small target “noise”. Since the
background of infrared images is typically smooth, recovering
a target-free background image from a noisy observation is
easier than reconstructing a natural image from pure noise.
Therefore, given an input image x and its noisy version xt

D. Detection Network
Similar to previous approaches [2], [3], [6], [10], the ob-
jective of our detection network is to accurately predict the
location mask of small targets. But, differently, benefiting
from the background prior provided by our reconstruction
network, the detection network can additionally receive the
corresponding target-free background image, so as to highlight
target regions through comparison analysis.
Specifically, we first take as input the input infrared image
x ∈RH×W and the reconstructed background image xB ∈
RH×W , and concatenate them along the channel dimension to
form a dual-channel input,
xdual = Concat(x, xB) ∈R2×H×W .
(16)
This dual-channel representation is thus fed into the detection
network to localize target regions by learning the discrepancy
between the two channels.
The detection network follows a standard U-Net archi-
tecture [33], consisting of an encoder, a decoder, and skip
connections. The encoder extracts multi-scale semantic fea-
tures through progressive down-sampling, while the decoder
gradually restores spatial resolution via up-sampling. Skip
connections fuse features from corresponding encoder and
decoder stages, helping preserve fine-grained spatial details
that are important for small target localization.
To further enhance feature discriminability, we adopt the
channel attention module, which is inserted after each encoder
block. The attention module aggregates contextual information

Discussion. In this paper, we intentionally adopt a simple U-
Net as the baseline detection network. This design choice
allows us to more clearly evaluate the effectiveness of the
proposed reconstruction network and directly support our
motivation. Importantly, our DiffIRSTD framework does not
depend on any specific detector architecture. The reconstruc-
tion network is designed as a plug-and-play component that
generates a clean background image. This reconstructed back-
ground can be concatenated with the original infrared image to
form a dual-channel representation, which can be readily used
by existing infrared small target detection models. As shown
in Tab. IX, integrating the reconstruction network with various
state-of-the-art detectors consistently improves their perfor-
mance. Furthermore, different from previous diffusion-based
approaches [17]–[19], the reconstruction and detection net-
works in our framework are loosely connected. This decoupled
design provides greater flexibility in practical deployment. De-
pending on computational constraints, the detection network
can be replaced with either lighter or more powerful models
without modifying the reconstruction network. Alternatively,
the reconstruction network can be deployed independently on
the cloud as a preprocessing step during inference.
E. Background Acquisition Strategy
Existing infrared small target datasets provide only infrared
images and their corresponding target masks, while ground-
truth background images are not available. However, such
background images are necessary to supervise the reconstruc-
tion process described in Sec. III-C. To address this issue, we
propose an inpainting-based background acquisition strategy
that removes target regions using the provided masks and
fills them to synthesize high-quality background images for
training. The workflow of this strategy is illustrated in Fig. 3.
More specifically, given an infrared image x and its target
mask
ˆ
M, we first apply a threshold of 0.5 to ensure that
the mask is strictly binary. Next, a morphological dilation
operation with a 5×5 kernel is applied to the mask to slightly
enlarge the target region. This step ensures that the dilated
mask ˆ
Mdilated fully covers the target boundaries and nearby
pixels, preventing residual target artifacts after inpainting.
Finally, we employ the OpenCV implementation of the Telea
inpainting algorithm [34] to fill the masked regions, with
the inpainting radius set to 3 pixels. The resulting image is
thus used as the pseudo-label background image, denoted by
ˆxB. This algorithm preserves structural continuity and texture
consistency in the surrounding background, making it suitable
for the reconstruction of small regions.
F. Training and Inference
Training. Our framework is trained in an end-to-end manner.
For the reconstruction network, it learns to generate high-
quality backgrounds under the joint supervisions of the pixel-
level reconstruction loss and frequency-guided perceptual loss,
Lrecon = Lmse(xB, ˆxB) + λ · Lhf(xB, ˆxB)
(18)
where λ is the balancing coefficient to control the importance
of the frequency-guided perceptual loss. Lmse denotes the
mean squared error between the predicted and ground-truth
images, and we empirically find it is better than the noise
supervision in Eq. 9. Here, the high-frequency loss Lhf plays
a more similar role to that of the classifier-based guidance,
guiding the denoising process to generate high-quality target-
free background images.
Meanwhile, the detection network learns to locate targets
and output segmentation masks under the supervision of
SoftIoU Loss and BCE Loss,
Ldetect = Lsoftiou(M, ˆ
M) + Lbce(M, ˆ
M)
(19)
where M and ˆ
M are the predicted and ground-truth masks.
The SoftIoU loss Lsoftiou optimizes the intersection over
union between target regions, while the BCE loss Lbce ensures
the pixel-level classification accuracy. The collaboration be-
tween two functions improves the quality of target localization.
Inference. During inference, the input image x first passes
through the reconstruction network to get the denoised target-
free image xB, and then both of them are fed into the detection
network to predict the target mask M.

Implementation Details. Our framework is implemented on
the PyTorch platform using one NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4090

models are jointly trained with a batch size of 8 for a total
of 800 epochs. We use the Adam optimizer with an initial
learning rate of 1 × 10−4, and the learning rate is adjusted
using a cosine annealing schedule. We evaluate models using
three widely-used metrics to provide a balanced assessment,
where Intersection over Union (IoU × 10−2) evaluates geo-

recall of targets, and false-alarm rate (Fa × 10−6) penalizes
false activations. These indicators jointly quantify localization
fidelity, recall, and specificity, offering a holistic assessment
of detection accuracy, precision, and reliability.

V. BROADER IMPACT AND LIMITATION
A. Broader Impact
In the field of infrared small target detection, this work
proposes an explicit decomposition paradigm of “background
reconstruction + target detection”. In critical scenarios such
as maritime search and rescue and missile early warning, this
work could improve the reliability of the system with a lower
false alarm rate and higher detection accuracy. Furthermore,
this work promotes the application of generative models in
the remote sensing field, utilizing diffusion models to achieve
“target erasure”. This idea can be migrated to other remote
sensing tasks (e.g., cloud removal, occluder elimination), ex-
panding a new perspective for the role of generative models
in visual tasks.
B. Limitations and Future Work
Although the framework proposed in this paper achieves
significant performance improvements in the infrared small
target detection task, it still has some limitations. In this
work, diffusion sampling is compressed to a single step, which
greatly improves the inference speed and reduces the inference
overhead, but in terms of computational cost, the reconstruc-
tion network still contains a large number of parameters.
For practical applications, a viable solution is to deploy the
reconstruction network independently in the cloud, serving as
a preprocessing step during the inference process. However, if
the entire framework needs to be deployed on edge devices,
further lightweight optimization is still required. An interesting
future work is to investigate more computationally efficient
reconstruction mechanisms and explore the applicability of
this framework to broader small-object detection scenarios.
VI. CONCLUSION
This paper presents DiffIRSTD, a diffusion-driven frame-
work for infrared small target detection that approaches the
problem from a reconstruction-oriented perspective. Rather
than directly inferring target regions, we first recover a
target-free background representation by suppressing target-
related signals through a diffusion-based denoising process.
The comparison differences between the reconstructed back-
ground and the original image thus naturally reveal candidate
target regions, providing informative guidance for detection.
Experiments on multiple benchmarks verify the effectiveness
and robustness of the proposed framework. Furthermore, the
modular design of the reconstruction component allows it to
be readily integrated with different detection architectures,
demonstrating strong adaptability and practical value.
