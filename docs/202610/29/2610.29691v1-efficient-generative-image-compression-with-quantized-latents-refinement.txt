Abstract
Generative image compression (GIC) delivers strong per-
ceptual quality at low bitrates. However, GIC methods with
strong perceptual quality often rely on pretrained diffusion
models with high latency, while recent lightweight GIC meth-
ods reduce latency but deliver lower perceptual quality. We
observe that, for lightweight GIC methods at low bitrates,
coarse deterministic quantization can collapse nearby en-
coder latents corresponding to different fine details to the
same point, making these details difficult to recover. Moti-
vated by this, we propose GR-GIC, an efficient GIC that
delivers competitive reconstruction performance with fast
inference. For better performance, we introduce Guided
Refinement (GR), which combines latent refinement guided
by entropy context with adaptive perturbations for training
regularization, jointly improving reconstruction at low bi-
trates. For fast inference, we introduce reorganized projected
residual vector quantization (P-RVQ), which equivalently
reorganizes residual updates into low-dimensional lookups
to expose greater parallelism and reduce sequential compu-
tation. Experiments on Kodak show that for performance,
compared with OneDC, GR-GIC achieves BD-rate savings
of 23.19%, 37.71%, 22.30%, and 22.14% for LPIPS, DISTS,
PSNR, and FID, respectively. For fast inference, the highly
optimized implementation of GR-GIC encodes and decodes
in 4.60 and 5.98 ms on an NVIDIA A100. Specifically, the re-
organized P-RVQ implementation achieves a 3.14× average
speedup over the original implementation under the same
FP32 setting. On an NVIDIA Jetson Orin NX, GR-GIC
achieves latency comparable to lightweight GIC methods.
1. Introduction
Generative image compression (GIC) provides strong per-
ceptual reconstruction at low bitrates. This is challenging
for traditional codecs and learned image codecs optimized
*Corresponding author.
primarily for pixel fidelity, which tend to smooth textures
and weaken fine details as the bitrate decreases [3, 24, 28,
40]. GIC addresses this limitation with adversarial genera-
tors [31, 33], discrete generative representations [34, 42], or
diffusion priors [8, 41, 46], whose learned priors can syn-
thesize plausible details that are not fully specified by the
compressed representation.
However, high-performing GIC methods often rely on
pretrained diffusion models, whose large parameter counts
and FLOPs hinder practical deployment [18, 36, 41, 46]. To
address this issue, recent work has explored lightweight GIC
methods with fewer parameters and faster inference [6, 7,
22], but their reconstruction performance still leaves room
for improvement compared with diffusion-based methods.
This leaves a trade-off between reconstruction performance
and fast inference.
We observe that, in lightweight GIC methods, images
with similar coarse content but different fine details can be
mapped to nearby points in the continuous latent space. At
low bitrates, coarse deterministic quantization can collapse
these distinct latents to the same quantized point, making
their fine detail differences unavailable from the quantized
representation, as illustrated in Figures 1a and 1b. By con-
trast, diffusion based methods introduce diversity through
Gaussian noise and use strong generative priors to synthesize
plausible details, providing a natural mechanism to compen-
sate for this uncertainty.
Motivated by this observation, we propose GR-GIC, an
efficient GIC that combines competitive reconstruction per-
formance with fast inference, as shown in Figure 1d. For
better performance, we introduce Guided Refinement (GR),
which uses entropy context to parameterize a Gaussian re-
finement distribution around the recovered latent. Its mean
shifts the recovered latent toward a refined center, while its
variance controls adaptive perturbations for training regular-
ization, jointly improving reconstruction at low bitrates. For
fast inference, we target projected residual vector quantiza-
tion (P-RVQ), which is a major encoder latency bottleneck in
EF-LIC [6]. We derive an algebraically equivalent reorgani-

Our contributions are summarized as follows:
• We introduce GR-GIC, an efficient GIC that combines
GR and reorganized P-RVQ for competitive reconstruction
performance and fast inference.
• GR uses entropy context to parameterize an adaptive refine-
ment distribution around quantized latents. Reorganized
P-RVQ replaces serial high dimensional residual updates
with low dimensional lookups, reducing sequential com-
putation and inference latency.
• For performance, experiments demonstrate competitive
results across multiple reconstruction metrics. For fast
inference, the highly optimized implementation achieves
low latency on both GPU and edge platforms.
2. Related Work
Diffusion-based generative image compression.
Diffu-
sion models provide strong generative priors for reconstruct-
ing details that are not fully specified by compressed rep-
resentations [8, 43]. Recent GIC methods increasingly use
pretrained diffusion models and improve their efficiency
through fewer diffusion steps, one-step reconstruction, or
lighter architectures [18, 22, 29, 41, 46]. Despite these ad-
vances, their model size and computation remain substantial
for efficient deployment.
VAE-based generative image compression.
VAE-based
GIC provides a more efficient alternative through adver-
sarial priors, improved latent representations, and vector
quantization [20, 23, 26, 31, 33, 42]. Recent work further
develops lightweight GIC. ProGIC combines a depthwise
convolutional backbone with RVQ [7], while EF-LIC models
correlations among latents in the representation domain [6].
GR-GIC builds on EF-LIC, introducing GR for better recon-
struction and reorganizing P-RVQ to move high dimensional
reconstruction outside the sequential search path.
Latent refinement after quantization.
Prior LIC methods
have compensated quantization loss through latent residual
prediction, post-quantization filtering, and adaptive quan-
tization or correction [15, 16, 32, 48]. Recent work also
studies latent and noise compensation [44]. These methods
mainly perform deterministic correction using decoder con-
text, rely on guidance from the encoder for content-adaptive
compensation, or model stochastic compensation without
adapting it to context. In contrast, GR uses entropy context
already available at the decoder to parameterize a refinement
distribution that adapts to spatial location and image content,

without additional payload, while adaptive perturbations pro-
vide training regularization for generative reconstruction.
3. Method
3.1. Overview
As illustrated in Figures 1a and 1b, at low bitrates, coarse de-
terministic quantization can collapse nearby encoder latents
corresponding to different fine details to the same quantized
point, making these details difficult to recover in lightweight
GIC. GR-GIC builds on EF-LIC [6], whose context model
provides the recovered latent and entropy context without ad-
ditional payload. GR uses the entropy context to parameter-
ize a Gaussian refinement distribution around the recovered
latent, with adaptive perturbations providing latent noise
regularization during training. GR-GIC retains the analysis
transform, hyperprior, groupwise context, and P-RVQ struc-
ture of EF-LIC, adds a GR head before synthesis as shown
in Figure 2, and reorganizes P-RVQ execution in Figure 3.

3.2. Guided Refinement of Quantized Latents

we introduce Guided Refinement (GR) after RVQ recovery.

from this distribution introduces adaptive perturbations that
provide latent noise regularization.

4. Experiments
4.1. Experimental Setup
Implementation Details.
Our implementation largely fol-
lows EF-LIC [6], with a GR head added before synthesis and
P-RVQ execution reorganized as described above. Section B
provides the complete architecture and codebook settings.
For fast inference, the highly optimized implementation fur-
ther uses fused CUDA operators following DCVC-UF [27].
Training Details.
Each operating point is trained on Ima-
geNet [9] in two 1M-iteration stages. Stage one samples
1% per epoch with 256 × 256 random crops at a learn-
ing rate of 10−4. Stage two samples 10% of images ex-
ceeding 512 pixels in both dimensions with 512×512 ran-
dom crops at 10−5. Both stages apply random horizon-
tal flipping and use AdamW [30] with weight decay 10−4,
(β1, β2) = (0.9, 0.999), batch size 16, and one NVIDIA
A100. Each training crop uses one standard Gaussian draw
ϵ ∼N(0, I) to sample the refinement distribution condi-
tioned on entropy context and estimate the expected image-
space objective, which includes a generative adversarial net-
work (GAN) term:

Metrics are LPIPS with a Visual Geometry

Compared Methods.
The pretrained diffusion group com-
prises ResULIC, StableCodec, RDEIC, OneDC, OSCAR,

measures. CoD-Lite uses our measurements of its official
code, with its paper profile in Sec. C.3.

4.4. Edge-Device Deployment
We evaluate GR-GIC on a 16 GB NVIDIA Jetson Orin NX.
Among the compared methods, GR-GIC achieves the low-
est decoding latency at all four resolutions and the lowest
encoding latency from 1080p to 4K in Tab. 3. Measurements
use MAXN SUPER mode, batch size one, and synchro-
nized wall-clock timing. GR-GIC remains deployable at 4K,
where OneDC and AEIC run out of memory and CoD-Lite
decoding exceeds 10 seconds. These results demonstrate
effective low-latency deployment on edge hardware.
5. Limitations
Our evaluation spans 0.0356 to 0.1426 BPP, with the lowest
point below the 0.05 BPP ultra-low bitrate threshold [46, 47].
Extending lightweight GIC to even lower bitrates requires
greater decoder generative capacity as less fine detail is
transmitted. Future work can strengthen lightweight priors
and synthesis capacity while retaining fast inference. Our
latency results characterize the complete deployed codec,
including P-RVQ reorganization and CUDA acceleration.
We do not claim the fastest backbone-only FP32 inference
under identical backends. Comparisons retain official or
released implementations and available acceleration paths,
reflecting system-level performance.
6. Conclusion
We introduced GR-GIC, an efficient GIC combining GR
and reorganized P-RVQ for competitive reconstruction per-
formance and fast inference. GR refines quantized latents
using entropy context, while adaptive perturbations provide
latent noise regularization during training. Reorganized P-
RVQ replaces serial high dimensional residual updates with
low dimensional lookups to reduce sequential computation.
Experiments across four datasets show consistent gains in
LPIPS, DISTS, and FID over OneDC, together with fast
inference on both an NVIDIA A100 and Jetson Orin NX.

A.3. Implementation and Runtime Evaluation
CUDA implementation.
Checkpoint conversion materi-
alizes the stacked search projection, codeword norms, code-
book transposes, reconstruction tables, and the cross stage
lookup tables in Equation (12). At runtime, the source pro-
jections in Equation (14) are evaluated with one matrix mul-
tiplication.
A fused CUDA kernel then processes the ordered P-RVQ
stages. Candidate dot products, distance evaluation, parallel
reduction for index selection, table lookup, and updates to
the later low dimensional states are performed within one
kernel launch. The kernel preserves the ordered greedy
decisions while parallelizing candidate evaluation and the
updates to future search states. This avoids separate search
launches at every stage and avoids materializing complete
distance and update tensors.
When reconstruction is required, a second kernel gathers
the selected high dimensional vectors, sums them, and writes
the reconstructed output. During decompression, these re-
construction operations are further combined with the sur-
rounding codec operations. This codec integration lies out-
side the isolated P-RVQ timing reported below.
Benchmark protocol.
We evaluate the original Torch
FP32 implementation, the reorganized Torch implementa-
tions in FP32 and FP16, and the CUDA FP16 implemen-
tation on one NVIDIA A100 with batch size one. All im-
plementations use weights converted from the same check-
points.
We evaluate M ∈{1, 2, 3, 4} at latent sizes correspond-
ing to 512 × 768, 1080p, 2K, and 4K images, giving 16
settings in total. Each latency is the median of 100 synchro-
nized repetitions after 20 warmup runs.
The reported latency isolates P-RVQ execution. Complete
codec latency is reported in Tabs. 1, 3 and 10.
Runtime results.
Under the same Torch FP32 backend and
precision, the reorganized P-RVQ achieves a 3.14× average
speedup over the original implementation across the 16 eval-
uated settings. The reorganized Torch FP16 implementation
achieves a 2.97× average speedup. These matched Torch
results isolate the effect of the algebraic reorganization and
numerical precision.
The CUDA FP16 implementation further increases the
average speedup to 7.15× relative to the original FP32 im-
plementation. This number includes the algebraic reorgani-
zation, FP16 execution, and fused CUDA kernels. Table 4
reports the latency and speedup for every resolution and
P-RVQ depth.
At the highest evaluated Kodak rate, the P-RVQ latency
during compression is 11.2824 ms for the original FP32
implementation, 3.2435 ms for the reorganized Torch FP32
implementation, and 0.6001 ms for the CUDA FP16 im-
plementation. The matched FP32 reorganization gives a
3.48× speedup at this operating point. The CUDA FP16
implementation gives an 18.80× speedup.
For complete encoder attribution, we construct an original
P-RVQ reference by restoring the latency difference between
the original and reorganized FP32 P-RVQ to the measured
Torch FP32 encoder. Using the reduction from this reference
to the final CUDA FP16 encoder as a common denominator,
the algebraic reorganization accounts for 23.54% of the total
encoder latency reduction. The complete P-RVQ acceler-
ation, including the CUDA implementation, accounts for
31.28%.
Numerical consistency.
The original and reorganized
Torch FP32 implementations select identical indices, with a
maximum absolute reconstruction difference of 4.77×10−7.

B.2. Fixed-Width Index Packing
Each power of two codebook in Tab. 6 uses a fixed index
width according to the size of its quantization alphabet [17].
The hyper latent uses 10 bits per index, while the four main
latent groups use 10, 9, 8, and 7 bits.
The multistage structure and low dimensional search
spaces follow the P-RVQ configuration of EF-LIC [6].
Stored indices are flattened in (stage, batch, height, width)
order and written with the least significant bit first. The five
streams are ordered as Qz →Q1 →Q2 →Q3 →Q4.
A standalone bitstream stores the original image height
and width as two unsigned 16 bit integers, followed by the
five index streams. No additional side information is trans-
mitted. We report payload BPP and exclude this 4 byte image
size header. For a 512×768 image, the header contributes

B.3. Padding and Cropping
The encoder pads the input on the right and bottom so that
both spatial dimensions are multiples of 32. The hyper anal-
ysis path additionally pads the main latent to a multiple of
four, while the main latent path retains its original logical
shape. The quadtree groups, main latent P-RVQ modules,
GR head, and synthesis transform therefore operate on this
original latent shape. The decoder removes the correspond-
ing padding and returns the reconstruction at the original
image size.

C. Evaluation Protocol
C.1. Datasets and Objective Evaluation
Kodak, Tecnick, DIV2K validation, and CLIC 2020 Profes-
sional test contain 24, 100, 100, and 250 images, respectively.
All reconstructed images are evaluated at their original image
resolution.
Objective evaluation.
We use standardized objective met-
rics as the primary quantitative evaluation. Subjective user
studies can provide complementary perceptual evidence, but
their results depend on the participant population, viewing
conditions, presentation protocol, and selected test images,
and naturally exhibit observer variability. These factors
make subjective scores less suitable for reproducible compar-
isons across many methods, datasets, and operating points.
Standardized objective metrics instead provide repeatable
measurements under a fixed evaluation protocol.
This practice is also common in generative image com-
pression.
Representative methods including MS-ILLM,
GLC, Control-GIC, DLF, OneDC, OSCAR, and RDVQ pri-
marily evaluate reconstruction using objective metrics such
as LPIPS, DISTS, and FID without reporting a subjective
user study [18, 20, 23, 26, 33, 41, 42]. We therefore use
LPIPS and DISTS to measure reference-based perceptual
similarity, PSNR to measure pixel fidelity, and FID to mea-
sure distribution-level alignment, together with qualitative
reconstructions for visual comparison.
LPIPS is computed with pretrained VGG features after
15


mapping RGB inputs to [−1, 1]. DISTS receives RGB inputs
in [0, 1], and PSNR is computed in RGB.
FID.
Following the patch evaluation protocol used for low-
rate generative image compression [20], we compute FID
from local image patches. Reference and reconstructed RGB
images in [0, 1] are rounded to unsigned 8-bit values and
passed to the TorchMetrics implementation of FID [10, 19].
Kodak uses 64 × 64 patches, while Tecnick, DIV2K, and
CLIC 2020 use 256 × 256 patches. We extract one non-
overlapping patch grid and a second grid shifted by half a
patch along both spatial axes. This increases the number of
Inception samples and improves the stability of distribution-
level evaluation on the 24-image Kodak set. All compared
methods use the same FID pipeline.
BD-rate.

rate difference between two rate-quality curves at matched

C.2. Runtime and Model Cost Protocol
All A100 codec runtimes use batch size one on one NVIDIA
A100 GPU. For GR-GIC, CPU payload processing is per-
formed on an AMD EPYC 7763.
The latency comparison retains the numerical precision,
inference backend, and acceleration path provided by each
available implementation. The reported values therefore
characterize realized system latency rather than architecture-
only speed under an identical backend. EF-LIC and ProGIC
follow their published FP32 evaluation settings [6, 7], while
the remaining methods retain their released inference set-
tings.
The highly optimized implementation of GR-GIC com-
bines reorganized P-RVQ with fused CUDA operators fol-
lowing DCVC-UF [27]. It uses FP16 execution and CUDA
Graphs together with custom kernels for quantization and
reconstruction. The remaining convolution operations use
optimized ATen and cuDNN kernels.
The reported encoding and decoding times cover the com-
plete codec path. Encoding includes index generation, trans-
fer to the CPU, and bitstream packing. Decoding includes
bitstream unpacking, transfer to the GPU, index recovery,
one GR realization, and image synthesis. Timing starts after
model loading, input construction, and CUDA Graph cap-
ture. We use two warmup runs followed by ten synchronized
repetitions.
For GR-GIC, the reported latency and model cost are
averaged over four independently trained operating points at
0.0356, 0.0713, 0.1069, and 0.1426 BPP. The same averag-
ing convention is used for the A100 and Jetson results.
Parameter accounting across operating points.
We re-
port the parameter count of the complete codec deployed
at one evaluated operating point. Each of the four inde-
pendently trained GR-GIC checkpoints contains 79.389M
parameters. The reported 79.389M therefore represents one
deployed model rather than the combined storage of all four
checkpoints.
This convention is also applied to methods that use sepa-
rate checkpoints for different target rates. Among the com-
pared methods, ResULIC, StableCodec, RDEIC, OneDC,
MS-ILLM, DLF, DiT-IC, AEIC, and CoD-Lite use sepa-
rately trained or fine-tuned model weights for different oper-
ating points [22, 25, 29, 33, 36, 41, 42, 46, 47].
We use the same parameter accounting rule for both types
of codec. The tables report the complete model required
for the evaluated operating point and do not sum multiple
checkpoints across the rate curve. The storage required to
retain all supported operating points is a separate deployment
property from the parameter count of one deployed codec.
FLOPs are measured for one encoder and decoder pass
with batch size one on the corresponding PyTorch FP32 path
under the profiler-supported operator scope. We count one
multiply-accumulate operation as two FLOPs. The same
pass definition and arithmetic convention are used for all
methods. Peak memory denotes the maximum allocated
GPU memory including the loaded model and active codec
buffers.

Except where noted, we evaluate released checkpoints on
the same NVIDIA A100 with batch size one. Each method
follows the coding and inference procedure provided by its
official implementation.

C.4. GR Ablation Protocol
The configurations in Tab. 2 are trained independently from
scratch at each bitrate. All configurations use the same train-
ing data, loss terms and weights, AdamW settings, learning-
rate schedule, and update budget. Each ablation model is
trained for 1M iterations. The main operating-point results
use separately trained models with the complete 2M iteration
schedule.
M0 bypasses GR and directly synthesizes from the recov-
ered quantized latent. It retains the direct synthesis path of
the EF-LIC backbone and provides a reference under the
same training conditions used for the GR configurations.
M1 predicts an offset from the recovered latent. M2 ad-
ditionally uses entropy context for deterministic refinement.
M3 introduces Gaussian perturbation with one learned scale
for each channel, shared across spatial locations. M4 further
adapts the perturbation scale to spatial location and image
content.
Each crop for M3 and M4 uses one standard Gaussian
draw to estimate the expected image objective. M2 and M3
have the same reported parameter count and forward FLOPs
as M4. They provide matched references for evaluating
stochastic perturbation and its adaptation to image content
and spatial location.
C.5. Stochastic Evaluation Protocol
The main results use a fixed decoder seed of 1926 and recon-
struct each bitstream once. Stochastic sampling is applied
only after deterministic RVQ index recovery, so the trans-
mitted indices and payload do not depend on the decoder
seed.
Inference perturbation ablation.

Decoder seed stability.
To measure sensitivity to the sam-
