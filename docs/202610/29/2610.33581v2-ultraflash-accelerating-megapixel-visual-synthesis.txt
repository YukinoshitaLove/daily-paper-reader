Abstract
Megapixel visual synthesis with latent diffusion models requires operating far
beyond the resolution at which these models are trained. Cascade refinement is
the most successful approach to handling this problem: it upgrades a base image
at a regular resolution to the target resolution via multiple cascade upsampling
steps, each involving a resolution upgrade traversed through pixel space and a
denoising-based refinement in latent space. Although denoising is often treated
as the main cost, we identify an underexplored source of overhead: repeatedly
moving representations through the VAE. We refer to these steps as pixel-space
transitions: intermediate latents are decoded to RGB, resized, and re-encoded be-
fore refinement, while final latents are decoded patch by patch with dense overlap
to suppress artifacts. We present UltraFlash, which shifts the focus of acceleration
from denoising alone to the VAE transitions around it. Its first component, Latent
Hyperloop, replaces the intermediate RGB round trip with a learned latent-space
shortcut that approximates the same transformation while avoiding repeated VAE
calls for decoding and encoding. Its second component, Sparse Patch-based De-
coding, makes final reconstruction efficient by tracing patch seams to inconsistent
GroupNorm statistics and reusing cached statistics across patches, enabling sparse
VAE decoding with much smaller overlap. Integrated into the current state-of-the-
art baseline, UltraFlash reduces 4K generation latency by ∼2× while improving
image quality, demonstrating that transition-level optimization can advance both
efficiency and fidelity. More broadly, these transition costs recur across megapixel
synthesis pipelines, suggesting that optimizing pixel-space transitions is an effective
route to fast, high-fidelity visual synthesis.

Introduction
Latent diffusion models have greatly advanced visual synthesis, but their native training resolution
remains a practical boundary. Although applications increasingly require 2K, 4K, and larger outputs,
models trained around 512 × 512 or 1024 × 1024 often produce repeated objects, distorted layouts,
or unstable textures when directly evaluated on larger canvases. Fine-tuning on high-resolution
data is a natural remedy, but it is difficult to scale: diverse megapixel-scale datasets are limited,
training at such resolutions is expensive, and the adapted model often becomes specialized to a fixed
target resolution [33, 35]. Existing alternatives therefore rely on inference-time strategies that reuse
pretrained models. Direct extrapolation methods modify inference on enlarged latents, but can suffer
from memory cost and structural instability at extreme resolutions. Cascade refinement methods
instead generate at a reliable native resolution and progressively construct larger images through
refinement stages [6, 9, 21, 29, 36]. Representative systems differ in how they refine enlarged latents:
DemoFusion uses progressive patch-based refinement, FouriScale and FreeScale modify frequency
or scale behavior, HiWave uses structure-preserving inversion, and PixelRush [13] accelerates the
cascade with one-step diffusion refinement. Among these, PixelRush currently provides the strongest
speed–quality trade-off, making it the most relevant stress test for remaining non-denoising overhead.
We therefore use PixelRush as the main instantiation of UltraFlash, while targeting VAE-heavy
pixel-space transitions that are shared by the broader cascade family.
Most acceleration efforts for megapixel synthesis focus on denoising or refinement. This is natural,
since diffusion sampling is expensive and larger spatial sizes increase the cost of each denoising
operation. However, we find that this view is incomplete. Modern cascade pipelines also spend
substantial time repeatedly moving representations through the VAE. We call these operations pixel-
space transitions: steps where a latent representation is converted to RGB or where RGB is converted
back to a latent representation. These transitions appear in two places. First, intermediate latents are
often decoded to RGB, resized, and re-encoded before refinement. Second, the final latent must be
decoded to RGB, but at megapixel scale this is typically done patch by patch with dense overlap to
suppress artifacts. As a result, a significant fraction of runtime is spent not on denoising itself, but on
VAE-heavy transitions around denoising.
This observation exposes an underexplored source of overhead. The two transitions have different
roles and therefore require different solutions. The intermediate RGB round trip is avoidable: it is
used as a convenient way to transform a latent before refinement, but it need not be performed through
pixels. The final transition is unavoidable because the output image must be RGB, but its dense patch
implementation is inefficient. Thus, the design goal is to replace the avoidable intermediate VAE
round trip and make the final VAE decoding sparse without introducing seams.
We present UltraFlash, which shifts the focus of acceleration from denoising alone to the VAE
transitions around it. UltraFlashcontains two components. First, Latent Hyperloop replaces the
intermediate RGB round trip with a learned latent-space shortcut. It approximates the decode–resize–
encode transformation while avoiding repeated VAE calls for decoding and encoding. Second, Sparse
Patch-based Decoding accelerates final reconstruction. We trace sparse patch-decoding artifacts
to inconsistent GroupNorm statistics across independently decoded patches. Sparse Patch-based
Decoding fixes this by caching normalization statistics from reference patches and reusing them
across all patches, enabling sparse VAE decoding with much smaller overlap.
Integrated into the current state-of-the-art cascade baseline [13], UltraFlash reduces 4K generation
latency by 2× while improving image quality. This result shows that VAE transitions remain a
substantial optimization spot even in highly optimized megapixel synthesis systems. More broadly,
RGB round trips and dense patch-wise VAE decoding appear across cascade pipelines, suggesting
that optimizing pixel-space transitions is an effective route to faster, high-fidelity visual synthesis.
Our contributions are:
• We identify repeated VAE-heavy pixel-space transitions as an underexplored source of
overhead in megapixel visual synthesis, beyond denoising and refinement.
• We introduce Latent Hyperloop, a learned latent-space shortcut that replaces the intermedi-
ate decode–resize–encode round trip while avoiding repeated VAE decoding and encoding.
• We introduce Sparse Patch-based Decoding, which enables sparse VAE decoding by
sharing cached normalization statistics across independently decoded patches.

Related Work
Megapixel Visual Synthesis. Scaling diffusion models beyond their native training resolution has
been explored through several routes. One route trains or adapts models for native high-resolution
generation, such as SANA, HunyuanImage, and other dedicated 2K/4K generators [33, 28]. These
methods can achieve strong quality, but they require high-resolution data, model adaptation, or
dedicated training. A second route directly extrapolates pretrained models to larger canvases through
inference-time modifications, such as convolutional dilation [7], frequency or scale fusion [9], flow-
aligned guidance [2], or positional extrapolation for FLUX-based models [10]. These methods avoid
retraining, but can suffer from memory cost, structural instability, or unnatural textures at extreme
resolutions [17]. A third route uses cascade or region-based refinement, synthesizing native-resolution
windows and merging them into a larger image through average blending [1, 6], DDIM inversion [29],
or few-step refinement [13]. This cascade line has progressed from multi-step patch refinement in
DemoFusion to structure-preserving inversion in HiWave, and most recently to PixelRush, which uses
one-step Turbo refinement to deliver the strongest speed–quality trade-off among cascade methods.
Our work focuses on the cascade setting because it reuses pretrained models in their reliable operating
regime while supporting megapixel outputs. We use PixelRush as the main experimental instantiation
because it is already optimized for denoising speed; improving it therefore tests whether VAE
transition overhead remains a meaningful bottleneck after the dominant sampling cost has been
compressed. Rather than introducing another refinement strategy, UltraFlash optimizes the VAE-
heavy transitions surrounding refinement: intermediate RGB round trips that prepare refinement
inputs, and final patch-wise VAE decoding that reconstructs the output image.
Pixel-Space Transitions. Latent diffusion models use a VAE to move between compressed latents
and RGB images [22]. In megapixel synthesis, this creates two transition costs. First, intermediate
latents are often decoded to RGB, resized, and re-encoded before refinement. This route is stable and
avoids artifacts from naive latent manipulation [21, 29], but requires an expensive decode–encode
round trip. Because the VAE encoder is nonlinear, ordinary latent interpolation is not that round
trip. FAM Diffusion [34] compensates with learned frequency and attention modulation over the
whole latent, and it changes the denoiser. Latent Hyperloop is a patch-wise shortcut for the same
decode–resize–encode transformation and leaves the denoiser unchanged.
Second, the final latent must be decoded to RGB. Since full-image VAE decoding is memory-
intensive at megapixel scale, pipelines decode overlapping patches and blend them. Large overlaps
hide seams but repeat computation; small overlaps reveal boundary artifacts. We identify inconsistent
GroupNorm statistics [32] across patches as a key cause, and use Sparse Patch-based Decoding to
share cached statistics across patches, enabling sparse reconstruction with much smaller overlap.
Super-Resolution. Super-resolution methods reconstruct high-resolution images from low-resolution
inputs [4, 5, 30]. Diffusion-based super-resolution methods [14, 31] further improve perceptual
quality, but are typically trained for restoration. Megapixel visual synthesis differs from restoration:
the input is a generated intermediate representation, and the goal is coherent visual synthesis rather
than recovering a unique high-resolution target [13]. Thus, although super-resolution and megapixel
synthesis both involve scale expansion, they address different problem settings and require different
evaluation criteria.

Accelerating Megapixel Visual Synthesis
We present UltraFlash, which shifts acceleration from denoising alone to the VAE-heavy pixel-space
transitions that surround it. In cascade megapixel synthesis, these transitions arise twice: intermediate
latents undergo an RGB round trip before refinement, while final RGB decoding must be performed
patch by patch due to memory constraints.
UltraFlash handles these two roles differently: Latent Hyperloop (Sec. 4.1) replaces the avoid-
able intermediate RGB round trip, while Sparse Patch-based Decoding (Sec. 4.2) accelerates the
unavoidable final decoding. An overview is shown in Fig. 2.
4.1
Latent Hyperloop: Bypassing Intermediate RGB Round Trips
Motivation. Many cascade refinement pipelines [6, 21, 29, 13] initialize each refinement stage
through a VAE-heavy RGB round trip. Given a latent zr
0, the pipeline decodes it to RGB, changes its
spatial scale, and re-encodes the resized image back into latent space:
UPpixel(zr
0) = E(Interpolate(D(zr
0), 2)) ,
(3)
where D and E denote the VAE decoder and encoder, respectively. The appeal of this RGB round trip
is that resizing in pixel space is simple and well-defined. Its drawback is computational cost: each
transfer invokes both the VAE decoder and encoder. In our main instantiation with the state-of-the-art
cascade method [13], this transition accounts for approximately 30–40% of cascade-stage runtime.
The challenge is that the RGB round trip cannot be replaced by ordinary latent interpolation. Since
the VAE encoder is nonlinear, spatial resizing in latent space is not equivalent to resizing in RGB and
then encoding the result: Interpolate(z, 2)̸ = E(Interpolate(D(z), 2)). Thus, we replace generic
latent interpolation with a learned latent-space shortcut that approximates the decode–resize–encode
transformation while avoiding the VAE decode–encode computation.

Once trained, Hϕ replaces the decode–interpolate–encode route during intermediate transfer. A
good Latent Hyperloop Hϕ must satisfy the following conditions: (1) Accurate in replicating the
upsampling operator UPpixel without going through pixel space, (2) Efficient in both memory usage
and computation, causing minimal latency, and (3) General to work at every cascade step. These
criteria are challenging, requiring novel designs in data flow, network structure, training data, and
training objectives. We will discuss below our proposals to handle each mentioned aspect.
Context-Expanded Patching. To make Hϕ general and work at every cascade step regardless of
input resolution, we design it to process patch-by-patch, using fixed resolutions for the input and
output latent patches. Since the follow-up refinement step also processes overlapping patches, a
convenient design is to use the same patch configuration in these two steps: the output patches of
Hϕ should have the same resolution and location as the ones used in the refinement. With that
configuration, the patches produced by Hϕ can be sent for processing at the refinement step directly,
skipping the operation to collect and aggregate them to form the full latent output ¯z4r
0 .
For each target patch in ¯z4r
0 , a natural input to Hϕ is the coordinate-matched source patch in zr
0.
However, this crop alone is insufficient. The decode–resize–encode route approximated by Hϕ
contains convolutional VAE layers, so the target patch depends not only on the aligned source region
but also on its surrounding context. We therefore expand each source crop by p context pixels on
every side before feeding it to Hϕ, so that a source patch of side length s becomes an input of
side length s + 2p; we use s = 64 and p = 8, i.e., 80 × 80 inputs for 128 × 128 target patches.
This context-expanded design captures the local dependencies of the original RGB round trip while
preserving patch-wise processing. Its necessity is confirmed by the ablation on p in Appendix B.2.
Lightweight Spatial Prior. Latent Hyperloop Hϕ needs to double the resolution of the input latent
zr

denoted as D(1), which already implements latent upsampling and has been well trained on massive
data. This pretrained block serves as a spatial prior, effectively guiding Hϕ’s spatial upsampling
operation. However, this block often produces feature maps with a different number of channels and
unaligned geometry compared to the desired output z4r

the number of channels and refine the geometry of the produced latent output.
In summary, our proposed Latent Hyperloop Hϕ consists of two components: a frozen VAE
decoding block D(1) and a trainable projection head hψ. Because context-expanded patching uses
an input crop larger than the coordinate-matched source region, the spatial size entering Hϕ is larger
than the nominal source patch size. We therefore adjust the padding in D(1) and the early layers of
hψ so that the output matches the required target patch size, requiring no major architectural change.

Latent Reconstruction (Lrec). A straightforward objective is latent reconstruction, implemented by
an ℓ1 reconstruction loss:
Lrec = ∥zout −ˆzout∥1.
(5)
While ℓ2 is a common choice, it is less effective for latent-space reconstruction, as noted in recent
works [16, 37, 19]. The ℓ1 objective provides better training stability and preserves the structural
sparsity of the upsampled latent.
Attention Alignment (Latten). Reconstruction loss alone is insufficient. Since the loss is operated
directly on the latent manifold, a small numerical distance error does not necessarily guarantee that
two latents are perceptually or semantically equivalent. Furthermore, by treating each latent pixel
independently, a simple reconstruction objective often tolerates subtle misalignments. Both lead to a
loss of fine-grained structure in the decoded image. In principle, a GAN loss could help, but it often
makes optimization substantially more sensitive to hyperparameters and empirically less stable.
Motivated by X2I [18], we instead seek supervision signals from attention responses of the refiner
model when processing zout and ˆzout, which are stable and sensitive to fine structure. Self-attention
maps in the refiner encode rich interactions across spatial locations in the latent, and thus can magnify
small deviations into larger, more detectable discrepancies in the network’s internal responses. By
monitoring the attention misalignment, we can penalize structured mismatches between zout and
ˆzout that Lrec may underweight.

4.2
Sparse Patch-based Decoding: Accelerating Final RGB Decoding
While Latent Hyperloop removes avoidable intermediate RGB round trips, the final pixel-space
transition is unavoidable: after refinement, the latent zR
0 must be decoded into RGB. At megapixel
scale, however, full-latent VAE decoding is often memory-prohibitive, so practical synthesis pipelines
decode the latent patch by patch and blend overlapping regions [1, 6, 29, 13]. Dense overlap helps
suppress seams, but it also repeats VAE computation over many regions. A natural acceleration is to
reduce the overlap, but naive sparse decoding introduces visible boundary artifacts, including color
shifts, brightness inconsistencies, and texture discontinuities at patch boundaries, as shown in Fig. 3.
Why Sparse Decoding Fails. We trace the root cause of sparse-decoding artifacts to the GroupNorm
(GN) layers in the VAE decoder. GroupNorm [32] normalizes activations within each group of
channels using statistics computed from the current input.
When the full image is decoded in a single pass, the mean µ and variance σ2 are computed over
the entire spatial extent, producing globally consistent normalization. However, when decoding
individual patches, each patch computes its own µ(j) and σ2(j) based only on its local spatial content.
Patches with different spatial content (e.g., a bright sky vs. a dark ground) yield substantially different
statistics. This causes the same latent values in overlapping regions to be decoded to different pixel
values depending on which patch they belong to, producing visible seams even after blending.

Main Results on Fast Cascade Inference
Table 1 shows that adding our transition operators to the fast cascade improves both efficiency
and fidelity. At 2K, the transition overhead is relatively small because the cascade contains fewer
high-resolution patches, yet the gains remain consistent: UltraFlash-SD reduces runtime from 3.17s
to 2.78s while improving FID from 52.56 to 50.02, and UltraFlash-XL achieves the best 2K FID
of 49.86. The benefit becomes more pronounced at 4K, where transition costs compound across
cascade stages and final VAE decoding. UltraFlash-SD reduces latency from 16.37s to 8.04s relative

configuration shows the same trend, reducing latency from 17.11s to 9.61s and improving FID from

transition overhead without sacrificing visual quality. Detailed ablations of both components are
provided in Appendix B.

Conclusion
We presented UltraFlash, a transition-efficient method for megapixel visual synthesis. Instead of only
accelerating denoising, UltraFlash targets VAE-heavy pixel-space transitions in cascade pipelines.
Latent Hyperloop replaces the intermediate decode–resize–encode round trip with a learned latent
shortcut, while Sparse Patch-based Decoding accelerates final RGB reconstruction by sharing
cached GroupNorm statistics across patches. Integrated into a state-of-the-art cascade baseline,
UltraFlash reduces 4K latency by 2× while improving image quality, showing that pixel-space
transitions are an effective target for improving the speed–quality trade-off of megapixel synthesis.

A.1
Implementation Details
Base Generation. Following previous works, we use SDXL as the default base model to generate
images at its native 1024 × 1024 resolution with a classifier-free guidance scale of 7.5. The reverse
diffusion process uses deterministic DDIM sampling (η = 0).
Cascade Upsampling. Our pipeline operates in a cascaded manner, doubling the spatial resolution at
each level. For example, generating an 8192 × 8192 image proceeds as 1024 →2048 →4096 →
8192, with refinement applied at each level. At each cascade level, the Latent Hyperloop adapter
upsamples the latent by 2×. Since the adapter backbone is lightweight (∼4.8M parameters), the
computational overhead of processing overlapping regions is negligible, so we use a generous 50%
overlap for the upsampling stage to ensure smooth transitions.

the upsampled latent is tiled into patches with 50% overlap along both spatial dimensions. In sparse
patching, we reduce the total number of patches for faster inference. Given the target number of
patches per row (or column) as e, the fixed patch window size w, and the upsampling factor relative
to the original patch window size u, we need to compute the overlap size for interior patches o and
the last patch olast that satisfy:

A.3
Latent Hyperloop
We adopt a SwinIR-based architecture for the Latent Hyperloop (LH) and incorporate structural priors
from the VAE decoder to guide the learned upsampling. With SDXL-Turbo refiner, LH accepts latent
patch inputs with resolution 80 × 80, i.e., a 64 × 64 source crop expanded by p = 8 context pixels on
each side (input side length s+2p with s = 64), to generate upsampled latent patches with resolution
128 × 128. With SD-Turbo, while we could use smaller input and output patch sizes to align with
SD-Turbo’s convention, we find it unnecessary and use the same config as with SDXL-Turbo (80×80
for input and 128 × 128 for output) for simple implementation. The model is trained from scratch
using the AdamW optimizer with a fixed learning rate of 10−4 (no scheduling) for 20K steps on 8
NVIDIA H100 GPUs (80GB each), with a batch size of 32 per GPU (global batch size 256).

Decoding procedure. The full Sparse VAE Decoding proceeds as follows:

Additional Qualitative Results
C.1
Latent Hyperloop vs. Image Bilinear Interpolation
Fig. A3 compares the output of our Latent Hyperloop adapter against the standard pixel-space
pipeline (VAE decode →bilinear interpolation →VAE encode). For each input latent, we decode
both versions to pixel space. The Latent Hyperloop closely matches the pixel-space ground truth
across diverse content types.
C.2
Effect of Training Objectives on Latent Hyperloop Reconstruction
Fig. A4 provides a qualitative comparison of the training objectives used for learning the Latent
Hyperloop adapter. Using only the ℓ1 reconstruction loss preserves the coarse object layout, but
the reconstructed image remains visibly over-smoothed, with weakened local contrast and softened
facial structure. Adding E-LatentLPIPS yields only limited perceptual improvement over ℓ1 alone. In
contrast, incorporating the proposed Attention Alignment objective produces substantially sharper
and more faithful reconstructions, especially in fine-grained regions such as the eyes, nose contour,
and surrounding fur. These visual differences support the design choice of using Attention Alignment
to improve latent-space upsampling quality.

Additional UltraFlash Outputs

Figs. A7 and A8 show results using SDXL as the base model. All images are generated with
UltraFlash refinement on a single A100 GPU, demonstrating that our pipeline generalizes across
different base architectures.

D
Limitations
UltraFlash is developed and evaluated for cascade-based megapixel image synthesis. This focus
allows us to isolate and optimize a concrete bottleneck that appears in practical high-resolution
text-to-image pipelines: VAE-heavy transitions between latent and pixel space. We therefore view the
method as a targeted acceleration strategy for image synthesis rather than a claim about all generative
settings. Other modalities, such as video generation, introduce additional temporal and architectural
considerations that are outside the scope of this work. At the same time, the central observation
19


behind UltraFlash is broader: as denoising becomes faster, the surrounding transition costs become
increasingly important. Exploring transition-aware acceleration beyond image cascades is a natural
direction for future work.
E
Broader Impact
UltraFlash improves the efficiency of high-resolution image synthesis by reducing transition overhead
in cascade pipelines. This can make megapixel generation more practical for creative tools, design
workflows, data visualization, and research settings where high-resolution outputs are useful but
computational cost is a barrier. At the same time, faster and higher-resolution generation should be
deployed with the same safeguards used for the underlying generative models, including content
filtering, provenance or watermarking mechanisms when available, and responsible-use policies.
UltraFlash does not introduce a new generative prior or bypass the safety behavior of the base
models; it accelerates the transition operations around existing cascade refinement. We therefore
view its impact as primarily improving efficiency, while inheriting the broader benefits and risks of
the image-generation systems on which it is built.

experiments in Section 5, and comparisons with native 4K and FLUX-based methods are

Question: Does the paper discuss the limitations of the work performed by the authors?
Answer: [Yes].
Justification: The paper discusses limitations in Appendix D. The discussion frames the
