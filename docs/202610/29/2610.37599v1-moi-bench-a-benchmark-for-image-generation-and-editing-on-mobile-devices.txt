ABSTRACT
Developing on-device image models requires considering practical user needs,
task types and device resource constraints. However, existing benchmarks provide
limited guidance on identifying capability gaps between on-device models and
server-side models. We therefore introduce MoI-Bench, a benchmark grounded
in mobile user needs, comprising 64 subtasks and 1150 samples. It defines 16 op-
erations, such as removal and replacement, and eight visual target categories cov-
ering content such as portraits and text. These form two orthogonal axes whose
applicable combinations define specific tasks, such as removing facial makeup.
To better guide model improvement, we further propose MoI-Eval, which in-
troduces task-specific criteria covering instruction following, visual quality and
detail preservation. Based on these criteria, we construct sample-level evaluation
questions and aggregate their scores into overall quality and capability profiles
along the operation, target and criteria axes. We evaluate representative on-device
and server-side models and measure mobile deployment costs. The results show
that this decoupled Operation × Target design provides finer-grained guidance
for improving compact models. For example, it highlights the need to prioritize
specific capability gaps (e.g., text-replacement) over broad application-level goals
(e.g., improving text editing). Unlike conventional benchmarks that rank mod-
els using a single score, we introduce a two-dimensional Quality-Deployability
capability map to assess models for practical mobile use. The map shows that
current on-device models can achieve efficient generation or editing but expand-
ing their task coverage deserves greater attention than further improving deploy-
ment efficiency. Further progress requires improving editing quality, particularly
identity preservation and text rendering, while broadening task coverage. When
resource constraints limit the capabilities achievable on-device, task-aware on-
device–cloud routing provides a complementary direction for future research. Our
benchmark data and inference and evaluation toolkits are available here.

INTRODUCTION

However, existing benchmarks such as GenEval Ghosh et al. (2023) and ImgEdit-Bench Ye et al.
(2026) primarily assess general generation and editing capabilities. They provide limited guidance

A request such as removing text from a picture specifies both an action and the visual content it
concerns. We define the requested action, such as removal or replacement, as the operation, and
the type of visual content to generate or modify as the target. Integrating more general application
categories such as Text Editing can extend coverage of mobile scenarios but does not always
make the task coverage explicit. For example, Text Editing can include both text removal and
text replacement, so a high category score of Text Editing does not establish proficiency in
both operations. Conversely, removing text and removing a person share the same operation ap-
plied to different targets. Grouping scores only by application category makes it difficult to compare
removal capability across targets and between on-device and server-side models. We therefore in-
troduce MoI-Bench, a Mobile Image generation and editing Benchmark grounded in a user survey
of mobile application needs. Fig. 1 illustrates how we connect these needs to structured capability
evaluation and model improvement. We define most operations following prior works Jiang et al.
(2026); Jia et al. (2026), then use a user survey to identify priority target categories for mobile
applications. It defines 16 operations and eight target categories as two orthogonal axes of an Oper-
ation × Target Task Matrix. Applicable combinations are manually reviewed, yielding 64 subtasks
comprising 1150 samples. This organization supports comparisons between operations for the same
target and between targets for the same operation, helping developers assess the requests relevant to
their applications.
Task performance alone does not explain which output requirements a model fails to satisfy. When
asked to remove a specified person from a group portrait, a model may remove the wrong person,
alter the remaining people’s identities or leave visible artifacts. Then we propose MoI-Eval, which
associates each subtask with applicable criteria (i.e., instruction following, visual quality and detail
preservation) in a sparse Operation × Target × Criteria Rubric Cube. Based on these criteria, we
develop detailed questions for each sample to evaluate models’ outputs and aggregate their scores
into overall quality Q and capability profiles along operation, target and criteria. Joint Operation
× Target profiles show performance on specific requests, while criterion profiles help researchers
identify whether improvements are needed in instruction following, visual quality or preservation of
existing details.
For mobile applications, output quality must also be considered alongside deployment costs. Even
high-quality models are unsuitable for on-device use if their memory requirements exceed device
capacity. We therefore retain quality Q and deployability D as separate dimensions in a Quality-
Deployability capability map. The quality dimension considers the overall score Q, task coverage
and criterion-group scores, while the deployability dimension considers end-to-end latency, peak
memory and deployed model size. Based on these measurements, we define quality tiers and de-
2


ployability tiers and use them to construct a Quality-Deployability capability map. The map posi-
tions models by capability and resource requirements, helping developers select suitable models and
researchers identify priorities for broader task coverage, higher output quality or lower deployment
costs.
We evaluate 19 generative models and measure text-to-image deployment costs on iOS and Android
devices. Our results first show that MoI-Bench provides finer-grained and clearer guidance for model
improvement. For example, Ovis-U1 Wang et al. (2025a) achieves comparable performance with
FLUX.2 [klein] Black Forest Labs (2026) on text removal but falls far behind on text replacement.
This challenges the assumption that compact models perform poorly across all text-related tasks and
suggests that improving specific tasks (e.g., text replacement) should take priority over text editing in
general. This distinction also helps users select models for their intended purposes. Criterion profiles
further distinguish on-device models’ development priorities: DreamLite Feng et al. (2026a) shows
weaknesses in visual integrity and coherence, whereas Ovis-U1 has a lower identity-preservation
score. Additionally, our findings suggest that broadening task coverage deserves greater attention
than further optimizing mobile deployment efficiency. Models such as BlazeEdit Deng et al. (2026)
and Moebius Duan et al. (2026) support only a single editing task, while even more general editing
models such as DreamLite Feng et al. (2026a) lack support for multiple input images. Mobile-O
and DreamLite both reach D3 on the iPhone 17 Pro, but their reference quality tiers are Q1 and
Q2, respectively. Further progress therefore requires improving editing quality and expanding task
coverage while preserving resource efficiency. The task-dependent capability gaps also motivate
exploring on-device–cloud routing, with requests assigned according to model capability and de-
ployment constraints.
In summary, our contributions are:
• We introduce MoI-Bench, a benchmark for on-device image models, built on an Opera-
tion × Target Task Matrix. Its 1,150 samples span 64 subtasks designed around mobile
application needs, covering image generation and four types of editing operations.
• We propose MoI-Eval, which derives overall quality Q and capability profiles along the op-
eration, target, and criteria axes from a sparse Operation × Target × Criteria Rubric Cube.
It further introduces, to the best of our knowledge, the first unified Quality-Deployability
capability map for benchmarking models instead of a single score.

RELATED WORK
On-device Diffusion Models.
Mobile diffusion models face constraints in storage, memory, com-
putation and operator support. Existing work addresses these constraints through graph optimization
and compression Chen et al. (2023); Choi et al. (2023), pruning and distillation Li et al. (2023); Zhao
et al. (2024b); Castells et al. (2024); Chen et al. (2025a); Chavhan et al. (2026); Hu et al. (2026);
Du et al. (2026); Feng et al. (2024), and low-bit quantization Zhao et al. (2024a); Li et al. (2024b).
Recent systems improve generation efficiency and extend capabilities to understanding and edit-
ing Shaker et al. (2026); Feng et al. (2026a); Deng et al. (2026); Kodavanti et al. (2026); Duan et al.
(2026). These advances motivate a unified evaluation of model quality and mobile deployability.
Benchmarks for image generation and editing.
Generation benchmarks assess prompt align-
ment, compositional reasoning and visual correctness Saharia et al. (2022); Hu et al. (2023); Huang
et al. (2025); Ghosh et al. (2023); Bakr et al. (2023); Lee et al. (2023); Li et al. (2024a); Wu et al.
(2024); Wang et al. (2025b). Editing benchmarks evaluate instruction following and content preser-
vation across increasingly diverse tasks Zhang et al. (2023); Ju et al. (2023); Ma et al. (2024); Qian
et al. (2025); Liu et al. (2025); Jia et al. (2026); Wu et al. (2026c); Ye et al. (2026); Yi et al. (2026);
Jiang et al. (2026). Unified suites cover generation, editing and reference-conditioned tasks Ku et al.
(2024b); Pan et al. (2025); Hua et al. (2025); Sani et al. (2026). MoI-Bench organizes tasks by op-
eration and target for task-specific analysis and, to our knowledge, is the first benchmark to jointly
evaluate the quality and deployability of on-device image diffusion models.

MOI-BENCH
MoI-Bench organizes tasks through an Operation × Target Task Matrix. Operations specify what
the model should do, and targets identify the visual content to generate or modify. Applicable
combinations define subtasks of the form “Operation on Target”.
To better align the benchmark with real-world mobile user needs, we survey 1,000 participants to
guide task scope and target coverage. As shown in Fig. 2(a), portrait retouching, object removal,
photo restoration and text editing are the most common desired uses. These preferences motivate
an emphasis on image editing, while text-to-image generation is retained to assess models’ basic
image-generation capabilities. The reported use cases guide our selection of targets and opera-
tions, whose applicable combinations define subtasks such as blemish adjustment and photobomb
removal. User priorities motivate criteria for instruction following, identity preservation and visual
quality. We refine these requirements into task-specific criteria and sample-level evaluation ques-
tions. Preferences for quick results, low storage use and avoiding device slowdown further motivate
our deployment measurements of latency, model size and peak memory. Survey details are provided
in the Appendix A.1.
3.1
OPERATION × TARGET TASK MATRIX
Operation taxonomy.
Following prior taxonomies Yu et al. (2025); Ye et al. (2026), we organize
16 operation units into five groups (Tab. 1):
• Generation. Creating an image from a prompt without an input image. Tasks specify the
desired subjects, attributes, composition and style. Generation conditioned on a reference
image is assigned to Reference Editing.
• Local Editing. Adding, removing, replacing or adjusting specified content while preserving
unrelated regions. Addition introduces new content, while removal deletes selected content
and reconstructs the exposed region. Replacement substitutes content, such as replacing a
hairstyle or a word. Adjustment changes attributes of existing content, such as color, size,
position or pose.
• Global Editing. Changing overall appearance or scene context through stylization, back-
ground modification, restoration or camera-view changes. Stylization applies a visual style
across the image, while background modification changes the surroundings and preserves
4


the foreground subject. Restoration addresses degradation such as blur, noise or overex-
posure, including localized defects. Camera-view changes alter the viewing perspective
rather than individual object positions.
• Understanding Editing. Inferring the required change or completing content from seman-
tic and visual context. Semantic editing requires interpreting an intended outcome rather
than an explicitly prescribed visual change, such as showing what happens to ice cream left
in the sun. Inpainting reconstructs missing content within a supplied mask using the sur-
rounding image. Outpainting extends content beyond the original image boundaries while
maintaining scene consistency.
• Reference Editing. Using reference images to specify desired content or attributes, such
as identity, pose, clothing or style. Following prior work Oshima et al. (2026), we orga-
nize these tasks by input configuration. A single reference without a base image guides
the generation of a new image. A base image with one reference supports transferring
specified content or attributes into the base image. Configurations with two or three refer-
ences combine information from multiple sources, with each reference assigned a role in
the instruction.
Target taxonomy.
To reflect mobile application needs, we include Animals, Portraits, and Text
& Documents alongside General Objects. Targets are categorized by the primary visual content to
generate or modify, rather than every entity present in the image. These four groups contain eight
target categories.
• General Objects. This group contains Concrete Objects and Abstract Objects. Concrete
Objects include vehicles, furniture and everyday items. Abstract Objects cover visual ef-
fects and relations, such as lighting, shadows and reflections.
• Animals. This category covers animals, including pets, their appearance and their acces-
sories. Tasks include generating animals, modifying their pose or fur, and adding or re-
moving accessories such as collars.
• Portraits. This group contains four categories: Body & Limbs, Face & Hair, Clothing &
Accessories, and Multi-Subjects. Body & Limbs covers body shape, limb configuration
and pose. Face & Hair covers facial features, expressions, makeup, hairstyles and identity.
Clothing & Accessories covers garments and worn items, such as coats, glasses and jewelry.
Multi-Subjects covers groups of people, including group-level changes and edits to selected
individuals within a group. Examples include adding or removing a person and changing
the pose of a specified individual while preserving the others.
• Text & Documents. This category covers text content and its visual presentation in doc-
uments, posters, signs and other text-bearing images. Tasks include adding, removing,
replacing or translating text, as well as modifying typography and layout. It also includes
restoring document clarity and correcting page perspective.
Task coverage.
We construct subtasks by pairing operations with target categories. For example,
Replacement × Text & Documents replaces specified text while retaining the surrounding
content and layout. We also manually review the applicability of each operation × target combi-
nation. For example, Addition × Body & Limbs is marked N/A because adding anatomical
parts falls outside our portrait-editing scope. Operations are adapted to practical target-specific
needs (e.g., camera-view editing becomes perspective rectification for documents rather than view-
point changes in general scenes). We also group target categories under a shared subtask when they
follow the same task definition (e.g., stylization of the whole image). Reference editing with two
or three reference images also uses shared subtasks to combine information from multiple sources.
Each merged cell counts as one subtask, yielding 64 subtasks in total (Tab. 1). Detailed task descrip-
tions and examples are provided in the Appendix A.2.

Subsequently, we retrieve input and reference images from Pexels and Wikimedia Commons. Im-
ages are selected to provide the visual information needed for the requested task, such as visible
facial features for makeup editing or readable text for text replacement. For reference editing, we
review the base and reference images together to ensure that the specified transfer is feasible and

degradations to the source images. All evaluated models receive the same processed inputs.

plates. We manually select and adapt these templates to each sample, specifying the requested
change, its target and any content that should remain unchanged. For tasks with multiple input
images, instructions also identify the role of each reference. Text-to-image samples contain only
prompts describing the desired image, without input images. Two annotators cross-review each sam-
ple for instruction clarity, task feasibility and consistency with the input images. They also check for
duplicates and review visual and instruction diversity within each subtask. The final subtask sample
counts are provided in Tab. 14 in the Appendix A.2.
Quality control.
Following prior work Ye et al. (2026); Jiang et al. (2026); Zhang et al. (2023), two
annotators cross-review each sample for instruction clarity, task feasibility and image–instruction
consistency. They check whether the instruction clearly identifies the requested change and its target,
and whether the input images provide the information needed to perform the task. For reference
editing, they inspect all inputs together and check that the role of each reference is clearly specified.
For text-to-image generation, they review the prompt for clarity and consistency with the assigned
subtask. We also check for duplicate samples and review visual and instruction diversity within each
subtask. This review informs instruction refinement and adjustments to sample allocation.

Comparison with existing benchmarks.
We summarize task categories used in prior benchmarks
to define the comparison dimensions in Tab. 2. These dimensions cover text-to-image generation,
local and global editing, pose and spatial changes, viewpoint changes, implicit editing and multi-
object editing. We also compare input configurations, including the maximum number of reference
images and support for multi-turn editing. MoI-Bench covers text-to-image generation and editing
tasks ranging from local modifications to implicit and multi-object edits, with up to three reference
images. Its distinguishing features are the Operation × Target organization, task-specific quality
criteria, and joint assessment of quality and mobile deployability. We exclude multi-turn editing
because our user survey indicates lower demand for this interaction pattern in mobile applications.

4.1
QUALITY RUBRIC CUBE
We extend the Operation × Target Task Matrix with a criteria axis to form a Quality Rubric Cube.
Each applicable cell specifies the evaluation requirements for an operation × target combination
under one criterion.
Evaluation criteria.
Following ImgEdit-Bench Ye et al. (2026), we organize evaluation into three
criterion groups: instruction following, visual quality and detail preservation. These groups contain
eight criteria that form the third axis of the rubric cube:
7


• Instruction Following (IF): It assesses whether the output satisfies the requested operation
and its constraints. Fidelity checks whether the intended outcome is achieved, including
specified attributes, relations and reference conditions. Selection checks whether the op-
eration targets the correct region or instance, such as the leftmost person in a group rather
than another person.
• Visual Quality (VQ): It assesses the visual quality of the generated or edited image. In-
tegrity checks whether generated or modified content has complete shapes and is free from
distortions, malformed parts and artifacts. Coherence checks consistency within the out-
put and between edited regions and their surroundings, including boundaries, textures and
lighting. Presentation assesses whole-image quality, including readability, composition
and physical plausibility.
• Detail Preservation (DP): It assesses whether content and attributes outside the requested
change remain preserved. Context checks non-target objects, animals and background de-
tails. Structure checks framing and spatial relations, including poses and positions that
should remain unchanged. Given the demand for identity preservation in our user survey,
Identity explicitly checks human identity and appearance features not targeted by the in-
struction. Preservation requirements exclude changes requested by the user; for example,
hairstyle replacement should preserve facial identity but not the original hairstyle.
Task-specific rubrics.
For each subtask, we define task-specific requirements and scoring rules for
the applicable criteria. Each rubric entry (o, t, c) corresponds to operation o, target t and criterion
c. For example, Removal × Multi-Subjects × Identity checks whether the remaining
people’s identities are preserved after removing the specified person.
We then manually mark each entry as always applicable, sample-dependent, or not applicable. For
text-to-image generation, all three DP criteria are inapplicable because no source image is provided.
For object editing, Identity is sample-dependent and applies only when the input contains people
whose identities should be preserved. These applicability labels define the sparse rubric cube. For
each sample, we include always-applicable entries and resolve sample-dependent entries using its
instruction and input images. Non-applicable entries are excluded from evaluation.
Sample-grounded questions.
Following question-based evaluation methods (e.g., TIFA Hu et al.
(2023)), we specify evaluation requirements as questions about whether the output satisfies them.
For sample s, we construct a question set Qs,c for each applicable criterion:

Capability profiles and Quality tiers.
We aggregate the underlying evaluation scores along the
operation, target and criteria axes to characterize model capabilities beyond overall quality Q. Op-
eration and target profiles average sample quality scores us within each category, using the target
assignments defined in Sec. 3. Joint Operation × Target profiles further reveal performance on spe-
cific tasks and connect model capabilities to concrete mobile application needs. For example, a user
who wants to remove text from a photograph can consult the Removal × Text & Documents score,
while replacing the text requires examining the corresponding Replacement score. This distinction
supports model selection for individual requests rather than relying on a broad text-editing score.
We also average vs,k and zs,c over their applicable samples to obtain profiles for the three criterion
groups and eight individual criteria. These profiles help researchers identify the capabilities each
model needs to improve within instruction following, visual quality and detail preservation. For
example, successful person removal may still involve unintended changes to the remaining people’s
identities. Examining Fidelity and Identity separately reveals this difference between completing
the requested change and preserving existing details. All profiles follow the same sample inclusion
rules as overall quality.

4.3
QUALITY-DEPLOYABILITY MAP
The quality score Q measures output quality but does not account for the time and resources required
for mobile inference. We assess deployability using end-to-end latency, peak memory and deployed
model size. These metrics capture waiting time, runtime memory usage and storage requirements
in mobile use. We therefore present quality and deployability on separate axes in the Quality-
Deployability Map.
Deployability measurement.
Mobile deployment involves model-specific quantization, operator
adaptation and runtime optimization. Differences in model architecture and device support make
it difficult to apply an identical optimization procedure across different models. We therefore eval-
uate the resource costs of deployed configurations from a mobile user’s perspective: storage re-
quirements, waiting time and runtime memory usage. Our protocol accommodates model-specific
deployment optimizations and measures the resulting deployment costs using consistent metrics.
A deployment configuration specifies model weights, component precisions, inference settings and
runtime. We report three metrics:
• Model Size. Total storage required for standalone inference, including the backbone, image
decoder, required encoders and supporting inference files. Shared files are counted once;
duplicate artifacts and development files are excluded.
• Inference Latency. End-to-end time covering prompt processing, text encoding, generation,
image decoding and post-processing. Initial model loading, warm-up and result-file writ-
ing are excluded. Loading and offloading performed during generation remain part of the
measured latency.
• Peak Memory. Maximum sampled application memory within each inference run, averaged
across runs. We use application physical footprint on iOS and process proportional set size
(PSS) on Android, reporting the two platform-specific measurements separately.
Storage and memory are reported in MiB. Failed and unmeasured configurations are distinguished.
Devices, workloads, precision settings and measurement procedures are detailed in the experimental
setup.
Deployability tiers.
Alongside quality tiers, we define deployability tiers D1–D4 using the joint
requirements in Tab. 3. D1 denotes unavailable local deployment under the tested conditions, in-
cluding deployment failure, invalid output, cloud- or API-only access, or a model that is too large for
the target device. D2 requires successful execution, while D3 and D4 impose progressively tighter
latency, storage and memory limits. D4 is the highest tier, designed to represent responsive mobile
inference with modest resource requirements. These tiers assess the deployment outcome achieved
10


by a model and its implementation on a specified device, allowing improvements from hardware and
runtime optimization to be reflected in its rating. Thresholds are shared across devices, models and
precision settings.
For the baseline comparison, we do not introduce bespoke operator adaptations or additional model-
specific performance optimizations beyond those required to execute the models. Among the evalu-
ated models, DreamLite is the only model for which we successfully deployed editing on-device. We
therefore use text-to-image generation as the common deployment task supported by all evaluated
on-device models.
Tier threshold calibration.
We first manually define initial thresholds for quality and deploya-
bility tiers. For quality tiers, we compare assignments obtained from GPT-4o and Gemini 3.1 Pro,
identify disagreements, and iteratively adjust the shared thresholds to improve agreement while
preserving meaningful distinctions between capability levels. For deployability tiers, we compare
assignments for matched deployment configurations on two devices with comparable hardware ca-
pabilities and calibrate the shared thresholds to reduce cross-device discrepancies. The resulting
thresholds are fixed for all reported comparisons and listed in Tab. 3. Sensitivity analyses are pro-
vided in the supplementary material.
Q-D Map construction.
Evaluating an on-device model requires considering both quality and de-
ployability. High quality cannot compensate for unsuccessful deployment, while low latency cannot
compensate for insufficient task capability. We therefore retain these dimensions as separate axes
rather than combining them into a single score. The Quality-Deployability capability map places
reference quality tiers from Sec. 4.2 on the horizontal axis and device-specific deployability tiers
on the vertical axis. The upper-right region represents the goal of an on-device flagship model,
combining high quality, broad task coverage and efficient mobile execution. The lower-right region
represents high-quality models with limited mobile deployability, including server-side models ac-
cessed through cloud APIs. The map thus indicates whether model development should prioritize
quality and task coverage, deployment efficiency, or both.
A complementary scatter plot presents continuous quality scores against end-to-end mobile latency.
Color indicates task coverage, and bubble area represents total parameter count. Models without
successful mobile inference are displayed separately without latency coordinates. Together, these
views show each model’s position and the measured differences behind its tier assignment.

Operation × Target analysis.
Fig. 5 reveals task-specific differences that operation-level and
target-level averages obscure. Although text and documents receive low aggregate scores, Dream-
Lite and Ovis-U1 achieve 0.66 and 0.71 on text removal, close to FLUX.2 [klein]’s 0.76. Their
scores fall to 0.03 and 0.27 on text replacement, and 0.07 and 0.11 on text addition.
These results suggest that limited text-generation capability is the main weakness. And improving
replacement therefore requires identifying which content types remain difficult, rather than treating
the operation as uniformly weak.
In reference editing, model performance also varies across targets. For R1 tasks involving facial
features and hairstyles, Ovis-U1 outperforms DreamLite (0.77 versus 0.46), but the ordering reverses
for multiple subjects (0.22 versus 0.70). A single reference-editing or portrait score would conceal
this distinction. Moreover, both models perform well on R1 tasks involving concrete objects and
animals, yet neither supports the evaluated base-plus-reference configurations. Their reference-
editing limitations thus involve both quality on particular targets and missing input configurations.
The joint analysis helps developers distinguish task-specific quality gaps from missing functionality
and supports model selection according to both the requested operation and its target.
Criterion-level analysis.
Tab. 7 shows that the gap between on-device and larger models varies
across quality requirements. Ovis-U1 approaches LongCat-Image-Edit in Integrity and Coherence
but trails in Selection and Identity, while DreamLite approaches it in Structure but trails in Integrity
and Coherence. These comparisons suggest that preserving image structure or producing visually
coherent edits does not ensure accurate target selection or identity preservation. Identity preservation
is also a weakness of some larger models: DeepGen scores 0.65, below DreamLite’s 0.73, despite
its higher Fidelity and visual-quality scores.
On-device model development should go beyond visual quality to improve where edits are applied
and whose identity is preserved, while addressing the visual defects that remain in models such as
DreamLite.

Precision configurations.
We vary backbone and decoder precision across FP16, W8A16 and
W8A8. W8A16 and W8A8 use 8-bit weights with 16-bit and 8-bit activations respectively. We
focus on FP16 and W8A16 combinations in Tab. 9, as W8A8 configurations frequently encounter
execution failures or severe visual degradation. Weight quantization generally reduces model stor-
age, but its latency and memory benefits are less consistent. For example, Mobile-O runs faster
with a W8A16 backbone on Android, while its peak memory increases substantially. Several con-
figurations that produce recognizable outputs on iOS also fail execution or output-quality checks on
Android, indicating that precision choices must be validated for each model and platform.

5.5
Q-D ANALYSIS.
Fig. 6 shows that fast mobile inference does not necessarily provide broad task capability. Mobile-O
and DreamLite have similar iOS latency (2.81 and 3.22 s), yet their task coverage differs substan-
tially (0.11 and 0.64), placing them in Q1–D3 and Q2–D3, respectively. Mobile-O’s low benchmark-
wide quality primarily reflects its generation-only scope, highlighting the distinction between effi-
ciently executing a supported task and meeting diverse mobile editing needs. Ovis-U1 achieves
a slightly higher overall quality score than DreamLite (0.66 versus 0.62), but lower task coverage
(0.59 versus 0.64), alongside substantially higher latency and peak memory. Thus, a higher average
quality score alone does not establish a more useful mobile model.

These results suggest different development priorities for different starting points. For efficient
generation-only models, a key next step is to support editing while retaining their deployment effi-
ciency. For models such as DreamLite, priorities include broader input support, particularly multiple
reference images, and better editing quality in the weak operation–target combinations identified by

evaluated alongside any changes in output quality. Moving toward the upper-right region of the
map therefore requires both broader, more reliable capabilities and lower deployment costs; the
evaluated on-device models currently reach neither Q3 nor D4.
When model-size constraints limit the capabilities achievable locally, an on-device agent could use
the capability profiles to route requests between local models and cloud services. Routing should
depend on the requested operation and target rather than broad application labels. For example,
Ovis-U1 performs comparably to FLUX.2 [klein] on text removal but falls far behind on text re-
placement, suggesting that these requests may warrant different execution choices. A router could
retain locally supported requests that meet the required quality and latency budgets, while sending
tasks such as difficult text replacement or unsupported multi-image editing to a more capable cloud
model. Evaluating this task-aware routing strategy is a direction for future work.

CONCLUSION
We introduce MoI-Bench and MoI-Eval to connect users’ needs for image generation and editing
on mobile devices with model evaluation and development. MoI-Bench organizes 64 subtasks along
operation and target axes, while MoI-Eval uses task-specific criteria to assess output quality and
construct capability profiles. For users, these profiles help select on-device or cloud models for
specific requests, such as makeup removal or text replacement. For researchers, they identify unsup-
ported tasks or unmet quality requirements, providing directions for expanding model capabilities
and improving quality. The Quality × Deployability map complements this analysis with mobile la-
tency, storage and memory costs, helping developers assess which capabilities are practical to offer
locally. Together, this framework supports evaluating image models by how well they meet users’
needs and guides the development of more capable and accessible image AI across mobile and cloud
platforms.

A.1
USER SURVEY
Survey overview.
We collected 1,000 anonymous responses on mobile image AI. Fig. 2(a) reports
interest, use cases and priorities. Interest in on-device image AI is 68% Yes, 24% Maybe and 8% No.
Tab. 11 presents the questionnaire structure and one example scenario. Q1 asks about current usage
and interest, and Q2 asks about desired use cases. Q3 contains 25 image-generation and editing
scenarios. Each scenario presents 10–15 requirements, including shared usability needs and task-
specific requirements. All respondents answer all 25 scenarios and select up to eight requirements
per scenario. Selection rates use all 1,000 respondents as the denominator. For shared requirements,
a respondent is counted once if they select the requirement in any scenario. Selection rates describe
the respondents rather than the wider population.

B.1
TASK-SPECIFIC RUBRICS AND CRITERION APPLICABILITY
A criterion specifies a quality requirement for an output. MoI-Eval defines eight criteria under
instruction following (IF), visual quality (VQ) and detail preservation (DP) (Tab. 15). Preservation
applies only to content or attributes not targeted by the instruction.
Table 15: Definitions of the eight evaluation criteria.
Group
Criterion
Definition
IF
Fidelity
Satisfying the requested outcome and explicit constraints.
Selection
Applying the change to the correct region or instance.
VQ
Integrity
Well-formed generated or edited content without defects or artifacts.
Coherence
Consistent boundaries, lighting and appearance within the output and around

Overall readability, composition and physical plausibility.
DP
Context
Preserving non-target objects and background details.

Preserving human identity and appearance features not targeted by the edit.
Task-specific rubrics.
Each entry (o, t, c) defines criterion c for operation o and target t. For
example, Fidelity checks text removal or correct replacement text, depending on the operation. For
person removal, Identity checks the remaining people. These requirements guide sample-grounded
questions.
Criterion applicability.
We manually label each rubric entry as:
• Always applicable. Required for every sample in the subtask, such as Fidelity in text re-
placement.
• Sample-dependent. Determined by the instruction and input images. For example, Identity
applies to object editing when people must remain unchanged.
• Not applicable. Excluded from the subtask. For example, text-to-image generation has no
source content to preserve, so DP is excluded.
We include always-applicable criteria and resolve sample-dependent criteria for each sample. The
selected criteria determine its questions and criterion groups.
Non-applicable criteria are excluded, not scored as zero.

B.3
ADDITIONAL EXPERIMENTAL SETTINGS
Output resolution.
Text-to-image generation uses each model’s default resolution; wallpaper
prompts use a 9:16 aspect ratio. Non-outpainting editing requests use the base-image dimensions
unless another canvas size is specified. Outpainting requests use a 1:1 canvas by default unless the
instruction specifies another size or aspect ratio. Each pipeline retains its native preprocessing and
resolution constraints. Editing outputs are not resized after generation, except for judge preprocess-
ing.
Judge image preprocessing.
Both judges use identical preprocessing. We resize input and output
images to a maximum side length of 1,024 pixels with bicubic interpolation, preserving aspect ratios
without upscaling. Images are converted to RGB and encoded as JPEG at quality 88. This affects
only judge inputs, not saved model outputs.
Deployment settings.
Deployment tests use the prompt: “A cinematic portrait of a young woman
surrounded by flowers, soft natural light.” Within each model, the prompt, seed, resolution and sam-
pling settings remain fixed across precision configurations. The Android Janus-Pro deployment uses
precomputed prompt embeddings, and its reported latency excludes prompt preparation. Loading or
offloading performed during generation remains part of measured end-to-end latency. Failed con-
figurations are excluded from successful-run averages. Some precision configurations also change
session partitioning or resource management, as noted in Tab. 9; their timing differences cannot
be attributed to precision alone. The backbone and decoder precision experiments in Tab. 9 do
not repeat the full benchmark quality evaluation, and recognizable outputs do not establish quality
retention.

D.4
SENSITIVITY TO TIER THRESHOLDS
Quality thresholds.
Starting from Tab. 3, we shift all minimum requirements for Q, C0.6 and
criterion-group scores by δ ∈{−0.05, −0.02, 0.02, 0.05}. The subtask quality cutoff used to com-
pute C0.6 remains fixed at 0.6, and all scores remain unchanged. Tier assignments use the stored
scores before rounding to the precision displayed in the paper. Between 15 and 19 models retain
their default tiers (Tab. 22). Lowering thresholds by 0.05 moves LongCat-Image-Edit, DeepGen,
Lance and Ovis-U1 from Q2 to Q3; a reduction of 0.02 moves only DeepGen. Increasing thresholds
by 0.05 moves Nano Banana 2 and Seedream from Q4 to Q3 because their IF scores fall below the
resulting 0.95 group requirement. All changes are limited to one tier.
Deployment thresholds.
We multiply all D3 and D4 limits for size, latency and peak memory by
α ∈{0.8, 0.9, 1.1, 1.2}, keeping execution status fixed. The analysis uses the six iPhone 17 Pro
baseline configurations in Tab. 8. All six models retain their tiers under a 10% budget reduction
or a 10–20% increase. A 20% reduction moves Mobile-O and DreamLite from D3 to D2 because
their model sizes exceed the reduced D3 storage limit of 1638.4 MiB. No evaluated configuration
reaches D4 under any tested setting. These results distinguish stability to aggregation weights from
sensitivity to the resource and quality requirements used to define tiers.
