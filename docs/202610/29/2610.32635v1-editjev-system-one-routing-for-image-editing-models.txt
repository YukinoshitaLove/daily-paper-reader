ABSTRACT
Instruction-based image editing models exhibit pronounced sample-specific per-
formance variation: different models can produce substantially different results
for the same source image and editing instruction, and the best-performing model
often varies across requests. These models also differ substantially in inference
latency, making fixed-model deployment inefficient. We therefore study image
editing model routing, which dynamically selects an editor from a candidate pool
given an input image and editing instruction. We introduce EditJev, a System One
routing model that directly predicts sample-level preference scores over candidate
image editing models in a single forward pass. A lightweight preference head
on a vision-language backbone produces a fixed-dimensional score vector with-
out autoregressive model-name generation or response parsing. To train EditJev,
we construct pairwise preferences from offline candidate outputs and optimize a
pairwise ranking objective to learn relative model suitability. At inference time,
EditJev combines the predicted preference scores with model-level speed scores
to balance editing quality and generation latency. Experiments on ImgEdit and
GEdit-En show that EditJev improves aggregate quality over the strongest fixed
editor, from 4.540 to 4.592 and from 8.194 to 8.255, respectively, while reducing
estimated end-to-end latency by 60.8% and 31.7%. These results demonstrate the
effectiveness of direct, structured model routing for efficient image editing.

INTRODUCTION
Instruction-based image editing enables users to modify an image through natural-language com-
mands. A single instruction can specify operations ranging from local object insertion, removal, and
attribute adjustment to global style transfer and illumination changes (Brooks et al., 2023; Zhang
et al., 2023; Sheynin et al., 2024; Huang et al., 2024). Advances in editing architectures, train-
ing data, and model scale have rapidly expanded both the capability and the diversity of available
models (Wu et al., 2026; Black Forest Labs et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2025; Cao et al., 2025).
Yet these models are not interchangeable. Differences in architecture, training strategies, and data
produce complementary behavior across editing tasks (Zang et al., 2026; Hertz et al., 2022; Huang
et al., 2025). A model that performs creative global restyling may alter fine details or spatial struc-
ture, whereas a model specialized in precise local manipulation may be less effective at globally
consistent transformations (Chen et al., 2025). Their computational profiles are similarly hetero-
geneous: larger and slower models can be more capable, but they also incur substantially greater
memory use and inference latency (Wu et al., 2025; Cao et al., 2025). Thus, model quality and cost
depend not only on the model itself but also on the particular image-instruction pair.
This heterogeneity creates a practical deployment dilemma.
As illustrated in the left panel of
Fig. 1, different image editing models exhibit complementary strengths across image-instruction
pairs (Team et al., 2026; Ye et al., 2026). Sending every request to a single default model, typically
the largest or most capable overall, can waste computation on easy inputs while overlooking alter-
natives better suited to particular requests (Zhang et al., 2026; Jiang et al., 2026b; Feng et al., 2026;
Chen et al., 2024). Yet manually selecting an editor for each request is impractical, as it requires
substantial human effort and detailed knowledge of the candidate models.
Motivated by this observation, we formulate the problem as image editing model routing: given an
image, a natural-language instruction, and a set of candidate editors, select a suitable editor before
generation while accounting for editing quality and inference cost. The central challenge is that the
quality each candidate would achieve is unknown at the time of selection. A router must infer the
most suitable editor directly from the input image-instruction pair.
To address this challenge, we introduce EditJev, a System One routing model that learns to predict
sample-level preferences over candidate image editing models to guide editor selection. Following
the System One principle of efficient decision making through structured prediction (TypeSafe AI,
2026), EditJev represents these preferences as a compact, fixed-dimensional score vector. As illus-
trated in the right panel of Fig. 1, EditJev equips a vision-language backbone with a lightweight
preference head to produce a fixed-dimensional score vector in a single forward pass, requiring only
94 ms for routing inference.
We construct training supervision by evaluating editing outputs generated offline by all candidate
models using a multimodal judge. We convert these evaluations into pairwise preferences and train
EditJev with a pairwise ranking objective, enabling it to learn relative model suitability for each
image–instruction pair. At inference time, as illustrated in the middle panel of Fig. 1, EditJev com-
bines its predicted preference scores with model-level speed scores to select an editor according to
the desired balance between editing quality and generation latency.
Our contributions are summarized as follows:
❑Problem formulation. To the best of our knowledge, we are the first to formulate sample-level
routing for instruction-based image editing, where a router selects among candidate editing models
by jointly considering editing quality and inference latency.
❑System One routing framework. We develop EditJev, a System One routing model that di-
rectly predicts preference scores over candidate editors in a single forward pass. Gap-filtered pair-
wise supervision enables the model to learn the relative suitability of candidate editors and supports
latency-aware model selection at inference time.
❑Improved quality–latency trade-off. We evaluate EditJev on ImgEdit and GEdit-En, where it
improves aggregate quality over the strongest fixed editor while reducing estimated latency, includ-
ing routing overhead, by 60.8% and 31.7%, respectively.

RELATED WORK
2.1
INSTRUCTION-BASED IMAGE EDITING
Instruction-based image editing modifies an input image according to a natural-language instruction.
Prior work has advanced this task through image-instruction datasets (Brooks et al., 2023; Zhang
et al., 2023; Hui et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2024) and dedicated editing frameworks (Sheynin et al.,
2024; Huang et al., 2024; Li et al., 2025), supporting diverse operations such as object insertion
and removal, attribute modification, and style transformation (Yu et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2025; Xu
et al., 2025). Recent methods further improve editing quality by scaling model capacity, training
data, and compute (Wu et al., 2026; Black Forest Labs et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2025; Cao et al.,
2025; Team et al., 2025; 2026). For example, Qwen-Image-Edit has 20 billion parameters, while
HunyuanImage-3.0-Instruct has 80 billion parameters in total (Wu et al., 2025; Cao et al., 2025).
Such scaling, however, increases inference latency and deployment cost. Since editing requests
vary in difficulty, lightweight models may suffice for some inputs, whereas others require stronger
models, motivating dynamic routing to balance editing quality and computational cost.
2.2
MODEL ROUTING
Model routing dynamically selects a model from a candidate pool for each input, typically bal-
ancing output quality and inference cost. It has been extensively studied for large language mod-
els(LLMs) as a strategy for cost-aware serving (Hu et al., 2024; Lai et al., 2026). Existing LLM
routers select models by estimating query difficulty, model capability, or expected quality-cost trade-
offs (Ong et al., 2025; Ding et al., 2024; Feng et al., 2025). Model routing has also been extended
to text-to-image (T2I) generation (Xin et al., 2025; Jiang et al., 2026a). However, model routing for
instruction-based image editing remains underexplored.
Recently, System One models have explored direct structured decision making, where models output
compact predictions that can be consumed by downstream systems without additional parsing (Type-
Safe AI, 2026). This design principle is particularly relevant to routing problems, where the objec-
tive is to select among a fixed candidate set rather than generate open-ended responses. In this work,
we introduce EditJev, a direct preference prediction framework that extends model routing to image
editing, enabling instance-level selection among editing models.

3.3
PREFERENCE-BASED ROUTER TRAINING
Overview. To train the editing router, we construct pairwise preference supervision from candidate
model outputs to capture the relative suitability of different editors. For each training request, all
candidate editing models are executed offline and evaluated by an MLLM-based evaluator along
complementary quality dimensions. We then convert the resulting quality scores into preference
pairs and optimize the router with a pairwise ranking objective.
Pairwise Preference Construction. For each training request (x, c), we execute every candidate
model Mi ∈M to obtain its edited image yi = Ei(x, c), where Ei denotes the editing function
implemented by Mi. Following VIEScore (Ku et al., 2024), we use an MLLM evaluator to assess
editing success, preservation of content that should remain unchanged, image naturalness, and free-
dom from visual artifacts, each on a scale from 0 to 10. The semantic-consistency score SCi is
the minimum of the editing-success and preservation scores, and the perceptual-quality score PQi
is the minimum of the naturalness and artifact-free quality scores. For this request, we define the
aggregate quality score of candidate model Mi as their geometric mean:

EXPERIMENTS
4.1
EXPERIMENTAL SETUP
Implementation Details. We use Qwen3-VL-4B-Instruct (Bai et al., 2025) as the multimodal router
backbone, freezing its pretrained parameters and optimizing LoRA adapters and a linear preference
head. The candidate pool consists of FLUX.2 Klein 4B, FLUX.2 Klein 9B, FLUX.1-Kontext-dev,
OmniGen2, and Qwen-Image-Edit. β is set as 0.7 and 0.02 on ImgEdit and GEdit-En. All latency
measurements are conducted on an NVIDIA RTX PRO 6000 GPU using each model’s official in-
ference configuration. We measure the generation time of each model over 20 different test requests
and report the average. We set the pairwise score-gap threshold to δ = 0.5. The routing latency
of each routing method is measured separately as the additional overhead before executing the se-
lected editor. For closed-source routing models, including API-based MLLM routers and Jev, the
latency is measured as the API response time and averaged over 5 requests. For EditJev, the latency
is measured using local inference and averaged over 5 requests.

Compared Methods. We compare EditJev with fixed-model policies, general-purpose MLLM rout-
ing baselines, and System-One routing baselines. Fixed-model policies always invoke the same can-
didate model; we report each candidate separately. For general-purpose MLLM routing, we evalu-

receives the source image, editing instruction, candidate model names, and their measured mean
generation times through the same prompt template. It selects the editor expected to provide the
highest quality, preferring faster editors when the expected quality is similar. Candidate outputs and
reference scores are not included in the prompt. Jev is included as a System-One routing baseline
that directly predicts preferences over candidate models without autoregressive model selection.
Benchmarks. We evaluate EditJev on the ImgEdit (Ye et al., 2026) and GEdit-En (Liu et al., 2025)
image-editing benchmarks, using GPT-5.1 as the primary evaluator and following the official evalu-
ation protocols. We report the benchmark-defined metrics for each dataset.

Qualitative Comparison. Fig. 3 illustrates how EditJev adapts its selection to editing quality and
model latency. For the instruction “Change the floral surface in the background to a simple white
fabric” , only FLUX.2 Klein 4B and FLUX.2 Klein 9B correctly replace the patterned background
with simple white fabric, whereas Qwen-Image-Edit, despite being the strongest fixed model on
average, incorrectly alters the garment itself. Among the two successful candidates, FLUX.2 Klein
9B better preserves the original garment colors than FLUX.2 Klein 4B, while incurring only a small
increase in generation latency, leading EditJev to select it. For the instruction “Extract the black T-
shirt and the black sheer skirt worn by the person in the image,” only Qwen-Image-Edit successfully
extracts the requested garments, and EditJev selects this model despite its higher generation latency.

4B successfully remove the racing car. Among these successful candidates, FLUX.2 Klein 9B has
the lowest mean generation time and is therefore selected. These examples show that EditJev does
not simply choose the strongest or fastest model globally; instead, it selects a model whose output
quality is suitable for the specific request while accounting for generation efficiency. This adaptive
behavior highlights the advantage of sample-level routing, where different editors can be utilized
according to their strengths across diverse editing scenarios.

We compare the

MSE Regression replaces the pairwise
ranking loss with mean squared error regression on the
candidate-quality scores, requiring the router to predict
absolute evaluator scores. MCQ Generation formulates
routing as multiple-choice generation, where the router
is trained to output only the index of the highest-scoring
candidate model. As shown in Table 4, MSE regression reduces the overall score on ImgEdit from
4.6002 to 4.5545, while MCQ generation further decreases it to 4.5491. These results indicate that
pairwise preference learning is more effective for routing than either absolute score regression or
single-label generation. The ranking objective directly optimizes the relative ordering among candi-
date models and can exploit multiple reliable pairwise comparisons from each request. In contrast,
MCQ generation compresses the supervision signal into a single best-model label, discarding the
preference relationships among the remaining candidates. MSE regression requires the router to
accurately predict the evaluator’s absolute scores, making the learning problem more challenging.

CONCLUSION
To the best of our knowledge, this paper is the first to formulate sample-level image-editing model
routing, where a router dynamically selects a suitable editing model for each source image and edit-
ing instruction. We introduce EditJev, a System One routing framework that learns preferences over
candidate editing models and selects suitable editors for different image–instruction pairs, enabling
quality–latency-aware and efficient routing.
Future work will explore more reliable and scalable routing supervision. In particular, we plan to
investigate stronger multimodal evaluation frameworks, including agentic multimodal judges that
can provide more comprehensive and fine-grained assessments of editing quality, to obtain more
reliable preference signals. We also aim to extend routing to larger and dynamically evolving pools
of editing models, developing more robust routers that support the addition and removal of editors
without extensive retraining. These directions may further improve the scalability and adaptability
of image editing model routing systems.
