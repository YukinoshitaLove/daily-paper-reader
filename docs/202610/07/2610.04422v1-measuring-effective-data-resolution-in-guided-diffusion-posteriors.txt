# Abstract 

Guided diffusion samplers are increasingly used to reconstruct physical fields from sparse observations, but standard diagnostics do not say how much of the reconstruction was actually determined by the data. We introduce effective data resolution for black-box generative posteriors: a comparison between the resolution warranted by the inverse problem, dof ref , and the resolution realised by the sampler, 

dof samp . A perturbation estimator measures dof samp and the spatial map R(x, x )

from sampler queries alone. We validate the estimator against exact references and use it to study guided diffusion. The resulting measurements show that guidance weight can strongly alter apparent information transfer, that mean, spread and resolution are not jointly corrected by one weight even with an exact prior and score, and that resolution fidelity does not follow reliably from the apparent principledness of a guidance rule. 

# 1 Introduction 

Reconstructing a physical field from a handful of sensors is a canonical ill-posed inverse problem [24, 25], and generative modelling has become a standard tool for it. The recipe is to train a diffusion prior over solution fields and then steer sampling toward the observations through a likelihood term, yielding full-field reconstructions with uncertainty from sparse data. DiffusionPDE [11], score-based inverse-problem samplers [22, 2, 14], score-based data assimilation [20] and function-space variants [26] are all instances of it. Such a reconstruction is usually assessed by how close it lands to the truth and by whether its uncertainty is calibrated [9, 15]. These metrics do not, by themselves, identify the thing a scientist needs to know before drawing a conclusion from the reconstructed field: which parts of it the sensors actually determined. A sampler can produce accurate-looking, well-calibrated output while the structure a reader finds interesting is supplied almost entirely by the prior, or, in the other direction, while it commits to detail far beyond what the noise justifies [24]. Conventional diagnostics score the reconstruction and its marginals, not the dependence of the reconstruction on the data. 

Two quantities, one comparison. The distinction we make precise is between the information the inverse problem warrants and the information the sampler realises. The first is a property of the problem. Given a prior, a forward operator, a noise level and a sensor layout, there is a classical answer to how many directions of the unknown field the data can constrain: 

> 40th Conference on Neural Information Processing Systems (NeurIPS 2026). Workshop: AI for Stochastic Dynamics. arXiv:2610.04422v1 [cs.LG] 3 Oct 2026

the resolution, or degrees-of-freedom-for-signal, of the inverse problem [1, 19, 24]. With n point sensors it is at most n, and in an ill-posed problem substantially less, because the forward operator destroys information before the sensors see it. It is computable in closed form from a Gauss–Newton Hessian without running any sampler. We write it dof ref and claim no novelty for it; we use it as a reference standard. The second is a property of the algorithm. A guided sampler at a particular guidance weight implements some map from observations to reconstruction, y 7 → Esamp [u | y], and how strongly that map responds to the data is a fact about the sampler, not about the inverse problem. We call it the sampler’s effective data resolution, dof samp . It need not equal dof ref , it is not currently reported, and, as we show, it is largely set by the guidance weight; Appendix D documents this for the DiffusionPDE configuration we inspected. What we propose measuring is their ratio, the resolution-fidelity ratio 

ρres = dof samp /dof ref , (1) read as follows: near 1 the sampler attributes about as much resolution to the observations as they warrant; below 1 it attributes less, so the reconstruction leans more on the prior than it appears to; above 1 it attributes more than the noise justifies. 

Why the sampler side is hard. For an estimator with an explicit Hessian, resolution is a matrix one differentiates. A guided diffusion posterior has neither a Hessian nor a closed form, can only be queried by running it, and is stochastic. The measurement therefore has to treat the sampler as a black box. We adapt the perturbation estimator for generalized degrees of freedom [28, 5], which perturbs the data and measures the response of the fit, combining it with stochastic trace estimation [12, 18]. The resulting estimator uses two evaluations of the sampler’s reconstruction map per probe, a count that does not grow with the field dimension, and it also yields the spatial map. The scalar says how much information the data appear to have supplied; the map says where that apparent influence lands. 

What the measurement reveals. We apply it to guided diffusion in two main settings and one appendix breadth check. In a 1D backward-heat problem with a Gaussian prior the diffused score and the true posterior are both available in closed form, so training error and prior mismatch are eliminated by construction and every deviation is attributable to the guidance scheme. There, the guidance weight moves effective resolution over 0.15 to 1.27 times the warranted value, and no single weight gets posterior mean, spread and resolution right together. In a 2D nonlinear Darcy problem with a non-Gaussian prior and a trained score, a comparable span reappears, but the advantage that principled guidance held in the controlled setting does not carry over. A non-PDE compressed-sensing check in Appendix E is included only as a modest breadth check: it confirms that the measurement is not tied to PDE forward operators, while leaving image restoration, MRI, CT and other application domains to future work.

# 2 Related work 

Guided diffusion for PDE inverse problems. DiffusionPDE [11] learns a joint distribution over coefficient and solution fields and guides sampling with sparse observations together with a PDE 2residual. Score-based methods solve inverse problems by steering a learned prior at test time [22, 2, 14]; score-based data assimilation [20] decouples the observation model from an unconditional trajectory prior, and function-space formulations [26] target discretisation invariance. All of them steer an unconditional prior with an observation term, and all inherit an approximation in doing so, because the exact guidance term ∇xt log p(y | xt) is intractable in general. Diffusion posterior sampling (DPS) [2] replaces it with ∇xt log p(y | ˆx0(xt)) , evaluating the likelihood at the denoised estimate. This substitutes a surrogate likelihood gradient for the intermediate-time likelihood gradient; its effect on how much information the sampler transfers has not, to our knowledge, been quantified. 

ΠGDM [21] models the x0 | xt covariance isotropically, and DDNM [27] and DDRM [14] motivate data-consistency projection schemes. These form the approximation ladder that we use in the experiments. 

Uncertainty quantification for neural PDE surrogates. Linearised neural operators yield function-valued Gaussian processes [17], and predictive calibration methods address reliability of reported intervals [15]; large pretrained PDE models [10] and neural operators [16] raise the same questions at scale. These quantify surrogate error: how wrong the learned operator is. Our question is different and complementary, concerning inverse-problem resolution, namely how much the sensors determined, which is a property of the data and the sampler rather than of the operator’s accuracy. 

Resolution analysis and perturbation-based degrees of freedom. The estimand is classical. Resolution operators and averaging kernels go back to Backus and Gilbert [1], degrees of freedom for signal are standard in atmospheric retrieval [19], and low-rank posterior structure is well studied in large-scale Bayesian inverse problems [25, 24, 7, 23]. So is the estimator: Ye [28] and Efron [5] define generalized degrees of freedom as P 

> i

∂ ˆyi/∂y i and estimate it by perturbing the data and measuring the response of the fit. Monte-Carlo SURE uses the same black-box perturbation idea for denoisers [18], and Hutchinson probing [12] is the standard device for the trace. Our contribution is not a new trace estimator. It is the transplant of this machinery to posteriors that can only be sampled, which requires common random numbers, a noise-scaled perturbation inside a window we characterise, and a smooth coupling condition; the certification of the resulting measurement against exact references; and the pairing of the measured quantity with a computable problem-side reference so that a fidelity ratio can be formed. To our knowledge no formulation of resolution analysis has been applied to a generative posterior, or used as a standard diagnostic for guided diffusion inverse problems.

The resolution operator, informally. Imagine changing the true field slightly at location x′ and asking how the reconstruction at location x responds. Collecting these sensitivities gives the resolution operator

A perfect estimator that recovered the field exactly would have R = I. In an ill-posed problem it cannot: the data do not determine the field, so R is a smoothing operator [1, 19]. Its diagonal R(x, x ) is the interpretable object. A value near 1 means the estimate at x tracks the truth at x, so that location is determined by the data. A value near 0 means the estimate is weakly determined by the data there, so prior information contributes more strongly to what appears at that location. Resolution fidelity. The comparison we propose is the resolution-fidelity ratio ρres =dof samp /dof ref of (1). As a definition it says only this: ρres ≈ 1 means the sampler attributes about as much resolution to the observations as the inverse problem warrants, ρres < 1 means it attributes less, and ρres > 1 means it attributes more. Both departures occur in our experiments. The interpretation is a separate step, and in our settings it is borne out by the other columns. A sampler with ρres < 1 responds weakly to the data, so structure in its reconstruction is less data-driven than it appears and is correspondingly more attributable to the prior. A sampler with ρres > 1 responds to the observations more strongly than the noise justifies, which in §6 coincides with variance ratios far above one and coverage at 100% : detail that the measurements do not support. 

Relation to existing diagnostics. Coverage, CRPS [9] and posterior variance ratios score the distribution a sampler reports: whether its intervals contain the truth at the advertised rate, and whether its spread is right. Resolution is complementary rather than a replacement: it differentiates the reconstruction map with respect to y, and therefore measures data dependence that fixed-observation diagnostics do not directly access.

Cost. Each probe requires two evaluations of the reconstruction map m, and the sampler is used only as a black box. It is worth being precise about what that costs, because m(y) is itself the posterior mean of a stochastic sampler and must be approximated by an ensemble of E trajectories, so the total is 2P E trajectories. What does not grow with the field dimension N is the number of estimator evaluations, 2P : the trace estimator introduces no dimensional dependence of its own, and whatever cost N imposes enters through the price of a single sampler trajectory, exactly as it would if one were merely drawing from the posterior. 4The spatial map. Replacing the random probes by the n coordinate directions of y gives the columns of ∂m/∂y exactly, and hence the diagonal R(x, x ), at 2n evaluations and with no probe variance. This is affordable precisely in the sparse-sensor regime of interest, and it is what turns the scalar into the map of §3. 

Common random numbers. The samplers we audit are stochastic, so m(y) is estimated from a finite ensemble. The two evaluations in (8) must be run under common random numbers, that is, with the same seed, so that the ensemble noise cancels rather than being divided by 2ϵ. For a diffusion sampler this cancellation is exact rather than approximate: the noise draws depend only on the seed and never on y, so for a fixed seed m(·) is a deterministic function of the data [8]. 

Coupling requirement. The estimator therefore requires more than reproducibility at a fixed input: the two perturbed runs must remain smoothly coupled as y changes. Diffusion and flow samplers satisfy this. Metropolis-corrected samplers such as pCN [3] can be replayed from a seed, but their accept/reject discontinuities can split the two perturbed paths, so chain noise swamps the O(ϵ) signal. We return to this in §8. 

Perturbation scale. The step ϵ must be scaled to the observation noise σy rather than set in absolute units, and it must lie inside a window. Its upper edge is ordinary finite-difference bias. It also has a lower edge for the residual-normalised DPS implementation we study, where the normalisation makes the reconstruction map near-singular at small scales; schemes without that normalisation show no such edge. We characterise the window in §8 and Appendix A, and report ϵ with every measurement. 

# 5 Validation 

Before using the measurement to draw conclusions about guided diffusion, we establish that the measurement itself is trustworthy. We do so on a backward-heat problem with a 128 -dimensional field and 12 sensors in a half-domain layout, where the posterior is Gaussian in closed form and 

dof ref = 5 .971 exactly. The estimator passes three checks (Table 3, Appendix C). First, it is unbiased against the exact reference: at P = 128 probes the bias is −0.009 ± 0.028 . Second, its standard error follows the ordinary Monte-Carlo rate 1/√P ; increasing P from 8 to 128 lowers the per-run standard deviation 

0.897 → 0.179 , a factor 5.0 against the 4.0 predicted. Third, it works on a stochastic but reproducible sampler: preconditioned unadjusted Langevin gives 6.33 ± 0.59 against the exact 5.97 .This last check also fixes the main prerequisite. Resolution can only be measured from a sampler whose posterior mean is itself trustworthy. Without low-rank posterior preconditioning [7, 23], the unadjusted Langevin sampler [4] had effective sample size about 40 out of 150 k steps and under-dispersed by 2 to 8×; after preconditioning it reached ESS 13 ,753 and matched the closed-form posterior to 0.3% in mean and 0.6% in standard deviation. The perturbation scale is a separate condition: reference samplers with affine-in-y means are flat in ϵ, but guided samplers are not. A higher-replicate stress test in Appendix E confirms that the exact-score control remains stable while high-weight DPS becomes realisation-sensitive at reduced probe budgets. We return to these conditions in §8.

The control arm validates the apparatus. Exact guidance returns 0.985 dof ref , and ΠGDM and projection land within 2% (Table 1). Since these schemes closely match the reference posterior across the reported diagnostics, a departure elsewhere in the table is a property of the guidance scheme rather than of the measurement. 

Guidance weight sets the apparent information content. Sweeping ζ ∈ [0 .1, 10] (Figure 1b, Appendix D) moves dof samp from 0.15 to 1.27 times dof ref : at ζ = 0 .1 the sampler behaves as though the sensors had determined about a fifth of what they carry, near ζ = 2 as though they had determined more than the noise justifies, and by ζ = 10 it falls back to 0.15 . The same observations, the same prior and the same score thus produce an eightfold difference in apparent information content, depending on a guidance weight rather than on the inverse problem alone. Thus, in this controlled setting, changing ζ substantially changes how much data dependence the sampler realises. 

No single weight is jointly correct. Reading down the DPS rows of Table 1: the weight with the best posterior mean ( ζ = 1 , mean error 0.074 ) carries 13 .8× the correct variance and reports 100% 

coverage at the nominal 90% level; the weight with the best distributional match ( ζ = 0 .3) resolves only 0.80 × the warranted degrees of freedom; and the weight whose resolution is closest to correct 6(ζ = 3 , at ρres = 1 .053 ) has 97 .7× the correct variance and the worst CRPS in the table. No setting gets mean, spread and resolution right together. This is not a failure of tuning that a finer sweep would repair, because the sweep is what exhibits it, and it holds under the most favourable conditions available. Mechanistically, the DPS rows differ from the exact and ΠGDM controls because the data term is evaluated at the denoised estimate and then scaled by the residual norm. Changing ζ therefore changes both the strength of the data pull and the region of the trajectory where residual-normalised corrections dominate the unconditional diffusion dynamics. Appendix A isolates the numerical consequence: for this DPS implementation the local derivative is singular as ϵ → 0, so the reported resolution is a finite-scale sensitivity, not an infinitesimal Jacobian. 

Matching resolution is therefore not a tuning rule. Some ζ does hit dof ref , but it is not the one with the best mean or spread, and the sweep is non-monotone, so dof samp = dof ref has at least two roots, near ζ ≈ 0.6 and ζ ≈ 4, belonging to very different samplers. The measurement reports a discrepancy; it does not select a setting. 

The scalar hides two spatial failures. Figure 1(a) shows what the trace alone cannot. DPS at ζ = 3 

mis-localises data influence: the exact posterior is nearly uniform over [0 .107 , 0.138] , whereas DPS spans [0 .019 , 0.464] and concentrates influence near x = 24 . The map is also unstable: across 16 

ensemble realisations, tr( R) ranges from −24 .0 to 68 .2, whereas the exact-score control reproduces the closed-form map to 0.002 with zero spread. A sampler whose resolution map changes sign between runs is not one whose reported uncertainty can be taken at face value. Coverage and CRPS in Table 1 respond to the over-dispersion at ζ = 3 , but, being computed at fixed y, cannot reveal where the influence lands or whether it moves between runs. 

What this experiment establishes, and what it leaves open. Because the score and the prior are exact here, this experiment establishes that effective resolution can change purely as a consequence of guidance, and within this controlled setting, distinguishes the schemes: exact guidance, ΠGDM and projection all recover dof ref to within 2% , while DPS does not produce a jointly correct posterior mean, spread and resolution at the weights studied. It cannot tell us whether either conclusion survives once the idealisations are removed. That is the next experiment.

# 8 Use and scope 

The measurement is useful only when its operating conditions are made explicit. The perturbation scale must be tied to the observation noise. In 2D Darcy, using the 1D absolute value ϵ = 10 −2

perturbs the data by 30 × the noise and returns dof samp = 10 .0 against a true 43 .1. Nor can ϵ always go to zero: for the residual-normalised DPS implementation studied here, ∥y − Aˆx0∥−2 makes the small-scale derivative singular (Appendix A). We therefore report ϵ, use ϵ ∈ [0 .1, 1] σy , and make no claim about DPS formulations with different scalings. The estimator also requires a reproducible, smoothly coupled sampler under common random numbers. Diffusion samplers satisfy this; Metropolis-corrected samplers such as pCN [3] can be replayed from a seed, but accept/reject discontinuities can split the two perturbed paths. For pCN we use the Gauss–Newton reference (6), not black-box dof samp . Randomised traces are useful mainly when observation dimension is large: the coordinate-wise map costs 2n evaluations and has no probe variance, while our calibration suggests P ≈ 12 probes for 10% accuracy and P ≈ 289 for 2% .Our scale is deliberate: the comparison requires a trustworthy dof ref , available analytically in 1D and through latent pCN in 2D. Released checkpoints, image restoration, MRI, CT, robotics, consistency models, flow matching, rectified flows and newer inverse solvers are natural targets, but each requires a defensible reference resolution. Our claims are therefore limited to the guidance schemes implemented here, with nonlinear resolution interpreted as a posterior-mean linearisation and severe over-dispersion treated as a practical measurability limit. Appendix E reports a non-PDE compressed-sensing check (Table 4) and a higher-replicate stability stress test (Table 5). 

# 9 Conclusion 

A generative posterior can produce accurate reconstructions and plausible uncertainty statistics while transferring the wrong amount of information from the data. We made that quantity measurable by comparing dof ref , the problem-side resolution budget, with dof samp , the sampler-realised resolution, and by estimating both ρres and the map R(x, x ) from black-box sampler queries. Applied to guided diffusion, the measurement shows that guidance weight can move apparent information content by nearly an order of magnitude, that no single weight fixes mean, spread and resolution together even with an exact score and prior, and that the controlled method ordering need not persist under nonlinear learned settings. Information fidelity is therefore something to measure, not something to infer from the form of a guidance derivation. 8References

# A The perturbation scale: window and singular behaviour 

Why the certification of §5 does not generalise. The estimate is flat across ϵ ∈ [10 −5, 10 −1] to twelve significant figures on both reference samplers. Both have a posterior mean that is affine in y at a fixed noise realisation: the exact Gaussian posterior by construction, and preconditioned unadjusted Langevin because its update is affine in y at fixed noise. For an affine map the central difference is exact at every ϵ, so flatness there is guaranteed and carries no information about guided samplers. 

Common random numbers are exact, so this is bias and not noise. The sampler draws its noise from a generator seeded independently of y, so for a fixed seed ˆm(y) is a deterministic, smooth function of y and the two evaluations in (8) share their randomness exactly. There is no Monte-Carlo residual being amplified by 1/2ϵ. What varies between runs is which realisation ˆm one obtains, which is why we report across-realisation error bars throughout.

# B Three formulations that do not work 

Three natural-looking alternatives to tr( R) are degenerate, one of them subtly. We record them so that they are not re-proposed. 

(i) The zero-prior limit. Sweeping prior strength and calling lim λ→0 Var[ u | y] the irreducible ambiguity. With sparse sensors this limit diverges, so the intended difference of variances is a difference of infinities. 

(ii) Fraction of prior variance removed. ρ(x) = 1 − Var post (x)/C (x, x ). For a stationary prior 

C(x, x ) is constant, so ρ is an affine function of the posterior variance map and carries no information the variance map does not already have. Our two-panel version of this diagnostic, which displayed the two as though they were independent views, had panels agreeing to four decimal places. 

(iii) Elasticity under prior rescaling. f (x) = ∂ log Var post (x)/∂ log κ2 for a prior scaled by κ2.This one is subtle, because it is well defined and we verified it to 1.7 × 10 −4 against finite differences before noticing the problem. In the eigenbasis of the prior-preconditioned Hessian it weights direction 

i by 1/(1 + λi), which is near zero exactly where λi is large, that is, exactly in the well-informed directions one is trying to see; at λ ≈ 4 × 10 4 the weight is 2 × 10 −5. On a planted test with a deliberately inhomogeneous information distribution it produced 1.1% spatial contrast. It measures where the prior still dominates, which is the complement of the question.

# E Additional breadth and stability checks 

A non-PDE compressed-sensing inverse problem. To check that the measurement is not tied to PDE forward maps, we repeated the controlled experiment with random compressed linear observations, y = Au + η, where u ∈ R64 has the same exact Gaussian prior, A ∈ R16 ×64 has independent Gaussian rows normalised to unit norm, and σy = 0 .05 . This is not a new modelling benchmark; it is a breadth check in which the prior, diffused score, denoiser and posterior reference all remain exact. The warranted resolution is dof ref = 15 .78 , close to the sensor count because the random measurements are informative under this prior. The compressed-sensing result is deliberately modest and should not be read as a third application benchmark. It does not address released checkpoints, independent codebases or image-domain inverse solvers, but it does remove the most immediate concern that the measurement only behaves on PDE operators. The same pattern recurs: exact guidance is certified against the reference, and ρres
