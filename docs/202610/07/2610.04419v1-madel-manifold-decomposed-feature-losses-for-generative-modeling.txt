## ABSTRACT 

Generative models are often trained with isotropic objectives such as mean-squared error. For data concentrated near a low-dimensional manifold, how-ever, such losses conflate displacement along the manifold, which may represent valid variation, with displacement away from it, which produces invalid samples. This mismatch is especially problematic in sparse, highly constrained domains, where ambient-space regression can encourage off-manifold interpolation. We ask whether a generative objective can distinguish manifold-parallel variation from manifold-orthogonal deviation directly from data, without explicitly estimating the manifold. We introduce a manifold-decomposed feature loss (MaDeL) that learns complementary representations from corrupted observations: one is trained to re-cover the clean sample, while the other is trained to recover the corruption. We show that, under a feature bottleneck, their Jacobians align with the tangent and normal spaces, exactly for linear manifolds and locally for smooth manifolds. To-gether, these representations define an anisotropic objective that separately mea-sures intrinsic variation and off-manifold deviation. Across synthetic, Earth and climate science, and torsion-angle benchmarks, MaDeL improves support recov-ery and average angular W1 under single-step sampling; on protein backbones, it reduces steric clashes across one- and few-step sampling budgets. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Generative models have achieved remarkable fidelity across images, video, molecules, and protein structures (Karras et al., 2022; Ho et al., 2022; Hoogeboom et al., 2022; Yim et al., 2023b). Despite substantial differences in architecture and formulation, many modern approaches share a common

1arXiv:2610.04419v1 [cs.LG] 3 Oct 2026 training principle: they learn a denoising, score, velocity, or reconstruction target using a largely isotropic regression objective. For example, diffusion and flow-based models commonly minimize an ℓ2 discrepancy between predicted and target quantities (Ho et al., 2020; Song et al., 2021; Lipman et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2023). Such objectives measure how far a prediction is from its target, but are largely agnostic to the direction in which the prediction is wrong .This isotropic treatment is poorly matched to the geometry of natural data. High-dimensional obser-vations are hypothesized to concentrate near a lower-dimensional, structured subset of the ambient space (Tenenbaum et al., 2000; Narayanan & Mitter, 2010; Fefferman et al., 2016; Pope et al., 2021). Locally, perturbations of equal magnitude can have very different consequences depending on their orientation relative to the data manifold (Rifai et al., 2011a; Alain & Bengio, 2014). A displacement parallel to the manifold may correspond to meaningful variation, such as a change in object pose or a valid protein conformation, whereas an orthogonal displacement can produce perceptual artifacts or physically invalid structures. An isotropic loss cannot distinguish between them. This distinction is especially important for generative modeling. Near the data manifold, a generator must both suppress deviations away from valid data and model variation along the manifold. The desired dynamics are therefore inherently anisotropic: orthogonal directions should restore samples toward the manifold, whereas parallel directions should describe variation within it . Recent anal-yses of diffusion models support this geometric perspective, showing that score functions recover directions associated with the normal bundle of the data manifold at low noise levels (Stanczuk et al., 2024). Ignoring this distinction encourages interpolation through off-manifold, low-density regions. Ideally, a generative objective would therefore measure prediction errors according to the local ge-ometry of the data distribution rather than a fixed isotropic metric. Explicitly estimating tangent and normal spaces for complex high-dimensional data, however, is challenging. Instead, we use learned feature spaces to induce data-dependent notions of similarity, building on their successful use as perceptual losses for image synthesis (Johnson et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2018). Concretely, given data x, we construct a corrupted observation ˜x = x+δ and learn two complemen-tary representations. Manifold-parallel features are learned by predicting x from ˜x (x-prediction ), encouraging them to preserve intrinsic variation while suppressing corruption. Manifold-orthogonal features are learned by predicting δ from the same observation ( δ-prediction ), encouraging them to capture deviations away from the manifold. Together, these representations provide a learned de-composition of errors into manifold-parallel and orthogonal components. We use these representations to define a geometry-aware training objective for generative models: rather than penalizing all prediction errors uniformly, it measures them through feature spaces spe-cialized for intrinsic variation and off-manifold deviation, and thus distinguishes predictions that are equally distant in ambient space but differ in their compatibility with the data distribution (Fig. 1). Specifically, our contributions are: • Theoretical characterization of learned geometry (Secs. 4.1 and 4.2). We show that, under suitable bottlenecks, optimal x-prediction and δ-prediction encoders have Jacobian row spaces contained in the tangent and normal spaces of the data manifold, respectively, exactly for linear manifolds and to leading order locally for smooth manifolds. Their fea-ture losses yield complementary manifold-parallel and manifold-orthogonal gradients. • Manifold-decomposed generative loss (Sec. 4.3). We introduce MaDeL, a geometry-aware feature loss that separately measures manifold-parallel variation and manifold-orthogonal deviation using these representations, without access to the data manifold. • Validation across diverse manifolds (Sec. 5). We evaluate MaDeL on a helix, Earth and climate science data, protein and RNA torsion angles, and protein backbone generation. MaDeL improves support recovery and average angular W1 on the single-step benchmarks and reduces steric clashes in protein backbones across one- and few-step budgets. 

## 2 BACKGROUND 

Isotropic regression objectives. Let ˆxθ and xtarg denote a model prediction and its regression target. Diffusion and Consistency Models (Song et al., 2023; Song & Dhariwal, 2024), for example, 2are commonly trained with isotropic ambient-space objectives of the form 

Liso (θ) := E[ρ(∥ ˆxθ − xtarg ∥22)] , (1) where ρ(u) = u yields mean-squared error (MSE) and ρ(u) = √u + c2 −c yields the pseudo-Huber loss. Although these methods construct xtarg differently, their losses depend only on the residual magnitude and therefore penalize equal-magnitude errors identically regardless of direction. 

Geometry-aware generative modeling. Natural data often concentrate near low-dimensional subsets of the ambient space, making their local geometry anisotropic: perturbations of equal mag-nitude can represent meaningful variation when tangent to the data manifold but invalid deviation when normal to it, a distinction ignored by isotropic regression.

Existing approaches primarily incorporate the geometry of aknown state space: Riemannian diffusion models (Huang et al., 2022) and Rieman-nian Flow Matching (RFM) (Chen & Lip-man, 2024) extend generative dynamics to non-Euclidean manifolds, while MeanFlow (MF) (Geng et al., 2025) and Riemannian MeanFlow (RMF) (Woo et al., 2026) en-able one-step generation in Euclidean and manifold-valued spaces, respectively, and Tango-DM trains only the tangential score component of a known manifold (Liu et al., 2025). Other work targets the geometry of the embedded data distribution, but extracts it from a pretrained model: low-noise diffusion scores approximate its normal bundle (Stanczuk et al., 2024), SDDM decom-poses scores into tangent and normal components (Sun et al., 2023), MPGD constructs manifold-preserving guidance from pretrained autoencoders (He et al., 2024), and the score Jacobian yields a tangent-aware metric (Saito & Matsubara, 2026). In contrast, we learn complementary tangent- and normal-sensitive features from data and place them directly in the training objective, with neither a known manifold nor a pretrained models. 

Feature losses as learned directional metrics. Learned features introduce anisotropy without an explicit parameterization of the data manifold: perceptual losses measure errors in representa-tions aligned with perceptual similarity (Johnson et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2018), and regularized autoencoders capture local geometry through denoising, Jacobian contraction, or reconstruction fields (Vincent et al., 2008; Rifai et al., 2011b; Alain & Bengio, 2014). Features have also been incorporated directly into generative training through pretrained discriminator features (Sauer et al., 2021) and feature-based consistency objectives (Song et al., 2023), including manifold-aligned tan-gents (AYT) (Kim et al., 2026). These approaches, however, provide a single notion of similarity rather than complementary sensitivities to intrinsic variation and off-manifold deviation.

Sanity check on a toy example. We first isolate the effect of the training metric on a manifold that is known exactly, using an oracle decomposition that the learned features of Sec. 4 are designed to approximate. The target distribution is a mixture of four von Mises distributions on the unit circle in R2. For each dataset size N , identical generators are trained with the same OT-matched objective and differ only in the regression metric; the crosses in Fig. 2 denote the training samples, and N = ∞ denotes training with fresh samples from the underlying distribution. As shown in Fig. 2 left, Cartesian MSE and Pseudo-Huber (PH) interpolate between sparse modes along Euclidean chords and produce off-manifold samples inside the circle. The oracle decomposed metric applies PH in polar coordinates with (λ∥, λ ⊥) = (1 , 5) , where angle and radius correspond to the manifold-parallel and manifold-orthogonal directions, and preserves the support. Fig. 2 right quantifies this behavior: the decomposed metric reduces the fraction of off-manifold samples to nearly zero across dataset sizes while attaining a comparable angular W1 distance to the target distribution. The improvement therefore arises from the manifold-orthogonal geometry without loss of variation along the manifold, which underscores the role of the training metric.

## 6 CONCLUSION 

We introduced MaDeL, a manifold-decomposed feature loss for generative modeling that distin-guishes variation along the data manifold from deviation away from it. We showed that, under a feature bottleneck, x-prediction and δ-prediction learn encoders whose Jacobians are confined to the tangent and normal spaces of the manifold, exactly for linear manifolds and locally for smooth manifolds, so that the resulting feature loss yields complementary tangent- and normal-aligned gen-erative updates without explicit access to the manifold. Across controlled manifolds and structured 9real-world domains, MaDeL better preserves data support and improves distributional or local geo-metric accuracy relative to isotropic regression, which suggests that explicitly shaping the geometry of the training signal is a practical alternative to parameterizing the data manifold itself, particularly in sparse and highly constrained generative problems. 

Limitations. Our analysis is local and holds to leading order in the corruption level, while the branch dimensions and loss weights must be chosen without knowledge of the intrinsic dimension. Our experiments cover a limited set of synthetic and structured scientific benchmarks, and do not yet establish scalability across models, datasets, or modalities. Future work will develop MaDeL for scalable manifold generative modeling; Sec. B discusses these limitations.

## B SCOPE OF THE ANALYSIS AND LIMITATIONS 

Our characterization is local and holds to leading order in the corruption level; finite corruption, strong curvature, manifold boundaries, and imperfect optimization can therefore mix the two com-ponents. The corruption distribution, the branch dimensions n∥ and n⊥, and the loss weights λ∥

and λ⊥ must be selected without knowledge of the intrinsic dimension, and the feature geometry requires a separate representation-training stage for each domain. Our experiments concern sparse structured distributions and single-step generators; broader validation across architectures, modali-ties, and scale remains to be established.

Dataset and generator. We use 3,673 SCOPe backbones containing between 60 and 128 

residues (Chandonia et al., 2022), matching the dataset used by RMF (Woo et al., 2026). Each back-bone is represented by residue-local rigid frames in SE(3) L. The generator follows the FrameFlow architecture (Yim et al., 2023a), using node and edge dimensions 256 and 128 , six invariant-point-attention blocks, eight attention heads, and self-conditioning. Both the baseline and MaDeL models use the RMF SplitMeanFlow objective, comprising a flow-matching anchor and a semigroup composition term. Rotational and translational losses are weighted by 1 and 2, respectively, with translations scaled by 0.1. The time floor is 0.01 , and both objective terms are clipped at 10 after 10 ,000 iterations. Models are trained with AdamW us-ing learning rate 10 −4, weight decay 0.01 , gradient clipping at 1.0, and EMA decay 0.9999 . Batches contain at most 54 proteins under a length-dependent memory budget. Although training runs for up to two million iterations, the reported steric evaluation uses the one-million-step checkpoints. 

Frozen protein encoder. The protein feature encoder is a split-latent U-Net over backbone frames with 64 feature planes, encoder depths [4 , 4, 4, 4] , and decoder depths [2 , 2, 2] . It is trained for 

250 ,000 iterations using batch size 2,048 , learning rate 10 −4, and equal clean- and corruption-prediction weights. Structured corruptions include rigid segment and loop displacements, chain breaks, and atom-level bond, angle, and torsion perturbations. Each of the 13 corruption operations is independently activated according to a base probability of 0.15 , producing approximately two corruptions per sample. The EMA encoder checkpoint is frozen during RMF training. The baseline sets the feature-semigroup weight to zero. The MaDeL model adds the decomposed feature semigroup term with weight 0.0029 while retaining the standard RMF objective. All other architecture, optimization, conditioning, and sampling settings are identical between the two runs.

0.1, which are refolded using ESMFold (Lin et al., 2023). Self-consistency RMSD is the minimum RMSD over the eight refolded sequences, and a backbone is considered designable when this value is below 2.0 ˚A.
