ABSTRACT 

Diffusion models are highly developed in continuous spaces for image and video domains. Recently, major advances have been made for discrete diffusion models for categorical data, specifically in the language domain. In contrast, diffusion models for discrete integer-valued data are less developed, despite the prevalence of this modality, ranging from images and music to gene counts. We introduce Jumping Up and Down (JUD)—a new family of denoiser-based diffusion models for discrete ordinal data. This is the first family of diffusion models for ordinal data which centers around training denoisers, which at the same time allows for bi-directional (up and down) perturbations of the data. The simplicity of the training objective, combined with the flexibility of bi-directional perturbations, leads us to obtain competitive results across different data modalities. 

1 INTRODUCTION 

Diffusion models for discrete ordinal data. The success of diffusion models in continuous do-mains such as image and video is driving an explosion of activity in applying diffusion models for discrete data. The vast majority of diffusion models for discrete data focus on language where the data is categorical , that is, a data modality with no natural order. In contrast, in many applications the discrete data also contains a natural order, i.e., the data is ordinal as its distribution is over (sub-sets of) ND or ZD . This modality naturally shows up in images (8-bit digital grayscale from 0 to 255), music (MIDI note numbers from 0 to 127), tabular data (amount in dollars of debt/credit), and in scientific applications ranging from biology (gene expression counts in cells) to chemistry (number of atoms in molecules). In addition to the aforementioned applications of discrete ordinal data, this modality is also interesting from the theoretical perspective as it lies in some sense be-tween categorical and continuous data: On the one hand, the data is discrete as in categorical data, while on the other hand the data has an intrinsic order as in continuous data in RD . Despite the importance of discrete ordinal data, the theory and practice of diffusion models for this modality are still developing. In this paper we contribute to both. 

Denoisers. The essence of diffusion models is learning clean data from progressively noisy ver-sions of the same data point. In continuous space, the noise is often taken to be Gaussian with the level of noise corresponding to the variance of the Gaussian. The choice of Gaussian noise is very convenient since it provides a clean relation between denoising and sampling . First, one learns the 

data denoiser ,

mdata (x, t ) := E[xdata | CorruptData (xdata , t ) = x], xdata ∼ pdata , (1) where CorruptData (xdata , t ) can be, for example, adding to xdata Gaussian noise with variance 

t. Second, the data denoiser mdata can be directly used for sampling via Tweedie’s formula , which 1arXiv:2610.02754v1 [cs.LG] 2 Oct 2026 connects the denoiser mdata to the score ∇ log pt, where pt is the distribution of t-level corrupted data. The validity of Tweedie’s formula is due to the use of Gaussian noise when corrupting the data. The advantages of formulating a training objective based on the denoiser were recently em-phasized in Li & He (2026); Hu et al. (2026). In contrast to the continuous setting, the absence of the Gaussian distribution in the discrete setting necessitates the development of a different concept of “data corruption”. For categorical data, the two prevailing choices for noise are uniform (choosing a token uniformly at random) or masking (adding an extra graveyard token), and the level of noise corresponds to the rate at which a token is flipped to a random or masked token. The lack of ordinal structure in categorical data has led to deviation from training a denoiser as in Equation (1), since the notion of average (expectation) does not make sense in such generality 1. Instead, the common training objectives in diffusion models for categorical data are either discrete scores (Meng et al., 2022; Lou et al., 2024), which are the analogues of ∇ log pt in the discrete setting, or the rates of the generative process (Campbell et al., 2022; Holderrieth et al., 2025). But for ordinal data, the definition of a denoiser analogous to (1) does make sense, and learning the denoiser has a number of advantages: • The denoiser can be trained with a large family of losses (Bregman divergences), including the simple mean-square error loss. Alternatively, the denoiser can be learned by training cross-entropy objectives for the marginal posteriors; see Section 2. • Techniques from continuous diffusion models, which are denoiser-based, can in principle be adapted to the discrete ordinal setting: EDM scaling (Karras et al., 2022), Distillation via Moment Matching (Salimans et al., 2024), dual score matching (Guth et al., 2025), blind denoisers (Sun et al., 2025; Wang & Du, 2025; Kadkhodaie et al., 2026), etc. See Section 3 for our use of the EDM scaling. In Figure 1 we compare various noising processes and methods for discrete diffusion models that are trained using the same set of 20 , 000 data samples from a bi-modal Negative Binomial Mixture dis-tribution; see Section F for more details. As we will demonstrate in this work, these improvements show up not only on toy examples but on real-world datasets as well. 

Jumping up and down In continuous spaces, the Gaussian noise blurs the data in all directions. For ordinal data, a data point in N or Z can be perturbed either up, or down, or both. As we will see below, uni-directional perturbations (either just up or just down) give rise to denoiser-based training and sampling (cf. Section D). That is, provided a suitable choice of up-perturbation, or down-perturbation, we can train an appropriate denoiser mdata and then use it to sample from the data distribution. But perturbing the data in only one direction can lead to inaccurate samplers. This is because once the sampler over/undershoots, due to inevitable approximation errors, the process cannot “undo” prior mistakes and correct the trajectory evolution. This is evident in Figure 1 when comparing Binomial-Poisson (one of our up-and-down methods), Binomial (only down) and Poisson (only up). Hence, our goal is to find perturbations and samplers which allow for up and down jumps, while at the same time being denoiser-based . This is a non-trivial task, as the up and down perturbations can interact in arbitrarily complex ways, which was not accomplished in previous works; for space reasons, related works are discussed in Section I. This is precisely the goal that we will achieve in this paper. 

Our contributions 

• We develop a novel framework of denoiser-based diffusion models (JUD) for discrete ordinal data on ND and ZD . Our key innovation is to train latent denoisers which predict 

partially noised data . Our main result (Theorem 2.1) shows that the latent denoisers are sufficient to sample from the data distribution via a stochastic process which allows for 

Up-and-Down moves. • We demonstrate the effectiveness of this framework on synthetic data and real-world data in domains such as images (CIFAR-10), and reasoning (Sudoku completion). In particular, we obtain state-of-the-art FID on CIFAR-10 among discrete diffusion models.

Comparing the processes Both the Binomial-Poisson process (for distributions over ND ) and the Poisson-Poisson process (for distributions over ZD ) are defined in terms of latent-up and 

latent-down denoisers. However, in the Binomial-Poisson process the latent variable Lt depends on the data xdata , while in the Poisson-Poisson process (L t, xdata ) are independent. We test the Binomial-Poisson model of Theorem 2.1 on the hard Sudoku benchmark of Deschenaux & Gulcehre (2026). Each puzzle has 30 visible digits and a unique solution. The dataset comprises 48,000 training and 2,000 validation puzzles. The metric is exact-board accuracy: all 81 predicted digits must match the reference solution. Note that this dataset is not ordinal since Sudoku is in-variant under permutations of the symbols. Our goal in this task is to explore the capabilities of our framework to general categorical data. 

Model and evaluation. We use the benchmark’s DiT backbone configuration. We use the same linear-time schedules as in CIFAR-10, with mean digit value 5. We train with two types of loss: 8(1) mean-square error (MSE) and (2) cross entropy (CE). MSE uses the same (L t, Nt) targets and affine preconditioning as CIFAR-10 (Tables 4 and 5); see Algorithm 1. CE instead learns a nine-class distribution for each digit, conditioned on the perturbed solution and context; see Algorithm 2. The denoiser(s) follow analytically from this posterior (Section H.2). 4 CONCLUSION 

In this work we introduce Jumping Up and Down (JUD) : a denoiser-based family of generative diffusion models for discrete ordinal data (either ND or ZD ) which allows for bi-directional per-turbations . Our main result (Theorem 2.1) shows that it suffices to learn latent denoisers in order to sample from the data distribution. Further, we show that theoretically a single denoiser suffices to sample from the data distribution, as the two latent denoisers can be expressed in terms of each other. In addition, we also prove that the data denoiser can be expressed in terms of the latent denoiser(s). We validate our methods on synthetic datasets, CIFAR-10, and the Sudoku completion task. In particular, on synthetic datasets we often obtain superior results over alternative discrete diffusion models, and we achieve a state-of-the-art result among discrete diffusion models for CIFAR-10. 95 LIMITATIONS 

(1) For the purpose of generative modeling considered in this work, the source distribution should be easy to sample (which is the case for us, as it is simply a Poisson or approximately Skellam). But in some applications (e.g., Fishman et al. (2026)) we would like to have an arbitrary source measure, which our method does not allow for. (2) Currently, our method requires a high number of neural function evaluations (NFE) for sampling. F.2 TRAINING 

Model. We evaluate all models using a common backbone model based on a residual MLP. A scalar input x ∈ R is first rescaled using EDM preconditioning by z = cin (t)x + sin (t) and linearly projected to a hidden width dmodel = 128 . The diffusion time t ∈ [T0, T ] is embedded via a sinusoidal timestep embedding with base frequency 10 4, and time-scale factor 100 to an embedding dimension dtemb = 128 . This is passed through a two-layer MLP (ReLU, hidden width 256) to produce a 4dtemb -dimensional conditioning vector. The main layers consist of 3 residual blocks with 256 hidden units followed by FiLM modulation. A method-specific head is applied to the final hidden state h ∈ R128 : a regression head for denoisers or a soft-plus for logit predictions. For the models that require two latent denoisers or probabilities to predict birth and death rates, we use a model with two heads that share the same hidden structure. 

Training. We use the Adam optimizer with the learning rate 2 × 10 −4, no weight decay, batch size 256 , gradient-norm clipped to 1.0, EMA decay 0.999 and train for 300 epochs. We compute the mean-squared error loss for the Binomial-Poisson model between the model’s prediction and the appropriate conditional-mean target, averaged over a batch of times t ∼ Unif( T0, T ) and one forward-noised sample per example. For the count-fm and the denoising score-entropy loss, the loss is based on the Kullback-Leibler divergence. F.3 SAMPLING

I RELATED WORKS 

Diffusion models: Continuous data Diffusion models in discrete time were first introduced in Sohl-Dickstein et al. (2015) with an evidence lower bound (ELBO) training objective. The method was then further developed in Ho et al. (2020); Song et al. (2021a), where the connection to de-noising score matching was made, with additionally better parameterizations and sampling, which ultimately led to a state-of-the-art (at the time) FID score of 3.17 on CIFAR-10. The progression of diffusion models to continuous-time as reverse-time stochastic differential equations was developed in Song et al. (2021b), which also unified diffusion models with the score matching with Langevin dynamics framework (Song & Ermon, 2019). Song et al. (2021b) wrote the training objective as denoising score matching, and lowered the FID score on CIFAR-10 to 2.20. In Karras et al. (2022), the design space for training the denoiser was explored systematically, which further brought down the FID to 1.97 on CIFAR-10. The EDM framework developed in Karras et al. (2022) is regularly used in state-of-the-art results for image generation. 

Discrete diffusion models: Categorical data Repeating history, the first diffusion models for cat-egorical data were developed in discrete time (Hoogeboom et al., 2021; Austin et al., 2021), while the continuous time formulation was later developed in Campbell et al. (2022). As for continuous data, the early versions of discrete diffusion models were trained using ELBO-based objectives, specifically training the posterior distribution (not the denoiser). Score-based objectives were devel-oped in Meng et al. (2022); Lou et al. (2024), and rate-based training was developed in Holderrieth et al. (2025). 38 Discrete diffusion models: Ordinal data Already at the origin of diffusion models, ordinal data appeared in the form of binary data (Sohl-Dickstein et al., 2015), with later developments in Bach & Saremi (2025); B´ en´ edict et al. (2023); Pham et al. (2025). In some biological applications, e.g., gene expression counts in single-cell RNA sequencing, recent works (Zhang et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026b; Bhattacharya et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026a) argued that it is beneficial to develop diffusion models that act directly on the discrete data, but these works neglect the ordinal structure and use categorical discrete diffusion models. The theory and practice for the ordinal data considered in this work, namely (subsets of) ND or ZD ,has only recently started to evolve. Let us begin with data on ND . One approach, developed in Chen & Zhou (2023); Montanari (2023); Bhattacharya et al. (2025), learns both a denoiser and a rate via a discrete Tweedie’s formula, but using perturbation of the form xdata 7 → Xt ∼ Poisson( t xdata ).This type of perturbation can only recover the clean data in the limit τ → ∞ , in contrast to our approach which recovers xdata at a final time. In addition, this perturbation can easily overshoot the effective support of the data. Alternative approaches (Santos et al., 2023; 2025; Shenfeld et al., 2026; Cheng et al., 2026) use only Binomial noise, which suffers from being a uni-directional perturbation. Another uni-directional perturbation was developed in Campbell et al. (2023). In Winkler et al. (2024); Wei & Pearson (2026), an up-down perturbation based on Binomial noise is developed. However, this formulation does not allow for a direct training of a denoiser. In Fishman et al. (2026), the Poisson-Poisson perturbation (6) is used, but the denoiser formulation of Theorem C.1 is lacking, which leads Fishman et al. (2026) to a distributional training objective. Similarly, the concurrent work Wei (2026) considers a Binomial-Poisson perturbation (7), but is also lacking a denoiser representation and trains using the generator matching framework (Holderrieth et al., 2025). To summarize, our work is the first to train a diffusion model for discrete ordinal data which allows for up-down perturbations, while training only denoisers. 39
