# ABSTRACT 

Diffusion models can reach useful sample quality before copying training exam-ples, but fast optimization can compress this generalization window by acceler-ating sample-specific fitting. We investigate this effect through update geome-try and propose Quality-Gated De-whitening (QGD), a controller that retains a fast polar-update prefix and progressively restores fixed-gain momentum. Our random-feature analysis separates covariance-controlled, curvature-equalized and amplitude-controlled memorization clocks. Under aligned spectral assumptions, it establishes a finite-exposure condition under which a fixed-gain tail recovers a delay proportional to dataset size. QGD implements this principle with a con-firmed quality gate, a bounded decay envelope and causal copy feedback. Imme-diate switching is the conservative limit; gradual control balances delayed copying against continued quality improvement. We pair QGD with Copy-Budgeted Se-lection (CBS), which applies simultaneous binomial calibration to a frozen check-point family, followed by a fresh evaluation of the released checkpoint. On 2,000-image CIFAR-10 subsets, QGD preserves the polar baseline’s quality-arrival time while expanding its useful interval by 8.32 × and reducing common-checkpoint copying by 75 .9% . With identical calibration and independent quality evaluation, QGD achieves FID 75 .56 versus 79 .37 for SGD with the same selector. Exposure-matched controls, independent detector audits and transfer to flow matching and dance generation support adaptive exposure control as a practical way to improve the quality–copying tradeoff. 

# 1 INTRODUCTION 

Diffusion models learn useful generative structure from finite training collections (Sohl-Dickstein et al., 2015; Song & Ermon, 2019; Ho et al., 2020), yet continued optimization can lead them to reproduce training examples (Carlini et al., 2023; Somepalli et al., 2023a). Quality and copying emerge at different times, creating a generalization window in which a model produces useful sam-ples before detectable memorization becomes prevalent (Bonnaire et al., 2025; Favero et al., 2026). This interval matters operationally: it provides room for further quality improvement and checkpoint selection. Reaching a quality target sooner is therefore one part of a successful training strategy; pre-serving useful choices after that point is another. Prior work establishes that optimizer choice changes these clocks. Bonnaire et al. (2025) compare Adam and momentum SGD, observing faster learning and memorization with Adam while retaining dataset-size-dependent delay. Learning-rate effects also influence memorization in denoising score matching (Wu et al., 2025). These findings motivate a more specific question: can update geometry 1arXiv:2610.02780v1 [cs.LG] 2 Oct 2026 change the mechanism governing the delay, and can that mechanism guide a controller that preserves fast initial learning? Answering it requires separating directional changes from update magnitude and measuring quality and copying along the same trajectories. Our random-feature analysis identifies three clocks. Gradient flow retains covariance-dependent rates that slow fitting in small-eigenvalue directions. Exact curvature preconditioning equalizes those rates. Aligned polar updates instead make fluctuation fitting depend on coefficient amplitude, so distributing fluctuation energy across more directions can shorten its clock. This rank-dependent compression gives a concrete control objective: limit polar exposure after useful population structure has been learned, then restore the small-gradient delay of a fixed-gain tail. Controlled spectra and directional interventions test this mechanism separately from neural architecture changes. We propose Quality-Gated De-whitening (QGD) to implement this objective. QGD begins with Muon, an existing optimizer that orthogonalizes matrix momentum (Jordan et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2025). A predefined quality target, confirmed by a subsequent probe, determines when control starts. Thereafter, causal copy feedback reduces the polar coefficient inside a deterministic decay envelope while preserving momentum and a fixed gain. The envelope bounds total subsequent polar exposure; feedback adapts its allocation to the observed trajectory. Avoiding per-step renormalization allows small-gradient directions to remain slow. Our recovery theorem gives a sufficient condition for a memorization delay proportional to dataset size: the fluctuation margin remaining at the gate must exceed subsequent polar exposure at the relevant scale. Immediate switching spends no additional polar exposure and provides the conser-vative endpoint of this analysis. QGD uses a gradual transition to retain quality improvement while delaying copying relative to continued polar training. We test this tradeoff against the same-gate hard switch and a pilot-fixed gradual schedule with matched exposure. Thus the theory motivates the controlled quantity, and the experiments test the practical value of its allocation. Optimizer transitions toward SGD are established (Keskar & Socher, 2017; Luo et al., 2019); MiMuon also adaptively selects Muon or momentum-SGD updates using a spectral criterion and practical norm proxy (Huang et al., 2026). QGD connects a confirmed generative-quality target and causal copying probes to a finite-exposure condition. Appendix 2 develops these relationships. This formulation makes a conservative switching baseline central to evaluation and tests feedback under varying copying dynamics rather than attributing every gain to adaptation. The resulting archive still requires a release decision. We pair QGD with Copy-Budgeted Selection (CBS), applying established exact binomial bounds and simultaneous calibration to a frozen check-point family (Clopper & Pearson, 1934; Angelopoulos et al., 2025). CBS chooses by development quality among eligible checkpoints, or abstains, while bounding the probability of release with a detector-budget violation. Fresh calibration never guides training, and an additional independent bank evaluates final quality. Figure 1 shows these distinct roles. Comparisons with valid fixed-sequence testing and preselection expose the quality–availability–cost tradeoff of searching more candidates. Across matched training conditions, QGD preserves Muon’s quality arrival, widens its useful in-terval, and improves independently evaluated quality under a common copy budget. Conservative baselines retain lower copying in some settings. Our contributions can be summarized as follows: • We develop an optimizer-geometric analysis of memorization in a random-feature de-noising model, distinguishing covariance-controlled, curvature-equalized, and amplitude-controlled clocks. The analysis identifies rank-dependent compression of memorization delay under aligned polar updates and motivates controlling their cumulative exposure. • We propose Quality-Gated De-whitening (QGD), which combines a confirmed quality gate and causal copying feedback to transition from fast polar updates to fixed-gain momentum. We establish a finite-exposure condition under which the aligned model recovers a memo-rization delay proportional to dataset size. • We introduce Copy-Budgeted Selection (CBS), a checkpoint-selection procedure that com-bines simultaneous binomial calibration over a frozen candidate family with quality-based selection. # 2 RELATED WORK 

Diffusion and score-based models learn generative processes through denoising (Sohl-Dickstein et al., 2015; Song & Ermon, 2019; Ho et al., 2020), with quality and cost shaped by objectives, noise schedules, architectures, guidance and samplers (Nichol & Dhariwal, 2021; Dhariwal & Nichol, 2021; Karras et al., 2022). Our paired protocol fixes these choices within each optimizer comparison, using a common pixel-space UNet despite broader latent and transformer designs (Rombach et al., 2022; Peebles & Xie, 2023). Diffusion models can reproduce training images (Carlini et al., 2023; Somepalli et al., 2023a), especially with small datasets, duplication and large models (Yoon et al., 2023; Gu et al., 2025; Kadkhodaie et al., 2024). We use the copy criterion of Yoon et al. (2023) and study how optimizer choice shifts the onset of detectable copying, complementing prior mitigation work (Somepalli et al., 2023b; Wen et al., 2024; Dutt et al., 2025). Bonnaire et al. (2025) identified an early quality time τgen and later memorization time τmem , with a window that widens with dataset size under gradient-based training. Related analyses study the same transition (Achilli et al., 2025; Aparicio Rodriguez et al., 2026), while Wang et al. (2026) shows that much diffusion behavior across data splits has a linear origin in empirical Gaussian statistics. We use the same tractable regime but ask how this quality-to-copy window depends on optimization geometry. Muon orthogonalizes matrix momentum through Newton–Schulz iterations (Jordan et al., 2024) and corresponds to spectral-norm steepest descent (Bernstein & Newhouse, 2024); related precon-ditioners include K-FAC (Martens & Grosse, 2015), Shampoo (Gupta et al., 2018), SOAP (Vyas et al., 2025), and diagonal Adam (Kingma & Ba, 2015; Loshchilov & Hutter, 2019). Recent work reports faster memorization under Muon than AdamW on small datasets and altered memorization– generalization transitions (Liu et al., 2026; Tveit et al., 2025; Huang et al., 2026). Optimizer transi-3tions such as SWATS, AdaBound and MiMuon adapt optimization over training (Keskar & Socher, 2017; Luo et al., 2019; Huang et al., 2026). QGD differs by using a quality-triggered continuous mixture tailored to diffusion: it preserves a fast prefix, limits polar exposure using copy feedback, and retains eigenvalue-dependent motion in the tail. Our finite-exposure analysis explains this mech-anism; related diffusion work also studies early stopping and large-step effects (Favero et al., 2026; Wu et al., 2025). CBS integrates detector-based checkpoint selection with exact binomial confidence intervals (Clop-per & Pearson, 1934) and development/calibration separation (Angelopoulos et al., 2025); Bonfer-roni correction controls selection across a frozen checkpoint family, while adaptive Learn-then-Test provides a sequential alternative (Zecchin et al., 2025). More broadly, mode-dependent learning dynamics in deep linear networks (Saxe et al., 2014; Advani et al., 2020), early stopping as regular-ization (Yao et al., 2007; Raskutti et al., 2014), random-feature models (Rahimi & Recht, 2007), and supervised memorization dynamics (Arpit et al., 2017) motivate viewing training time as part of the model. We connect these perspectives to diffusion and identify how whitening reshapes its useful stopping region.

# 6 DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSION 

Update geometry governs how quickly useful learning gives way to memorizing sample-specific de-tail. Finite polar exposure suffices to recover a spectral delay, linking optimizer geometry directly to the quality-to-copy interval. QGD turns this mechanism into quality-gated control: the hard switch suppresses copying most strongly, gradual QGD better preserves quality, and matched-exposure controls isolate the value of feedback rather than exposure reduction alone. With CBS fixed, an in-dependent final bank measures the quality available for release across optimizers, while calibration shows when a larger checkpoint family justifies its additional cost. Together, these results suggest that optimizer choice, stopping time, and release calibration should be considered jointly rather than as separate design decisions. 

Limitations. The theoretical delay guarantee assumes aligned, fixed features and idealized update dynamics. Extending the analysis to momentum, approximate orthogonalization, nonlinear feature learning, and representations that evolve throughout training would better capture modern diffusion systems. Our empirical study also focuses on a limited set of architectures, datasets, and optimizer families, so the magnitude of the observed effects may change at larger scales. CBS provides cov-erage only with respect to the specified copy detector and threshold (Appendix J); it does not rule out memorization under alternative notions of similarity or extraction. Finally, probe cadence and checkpoint-family size determine the generation and calibration cost (Table 3), leaving a practical tradeoff between finer selection and evaluation expense. 10 REFERENCES

# G IMPLEMENTATION AND EVALUATION PROTOCOL 

Data, Paired Trajectories and Model. CIFAR-10 inputs are scaled to [−1, 1] . Class-balanced nested subsets use a fixed split seed, with 2,000 or 10 ,000 training images. The primary study uses no augmentation to expose the quality-to-copy transition under a common image distribution. The 

10 ,000 held-out test images provide the fixed FID reference statistics. Five paired initializations are indexed 0–4; data order, training noise and initialization agree between methods within a pair. They are the unit of the reported mean and sample standard deviation. 25 The image model is a 6.47 M-parameter DDPM-style UNet with GroupNorm, SiLU, two residual blocks per level, attention at 16 × 16 , and no dropout. Training uses batch 128 , ϵ prediction, a 1,000 -step linear noise schedule, bf16 computation and fp32 optimizer state. The observation horizons are 

200 k and 400 k updates. EMA decay is 0.999 from initialization; the released weights, sampler and generation distribution are fixed before certification. 

Optimizer Conventions. AdamW uses (β1, β 2) = (0 .9, 0.999) and zero weight decay. Muon uses momentum 0.90 , Nesterov momentum, five Newton–Schulz iterations with coefficients 

(3 .4445 , −4.7750 , 2.0315) , and scale 0.2pmax( r, c ) for an r × c matrix. Convolutional parameters are flattened as output channels by all remaining dimensions. Biases, normalization parameters and time embeddings use AdamW at 2 × 10 −4 for every matrix-method comparison. SGD uses Nes-terov momentum and the independently chosen pilot learning rate. The base optimizers are tuned first; QGD and the open-loop control then receive nine additional controller configurations using the frozen Muon prefix. Matrix learning rates and branch thresholds are selected before evaluating the paired trajectories; Table 16 records the candidate menus and selected configurations. QGD preserves the Muon momentum buffer and its Nesterov convention. At the first update, the gain is the ratio of Muon-direction RMS to momentum-direction RMS, with ϵ = 10 −12 and a fallback of one if either RMS is below that value. The gain is fixed thereafter. Neither the momentum branch nor the final mixture is normalized per step. If α = 0 , the implementation skips the orthogonalization calculation while retaining the common momentum state. 

Causal Event Order. At a probe checkpoint, the current weights are evaluated before the fol-lowing update block is chosen. A crossing is confirmed only if the next probe also meets its cri-terion. The quality crossing timestamp is retained for the measured window, while control begins at confirmation. Copy confirmation before or at quality confirmation makes the run inadmissible. An unconfirmed final event remains unresolved. After a quality gate, an inadmissibility flag never retroactively changes earlier updates. Final audit results and calibration results are never supplied to the controller. 

Sampling and Quality Banks. DDIM uses 50 evenly spaced inference steps and η = 0 . Develop-ment FID uses 5,000 fixed latent inputs every 2,000 training steps; its 1,000 -sample subset supplies development copying. Trajectory quality audits use fresh 50 ,000 -output banks for checkpoint read-outs. Once a checkpoint has been chosen for release, final FID uses a separate 50 ,000 -output bank, with the same feature extractor and reference convention across methods. The independent audit is not used to select a different checkpoint. Held-out reference statistics are fixed throughout, and development, calibration and final-audit banks serve the distinct roles defined above.

# J FROZEN -FAMILY CALIBRATION AND SELECTOR COMPARISON 

The small-subset archive contains checkpoints at 2, 4, . . . , 200 k, fixed before certification. Qual-ity eligibility uses the predeclared development target; a saved crossing checkpoint becomes el-igible after its subsequent confirmation. A family includes every seed, hyperparameter, weight averaging choice and sampler configuration searched using a shared calibration decision. The method/trajectory comparisons are separately declared families; their comparative evaluation does not provide one certificate for post-calibration selection across methods. For each fixed family and bank, candidate k contributes a count Sk ∼ Binomial(5 ,000 , p k). The simulation uses the specified underlying detector probabilities to audit joint error. These underly-ing probabilities are not revealed to the controller or any selector. Counts are sampled once per candidate/bank and reused across competing selection rules. Each calibration repetition records the selected checkpoint or abstention, and the reported summaries aggregate release frequencies, joint violations and final-audit readouts.

Fixed-sequence testing orders the eligible family using development information alone. It applies level-δ tests in that fixed order and stops at the first failure, selecting the best development FID among the consecutively certified candidates. The order affects power but not validity; stopping at the first failure is essential (Angelopoulos et al., 2025). CBS instead constructs simultaneous upper bounds with per-candidate error δ/M and chooses the best development FID among all eligible certified candidates. Ties use the earlier checkpoint. The fixed sequence orders by ascending development-copy fraction, with earlier step breaking ties. Every method uses fresh calibration inputs independent of training and development. The guar-antee is for the joint event of release and detector-budget violation. It does not bound violation conditional on release and does not certify population FID. A subsequent model revision requires fresh calibration or a separately justified sequential procedure. Adaptive Learn-then-Test addresses a complementary setting with sequential sampling and early termination (Zecchin et al., 2025). 

Family Size and Uncertainty. All denominators for release and joint error include abstentions. Final FID and final audited copying average released checkpoints. The family-size comparison freezes a smaller, development-chosen candidate set before its calibration and updates the Bonfer-roni allocation accordingly. Repeated-bank error frequencies describe the declared fixture families; they do not replace Proposition 2’s distribution-free argument.
