A B S T R A C T
Background: Histopathology images are recognized as a reliable and comprehensive modality for
Deep Learning (DL) based diagnosis for a range of medical diseases. However, the effectiveness of
DL approaches is constrained by the limited availability of large-scale annotated datasets, as collecting
expert-labeled medical data is costly and time-intensive. Recent advances in generative models,
particularly diffusion models, have demonstrated strong capabilities in synthesizing medically relevant
content and supporting dataset augmentation. Existing generative frameworks treat histopathology
patches as generic natural images, failing to enforce biological constraints such as histomorphological
structures and heterogeneous staining patterns essential for diagnostically valid synthesis.
Methods: We propose a guided wavelet diffusion model (GWDM) framework that captures image
details at multiple scales using wavelets, and leverages controllable conditioning based on domain-
specific histopathological features (e.g., tissue patterns, color variations, etc.). We further formulate a
histomorphological guidance loss applied to both real and generated patches. A loss ramping strategy
that gradually introduces these constraints during training, ensuring stable optimisation and robust
modelling of tissue architecture.
Results: GWDM was evaluated on three benchmark datasets, including BCSS, MoNuSeg, and
PAIP2019, consistently outperforming state-of-the-art generative methods. Ablation studies con-
firmed that domain-guided conditioning, perceptual loss, and structural similarity loss each contribute
meaningfully to synthesis quality. Downstream nuclei and tissue segmentation experiments further
validated the clinical utility of GWDM-generated images, demonstrating consistent segmentation
performance improvements when synthetic data augments real training sets.
Conclusions: This work shows that domain-specific conditioning and dual-loss optimization generate
realistic histopathology patches, reducing data scarcity and improving clinical downstream segmen-
tation performance.
1. Introduction
Whole Slide Imaging (WSI) has enabled precise micro-
scopic analysis of tissue samples, aiding in accurate diag-
nosis, prognosis, and treatment [1]. However, WSI analysis
is typically performed on small fixed-size histopathology
patches, which remains challenging due to subtle variations
in tissue morphology, complex staining patterns, and the
high cost of expert annotation [2]. Deep learning-based
Computer-Assisted Diagnosis (CAD) algorithms have been
used to address these challenges and they have shown great
success in automatic analysis of histopathology [1, 3]. The
performance of these algorithms, however, greatly relies
on the availability of large-scale data, which is often dif-
ficult and expensive to obtain. The process of acquiring
histopathology images includes tissue collection through
biopsies or endoscopy, slicing the tissue into thin sections,
and mounting them on glass slides. The slides then un-
dergo staining, typically with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E),
for better visualization of cellular structures. Subsequently,
high-resolution imaging systems digitize the slides, creating
gigapixel-scale WSIs for in-depth analysis [4]. The intricate

Recently, wavelet transforms are incorporated into the
diffusion process to lower the data dimensionality, which
leads to the development of the Wavelet-Diffusion Model
(WDM) [12]. This approach reduces inference time by ap-
proximately fourfold compared to traditional DDPMs. This
combination maintained generative quality while signifi-
cantly reducing inference time. The wavelet transform de-
composes an image into multi-frequency components, pre-
serving spatial correspondence and directional properties.
It effectively extracts features and structures by separating
image content into frequency bands at various scales, im-
proving feature extraction [13]. Following this, Friedrich et
al. [14] applied WDM for high-resolution medical image
synthesis, reducing memory usage by transforming images
into the wavelet frequency domain and enabling spatial
dimensionality reduction. This approach is particularly ben-
eficial for generating high-resolution images, such as 3D
histopathology gigapixel images. However, WDM has inher-
ent limitations, performing decomposition beyond the first
level increases the likelihood of losing critical information.
Although deeper decomposition can improve computational
speed, it often results in a decline in image quality. WDM
[12], built upon a GAN backbone [6], leverages the gener-
ative strength of GANs during both training and inference.
However, its conventional approach of denoising Gaussian
noise without integrating domain-specific knowledge can
lead to a loss of structural and semantic consistency. This is
an important gap, where preserving structural and semantic
consistency is critical for accurate analysis. As such, in
this paper, we introduce a Guided Wavelet Diffusion Model
(GWDM), which incorporates histomorphological features
to guide the denoising process. This guidance ensures con-
sistency between real and generated patches, preserving
clinically relevant tissue structure (see Fig. 1). GWDM is
designed specifically for histopathology patch generation
to support reliable downstream clinical tasks. Our main
contributions are summarized as follows:
1) We propose a GWDM for generating high-resolution
histopathology images by conditioning the diffusion

2. RELATED WORK
2.1. Diffusion Model in Medical Imaging
Diffusion models have recently shown promising results
in medical imaging by generating high-quality images, al-
lowing better control during image synthesis and providing
more reliable uncertainty estimation compared to GAN-
based methods[15]. For example, Xu et al. [16] utilized
wavelet-based optimization within a diffusion framework
for sparse-view CT reconstruction, addressing the ill-posed
nature of incomplete data recovery. Similarly, Chen et al.
[17] proposed DDMM-Synth, leveraging denoising diffu-
sion models for cross-modal image synthesis, effectively re-
fining missing information and outperforming SOTA super-
vised methods under noisy or limited-projection conditions.
Özbey et al. [18] extended diffusion-based synthesis to un-
supervised cross-modal MRI translation, demonstrating that
score-based models can bridge modality gaps without paired
training data. Kim et al. [19] proposed a lesion-aware post-
training framework incorporating pixel-space objectives into
pre-trained LDMs for medical image-to-image translation,
demonstrating improved lesion delineation and overall im-
age quality for CT-to-MRI synthesis in acute ischemic stroke
patients. In dermatology, Farooq et al. [20] employed stable
diffusion models to generate high-quality and diverse syn-
thetic dermoscopic images, enhancing dataset variety and
addressing the scarcity of labeled skin lesion data. Further-
more, Akrout et al. [21] showed that diffusion-generated
synthetic skin images enhance the accuracy of classifier
when they were combined with real data.
2.2. Diffusion-Based Histopathology Image
Generation
Diffusion-based generative modeling has recently gained
significant attention in computational pathology. Yellapra-
gada et al. [22] introduced PathLDM, which combines tex-
tual descriptions with a latent diffusion model (LDM) [23]
through a two-stage encoding and diffusion pipeline for
histopathology image synthesis. Similarly, Nakhli et al. [24]
proposed NASDM, which generates realistic 𝐻&𝐸tissue
images from semantic instance masks of up to six nu-
clei types, improving controllability for nuclei segmentation
augmentation. More recently, Bhosale et al. [25] introduced
PathDiff, a diffusion-based framework that conditions gener-
ation on unpaired masks and diagnostic text, improving tis-
sue structure control, text-image alignment, and downstream
nuclei segmentation performance. Moghadam et al. [26]
used a diffusion-based framework focused on morphological
patterns and colour normalization to generate realistic brain
cancer histopathology images. Xu et al. [27] introduced a
hybrid diffusion architecture combining vision transformers
and diffusion autoencoders to better capture complex struc-
tural and textural features. Similarly, Porter et al. [28] de-
veloped conditional diffusion models with a dual-weighting
mechanism that preserves both perceptual tissue structures
and subtle statistical characteristics, improving the realism
of synthesised histopathology images.
2.3. Domain-Guided Generative Conditioning
Most diffusion models start from pure Gaussian noise,
leading to slow convergence and generic outputs [29, 30].
However, by introducing conditioning mechanisms address
these limitations by steering generation toward domain-
specific targets. For instance, Zhang et al. [31] introduced
ControlNet, which incorporates spatial conditioning signals
into pretrained diffusion models. It enables domain-specific
generation without disrupting the pretrained backbone. Sim-
ilarly, ProtoDiffusion [32] employs class prototypes as con-
ditioning inputs, which provides structured guidance to ac-
celerates convergence. Furthermore, Chambon et al. [33]
proposed presented a method leveraging radiology reports as
conditions for chest X-ray image synthesis. In parallel, Seg-
GuidedDiff [34] guides brain MRI generation using anatom-
ical segmentation masks at each sampling step, improving
the anatomical fidelity of the synthesized outputs. Moreover,
Moreover, Moghadam et al. [26] further extended this idea to
histopathology image generation, where genotype data were
used as guidance to enable morphologically and genomically
diverse synthesis. These works collectively motivate the
integration of domain knowledge into diffusion models, as
adopted in GWDM.

4. EXPERIMENTS
4.1. Datasets and Preprocessing
The proposed method was rigorously evaluated using
three benchmark histopathology image datasets: A multi-
organ nucleus segmentation challenge (MoNuSeg) [41], the
Breast Cancer Semantic Segmentation (BCSS) [42], and
the Pathology Artificial Intelligence Platform (PAIP) 2019
challenge dataset [43]. These datasets were utilized to quan-
titatively and qualitatively assess the model’s efficiency in
generating high-fidelity histopathology images.
4.1.1. BCSS dataset
The BCSS dataset [42] comprises H&E-stained WSIs
from breast cancer cases. Regions of interest (ROIs) were
initially annotated by 25 participants and subsequently vali-
dated by a senior pathologist to ensure accuracy. Each ROI
averages 1.18 mm2 (SD: 0.80 mm2) at 40× magnification
(0.25 µm/px). As part of preprocessing, WSIs were divided
into non-overlapping 256×256 pixel patches at 40× magni-
fication, ensuring consistent spatial resolution for analysis.
4.1.2. MoNuSeg dataset
The MoNuSeg dataset [41] comprises 37 histopathology
images of size 1000×1000 pixels, containing 21,623 expert-
annotated nuclear boundaries across seven organ types
(breast, liver, kidney, prostate, bladder, colon, and stomach).
Images were acquired at 40× magnification from individual
patients through TCGA [44], covering both benign and ma-
lignant tissue to ensure morphological diversity. For training
and testing, each image was partitioned into 256 × 256 pixel
patches.
4.1.3. PAIP2019 dataset
The PAIP 2019 dataset [43], developed as part of the
Liver Cancer Segmentation Challenge, provides a bench-
mark for evaluating both established and novel algorithms
in automated liver cancer detection using WSIs. The dataset
consists of H&E-stained WSIs scanned using an Ape-
rio AT2 scanner at 20× magnification (0.5021 𝜇m/pixel),
with image resolutions ranging from 35,855 × 39,407 to
64,768 × 47,009 pixels. For analysis, WSIs were partitioned
into non-overlapping 256×256 pixel patches at same magni-
fication, preserving consistent tissue detail.
4.2. Implementation details
The proposed framework was developed using PyTorch
and equipped with a three-NVIDIA RTX A5500 GPU clus-
ter for model training across each dataset, while inference
and evaluation were performed using a single GPU. For

6. Downstream Segmentation
To evaluate the clinical utility of the proposed generative
framework, we conduct downstream segmentation experi-
ments on two benchmark histopathology datasets: MoNuSeg
and BCSS. Three widely used segmentation architectures
with different inductive biases are considered: DeepLabV3+
[52], UNet++ [53], and SegFormer [54]. All encoders are
pretrained using MoCoV2 with a ResNet-50 backbone. Each
model is trained under three settings: (i) real images only,
(ii) real image combined with 10k synthetic images, and (iii)
real image combined with 50k synthetic images generated by
the proposed GWDM. Performance is evaluated using Dice
score, mIoU, and pixel accuracy (pAcc) on held-out test sets
under identical augmentation and optimisation settings.
MoNuSeg. As shown in Table 4, incorporating GWDM
generated synthetic data consistently improves segmentation
performance across all three architectures. UNet++ achieves
the highest overall performance, reaching a Dice of 0.956
and mIoU of 0.917 with 50k synthetic images, compared to
0.954 and 0.914 with real data only. DeepLabV3+ shows
the most notable gain, improving from Dice 0.931 to 0.936
(+0.5%) and mIoU from 0.872 to 0.882 (+1.0%) with 50k
synthetic augmentation. SegFormer similarly improves from
0.926 to 0.930 in Dice. These consistent gains across all
architectures confirm that GWDM-generated synthetic data
effectively enriches nuclear morphology representation, par-
ticularly beneficial in data-limited settings.
BCSS. On the more challenging five-class BCSS dataset
(see Table 4), DeepLabV3+ benefits most from synthetic
augmentation, improving Dice from 0.738 to 0.745 and
mIoU from 0.599 to 0.606 with 50k synthetic images.
UNet++ improves from Dice 0.719 to 0.730 (+1.1%) and
mIoU from 0.575 to 0.588 (+1.3%) with 10k synthetic
images, demonstrating that even limited synthetic augmen-
tation yields meaningful gains. SegFormer shows marginal
improvement, suggesting that transformer-based architec-
tures may require larger synthetic volumes to fully benefit
from augmentation. Overall, results confirm that GWDM-
generated synthetic data consistently improves segmenta-
tion, particularly under data-limited conditions where an-
notated samples are scarce.

7. Discussion
Our findings show that the proposed GWDM consis-
tently outperforms conventional generative models across
multiple histopathology datasets. It achieves state-of-the-art
or competitive performance among diffusion-based meth-
ods. The generated images show high fidelity, realistic tex-
tures, and strong structural consistency (Fig. 3). The key dis-
tinction from standard diffusion baselines lies in the domain-
informed measurement operator at the core of GWDM.
This operator explicitly encodes three types of features:
morphological features (nuclei density, edges, and texture),
multi-scale spatial features spanning fine cellular detail to
broader tissue architecture, and staining features that stan-
dardise H&E contributions. Together, these guide the model
to emphasise biologically meaningful tissue structures at
each denoising step. As a result, the model avoids learning
generic image statistics. This targeted conditioning improves
robustness to variations in resolution and tissue composi-
tion. It also mitigates common failure modes such as mode
collapse and structural inconsistency [6, 8], yielding more
stable convergence and stronger generalization.
Quantitative results in Table 2 confirm these obser-
vations across all three benchmarks. On BCSS, GWDM
achieves the lowest FID and KID among all compared
methods. This indicates that its samples most closely fol-
low the real tissue distribution while maintaining strong
diversity. On MoNuSeg, the dataset is relatively homo-
geneous, comprising densely packed nuclei with uniform
morphology. Despite this limited scope for differentiation,
GWDM remains competitive on distribution-level metrics.
On PAIP2019, GWDM achieves the lowest KID, demon-
strating the strongest statistical consistency with real data
across cancer subtypes. These consistent trends confirm
the generalization of GWDM and also validate that the
domain-specific priors help the model learn biologically
meaningful representations rather than superficial image
statistics. Qualitative results are shown in Fig. 3. GWDM
faithfully reproduces nuclei organisation, staining balance,
and tissue architecture. It also normalises colour varia-
tion effectively. Breast-cancer samples exhibit distinct cel-
lular boundaries nearly indistinguishable from real im-
ages (Fig. 3a). Liver-cancer samples show compact, well-
defined structures (Fig. 3b). MoNuSeg samples display
complex cellular boundaries (Fig. 3c). The clinical rele-
vance of GWDM extends beyond generation quality. This
was validated through downstream segmentation exper-
iments. GWDM-synthesised patches were used to aug-
ment a self-supervised pretraining and limited-label fine-
tuning pipeline. As reported in Table 4, consistent gains
in mIoU, Dice, and pixel accuracy were observed across
DeepLabV3+, UNet++, and SegFormer. These gains con-
firm that the structural fidelity of GWDM-generated images
translates directly into improved segmentation generalisa-
tion. This is particularly significant in digital pathology,
where large-scale annotated datasets remain costly to ob-
tain [55]. To further investigate the impact of the proposed
synthetic augmentation, we stratified the test images into
Easy and Hard subsets based on the median per-image
IoU obtained by the real-only DeepLabV3+ baseline. The
results, summarized in Table 5, show that the proposed
method consistently improves segmentation performance

across both subsets. Notably, the largest gains are observed

to high-fidelity histopathology image generation, providing
a strong foundation for synthetic data augmentation in
computational pathology workflows.
8. Conclusion
In this work, we introduced GWDM, a guided wavelet
diffusion framework for histopathology image generation.
It incorporates domain-specific tissue priors directly into
the diffusion process. Specifically, GWDM integrates mul-
tiscale morphological, spatial, and staining features. These
are combined with wavelet-based parameterisation and a
dual perceptual-structural loss. Together, they guide the
model toward biologically meaningfulreconstruction. The
result is high-frequency cellular detail, stain consistency,
and strong structural fidelity. Experiments were conducted
across three benchmarks: BCSS, MoNuSeg, and PAIP2019.
GWDM achieved consistent improvements in FID, KID,
pFID and path-FID across all datasets. The generated im-
ages exhibit realistic tissue textures and nuclear organ-
isation that closely align with true pathological appear-
ance. Downstream segmentation experiments further vali-
date these findings. GWDM-synthesised images consistently
improved performance across DeepLabV3+, UNet++, and
SegFormer. This confirms their practical value for training
robust segmentation models in clinical digital pathology
settings.
