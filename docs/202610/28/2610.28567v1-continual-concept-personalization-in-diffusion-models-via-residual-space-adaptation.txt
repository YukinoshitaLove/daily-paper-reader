Models via Residual-Space Adaptation

Abstract
Text-to-image personalization aims to equip pretrained diffusion models with the
ability to learn user-specific concepts from only a few reference images. Existing
methods, however, typically train each concept independently or assume that all
concepts are available simultaneously. In practical applications, concepts often
arrive sequentially, requiring models to continually learn new subjects, objects,
or styles while minimizing interference with previously acquired concepts. We
address this continual concept personalization setting by extending residual-space
adaptation to the stochastic adaptation process of pretrained diffusion models. Our
work achieves the highest concept fidelity among competing continual personal-
ization methods while maintaining near-zero forgetting. Beyond these empirical
gains, the learned residual-space dynamics reveal where adaptation capacity is con-
centrated within the diffusion model. Restricting adaptation to the identified layers
preserves nearly the same performance while reducing the number of trainable
parameters by up to 72%, demonstrating that residual-space dynamics can identify
the layers that contribute most to continual concept personalization and directly
guide more parameter-efficient adaptation.

Introduction
Recent text-to-image models such as Stable Diffusion [15, 17] and FLUX [11] have enabled high-
quality image synthesis from natural language descriptions, making generative models increasingly
accessible to end users. Their flexibility has further motivated personalization methods that adapt
pretrained models to user-specific concepts, subjects, objects, or artistic styles from only a few
examples.
Personalization methods commonly rely on parameter-efficient fine-tuning techniques such as
LoRA [9] to adapt pretrained diffusion models to new concepts. Most existing approaches [7,
10, 14, 21] perform a separate fine-tuning procedure for every concept, and subsequently merge the
resulting adapters into a unified personalized model. In many practical scenarios, however, concepts
are acquired sequentially over time as new subjects, objects, or styles become available. Under this
setting, whenever a new concept is introduced, the expensive merging procedure must be repeated to
reconstruct the final personalized model. As the number of concepts grows, this repeated merging
process becomes increasingly inefficient. This motivates continual concept personalization, where
a single model is updated incrementally to incorporate new concepts while preserving previously
acquired ones. The main obstacle to continual concept personalization is interference: learning
new concepts can degrade performance on previously learned concepts. At the same time, the fixed
model capacity must be used parsimoniously to preserve sufficient adaptation capacity for future
concepts. Several continual learning methods [1, 4, 12, 19] achieve low levels of interference by
constraining the parameter update to a pre-computed safe subspace. The most successful among

Contributions.
Our contributions are as follows:
• We extend residual-space adaptation from deterministic language models to stochastic diffusion
training, providing a new competitive option for continual concept personalization that mitigates
interference while maintaining adaptivity at low cost.
• We introduce a systematic evaluation framework for continual personalization that captures concept
fidelity, going beyond judging only image-generation quality.
• We show that residual-space dynamics can serve as an efficient probe for identifying the layers that
contribute most to continual concept adaptation, providing insights into layerwise specialization
and guiding more targeted parameter-efficient fine-tuning strategies.

Related Work
2.1
Diffusion Personalization
Text-to-image personalization has been widely studied for adapting pretrained diffusion models to
user-specific identities, objects, or styles from only a few examples. Early approaches such as Textual
Inversion [6] learn new concepts in the text embedding space while keeping the diffusion model
fixed. DreamBooth [18] improves personalization quality by fine-tuning the diffusion model using a
prior-preservation objective. Subsequent methods such as Custom Diffusion [10] restrict adaptation
to selected cross-attention layers, improving parameter efficiency while supporting multi-concept
customization. More recent personalization methods increasingly adopt parameter-efficient fine-
tuning techniques such as LoRA. Mix-of-Show [7] introduces ED-LoRA together with structured
concept tokens for multi-concept personalization, while LoRACLR [21] improves composition
through contrastive alignment of independently trained adapters.
Another line of work reduces interference through the design of the adaptation space itself. Orthogonal
Adaptation (OA) [14] constructs approximately orthogonal LoRA subspaces, allowing independently
trained adapters to be combined through simple summation with minimal interference. Although
originally proposed in a non-continual setting, its orthogonal structure naturally lends itself to
sequential adaptation. Overall, most existing personalization methods assume that all concepts
are jointly available during training or are learned independently and combined afterward, and are
therefore not explicitly designed for continual settings.
2.2
Continual Adaptation
Continual learning methods [8] operate in a sequential training fashion, aiming to preserve knowledge
and plasticity over time. Classical approaches rely on regularization, replay, or gradient projection
mechanisms to mitigate catastrophic forgetting [23].
Within diffusion adaptation, C-LoRA [22] extends Custom Diffusion to a sequential setting by
introducing regularization to preserve earlier concepts. Orthogonal Adaptation [14] can also be
naturally extended to continual settings through additive composition of orthogonal adapters.
Several methods constrain the parameter update to a safe subspace based on the layer-wise input
statistics. Among these, Gradient Projection Memory (GPM) [19] estimates layer-wise feature
covariance from previous tasks and constrains the new updates to its low-energy subspace. The
more recent InfLoRA [12] applies the same strategy to LoRA-based fine-tuning, where each task is
learned within a fixed low-rank budget. Building upon InfLoRA, Barinka et al. [1] propose a dynamic
rank allocation method with simplified updates which allows for significant capacity savings. In
addition to providing theoretical guarantees for mitigating forgetting, the method demonstrates strong
empirical performance on challenging continual language model adaptation tasks.
2


Although effective, these approaches have been studied primarily in deterministic architectures.
Whether residual-space methods remain effective in stochastic diffusion training, where layer statistics
are influenced by sampled noise, remains unexplored.
2.3
Evaluation of Personalized Generative Models
Evaluating personalized generative models typically involves measuring text alignment, image
similarity, and identity preservation. Text alignment is commonly assessed using CLIP-based [16]
text-image similarity, which measures whether generated images are semantically consistent with
their prompts. Image alignment metrics compare generated images with reference examples, often
using CLIP image embeddings. Several works additionally evaluate identity preservation through
specialized recognition models such as ArcFace [3]. However, these metrics are mainly designed for
human faces and do not generalize to arbitrary objects.
Beyond specialized identity metrics, a variety of pretrained embedding models have been used to
assess perceptual similarity. CLIP [16] is trained on large-scale image–text pairs and provides repre-
sentations that emphasize global semantic correspondence between images and language. In contrast,
DINO-based models [2, 13, 20] learn self-supervised visual representations without text supervision
and preserve fine-grained visual structure and local semantic information. DreamSim [5] combines
multiple pretrained visual embeddings together with human similarity annotations, leveraging both
global semantic understanding and fine-grained visual details to produce perceptual representations
that closely align with human judgments.
Overall, most existing evaluation protocols are designed for static personalization settings and do not
measure identity preservation or forgetting over time.
3
Evaluating Continual Performance
Evaluating continual text-to-image personalization requires measuring not only the quality of gen-
erated images but also how well previously learned concepts are preserved throughout sequential
adaptation. Unlike discriminative tasks, where forgetting can be directly quantified through accuracy,
there is no canonical notion of forgetting for generative models. As discussed in Section 2.3, existing
evaluation protocols are primarily designed for static personalization settings and focus on aggregate
measures of text or image alignment. In order to assess the degradation of the concept identity over
time, it is important to have metrics that distinguish concept fidelity from general image quality. To
address this problem, we first introduce a concept-level fidelity measure that quantifies how faithfully
a personalized concept is preserved. We then derive continual evaluation metrics directly from this
quantity.
3.1
Concept Fidelity
Our objective is to evaluate the personalized concept itself rather than the similarity between individual
images. A generated image may differ substantially from its reference in pose, background, or artistic
style while still faithfully depicting the same concept. We therefore introduce concept fidelity, which
measures similarity at the concept level rather than the image level.

Residual-Space Adaptation in Diffusion
We build upon the residual-space adaptation framework DyRA [1], originally designed for LLMs,
which constructs layer-wise adaptation subspaces from the input representations. Applying this
framework to diffusion models is nontrivial, as these representations depend not only on the input
concept but also on the randomly sampled diffusion noise and denoising timestep. We therefore
first review the residual-space adaptation procedure and then develop a stochastic interpretation that
justifies applying the framework to diffusion models and motivates our implementation.

5
Stable Continual Personalization
This section evaluates whether residual-space adaptation can be successfully extended to continual
diffusion personalization. We first assess its ability to preserve concept fidelity while mitigating
forgetting throughout continual learning, and then examine scalability and capacity preservation.
5.1
Experimental Setup
We conduct all experiments on Concept101 [10], a benchmark containing 101 concepts spanning
17 semantic classes, including visually similar concepts, making it particularly suitable for studying
interference and identity preservation in continual learning. All concepts are learned sequentially in
a rehearsal-free setting, where only the current concept data are available during training. Unless
otherwise stated, all methods follow the same randomly shuffled task ordering. Throughout the
experiments, we denote DyRA configured with the sigular value threshold τ as DyRA-τ. We compare
DyRA with τ ∈{0.5, 1.0} against three continual LoRA baselines: Trainable-AB, which optimizes
both LoRA projection matrices A, B and corresponds to a continual variant of Mix-of-Show [7];
Rank-Fixed-B, which optimizes only the LoRA up-projection A while keeping the randomly
initialized down-projection B fixed; and OA, the continual version of Orthogonal Adaptation [14].
Unless otherwise specified, all methods adapt the linear projections in the transformer blocks of
both the text encoder and the UNet, and all fixed-rank methods use rank r = 4. All experiments are
conducted on Stable Diffusion v1.5 [17]. Models are periodically evaluated throughout continual
training. For each concept, images are generated using only the learned concept token as the prompt,
without additional textual descriptions, thereby isolating the learned concept representation and
enabling a direct comparison of concept fidelity across methods. At the end of training, we further
validate the model using diverse prompt templates. Additional implementation details and training
hyperparameters are provided in Appendix C.

5.3
Dynamic Rank Improves Parameter Efficiency
The performance gains of DyRA stem not only from restricting adaptation to the residual space,
but also from allocating adaptation capacity according to the residual-space structure of each layer.
We therefore compare DyRA with the fixed-rank OA under different trainable parameter budgets to
evaluate the effectiveness of dynamic rank allocation.
Figure 5 compares concept fidelity against the number of trainable parameters for OA with differ-
ent fixed ranks and DyRA with different singular-value thresholds. Each point corresponds to a
different parameter budget. Across the entire range of budgets, DyRA consistently achieves higher
concept fidelity than OA with comparable numbers of trainable parameters, demonstrating a superior
performance–parameter trade-off.
Notably, even under high singular-value thresholds, where DyRA has substantially fewer trainable
parameters, it outperforms OA with considerably larger parameter budgets. These results indicate that
allocating rank adaptively according to the residual-space structure is substantially more parameter-
efficient than assigning a uniform rank to every layer, allowing DyRA to maintain strong concept
fidelity under a reduced parameter budget.

Adaptation Capacity Concentration
We first analyze how DyRA distributes adaptation capacity across the adapted layers by averaging
the allocated ranks over all 101 concepts. Figure 6 illustrates the average rank allocated to each layer.
The x-axis indexes the adapted layers in architectural order, and the corresponding module names are
provided in Appendix D.
Rather than allocating the same rank to every layer, DyRA exhibits clear layer-dependent allocation
patterns that closely follow the repeated transformer architecture. In the text encoder, the allocated
ranks exhibit a four-layer periodicity that exactly mirrors the repeated key(K), value(V), query(Q), and
output projection layers within each transformer block. A similar correspondence is observed in the
UNet, where every group of eight adapted layers corresponds to the self-attention and cross-attention
projections within a transformer block. Within every block, the two dominant rank peaks consistently
coincide with the cross-attention key(K) and value(V) projection layers, which repeatedly receive
substantially larger ranks than all other projections.
The repeated concentration of residual rank in the UNet cross-attention K,V projections indicates
that these layers consistently consume more adaptation capacity than other adapted modules during

6.3
Layer-Guided Parameter-Efficient Adaptation
The analyses in Sections 6.1 and 6.2 consistently identify the UNet cross-attention K,V projections as
the layers that require the largest residual adaptation subspaces throughout continual learning. This
suggests that these layers differentiate newly learned concepts through distinct adaptation directions.
If this interpretation is correct, restricting adaptation to the identified layers would be expected to
preserve most of the continual personalization performance while substantially reducing the number
of trainable parameters.
Table 3 supports this hypothesis. Restricting adaptation to only the UNet K,V projections reduces
the trainable parameter count by approximately 60% (67.56M →27.12M) while achieving virtually
identical concept fidelity (Q = 0.6496 vs. 0.6497) and comparable forgetting. Restricting adaptation
further to only the UNet cross-attention K,V projections reduces the trainable parameter count by
approximately 72% (67.56M →18.87M), yet still preserves nearly all continual personalization
performance in practice.
Beyond validating the proposed interpretation, these results also provide a structural explanation for
previous personalization methods such as Custom Diffusion [10] and C-LoRA [22], which restrict
adaptation to cross-attention K,V projections based primarily on empirical observations of large
weight updates during training. In contrast, residual-space dynamics explain the same design choice
from the perspective of feature-space utilization, providing a principled interpretation of why these
layers are particularly effective adaptation targets.
More broadly, residual-space dynamics provide a principled way to identify where adaptation capacity
is most effectively utilized. Rather than relying on empirical heuristics or exhaustive layerwise search,
the proposed analysis directly guides targeted adaptation, enabling substantially more parameter-
efficient continual personalization while preserving nearly the same overall performance.

Conclusion
Limitations and Future Directions.
Although DyRA achieves strong continual personalization
performance, several limitations remain. First, the adaptation capacity of residual-space methods is
fundamentally bounded by the width of individual layers. As continual learning progresses, layers
with consistently high adaptation demand may eventually exhaust their available residual subspaces,
limiting scalability to arbitrarily long task sequences. Second, the method is implemented only
on Stable Diffusion v1.5 and Concept101. Nevertheless, the residual-space analysis developed
in this work is considerably more general than the specific adaptation algorithm. Since it relies
11


only on layer input statistics and residual-space dynamics, it can potentially be applied to other
diffusion backbones, transformer-based generative models, or broader neural architectures to identify
the layers that contribute most to learning new tasks. Exploring these adaptation patterns across
architectures may provide further insights into representation specialization and guide the design of
future parameter-efficient adaptation methods.
Key Takeaways.
Our work shows that residual-space adaptation extends naturally to stochastic
diffusion models, enabling continual concept personalization with stable performance, negligible
interference, and strong parameter efficiency. Beyond its empirical performance, the residual-space
dynamics observed during continual adaptation reveal how adaptation demand is distributed across
the adapted layers. By identifying the layers that consistently require new concept-specific adaptation
directions, it provides a structural interpretation of existing adaptation strategies and directly guides
more parameter-efficient adaptation. We hope this perspective motivates future work that uses
adaptation dynamics not only to improve continual learning algorithms, but also to better understand
layer specialization in modern generative models.

Embedding Metric Comparison
We quantitatively compare the embedding spaces used to compute concept fidelity in the main text.
The goal of this comparison is to measure whether an embedding model captures concept-level
identity rather than only low-level visual similarity. A suitable embedding should map images of the
same personalized concept close to one another while separating images from different concepts.
We evaluate this property using a clustering protocol on the ground-truth reference images. Given
image embeddings, we define the pairwise clustering distance as
d(i1, i2) = 1 −sim(i1, i2),
(10)
where sim(·, ·) denotes the cosine similarity in the corresponding embedding space. We cluster all
ground-truth images into 101 clusters, matching the number of personalized concepts. To make the
comparison deterministic and reduce sensitivity to random initialization, the cluster centroids are
initialized using one image from each ground-truth concept.
We report clustering accuracy and Adjusted Rand Index (ARI) with respect to the ground-truth
concept labels. Clustering accuracy is computed as the fraction of images assigned to their correct
concept cluster. ARI measures the agreement between the predicted clustering and the ground-truth
partition based on pairwise co-clustering decisions, adjusted for chance. Higher values indicate that
the embedding space better recovers the underlying concept structure.

Implementation Details
We provide additional implementation details for the continual personalization experiments in the
main text.
Dataset and task order.
We randomly shuffle the 101 concepts once and use this fixed task order
for all methods and random seeds in the main experiments. This avoids presenting concepts in
semantic clusters, such as all visually similar instances from the same class arriving consecutively.
During each task, the dataloader contains only the reference images for the current concept. To check
robustness to task ordering, we additionally repeat all experiments with two other independently
shuffled concept orders and observe results consistent with those reported in the main text.
Backbone and adapted layers.
We use Stable Diffusion v1.5 [17] as the pretrained text-to-image
backbone. The model uses a CLIP ViT-L/14 text encoder with 12 transformer blocks and a UNet
with 16 cross-attention blocks. LoRA modules are inserted into the query, key, value, and output
projections of the attention layers in both the text encoder and the UNet. This yields 48 adapted
projection layers in the text encoder and 128 adapted projection layers in the UNet.
Training configuration.
Each concept is trained for 400 gradient-update steps. Since each concept
has only a small number of reference images, the concept dataset is cycled during training. We train
with fp16 mixed precision on two NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPUs. All methods are run with three global
random seeds, {0, 42, 619}. The training prompt for concept c follows the dual-token format
“a photo of ⟨c1⟩⟨c2⟩”.

Residual-space construction.
Layer inputs for subspace estimation are collected before training
each new task using the same stochastic inputs as diffusion training: Gaussian noise is sampled for
each image and the denoising timestep is sampled uniformly.
