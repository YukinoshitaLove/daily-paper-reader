# Training-free Acceleration for Blind Compressed Image Restoration via Activation-Calibrated Singular Value Decomposition 

Suhang Yao, Xin Li, Xijun Wang, Yufei Wang, Yeying Jin, and Zhibo Chen 

Abstract —Transformer-based models achieve strong perfor-mance on blind compressed image restoration (CIR) under unknown compression settings, but their densely stacked projec-tion operators make deployment costly. We study training-free acceleration for such models through an empirical, operator-level analysis. Specifically, we profile where computation is concentrated, characterize channel-wise activation distributions under compressed inputs, and examine the singular-value decay of representative projection weights. These measurements identify the recurring projections in the attention, feed-forward, convolu-tional, and prompt-conditioning pathways as effective low-rank replacement targets that preserve the original inference graph. They further establish truncated SVD (TSVD) as a principled training-free baseline for blind CIR and reveal why a uniform low-rank objective is mismatched to the channel-imbalanced feature distributions of compressed images. Accordingly, we formulate Activation-Calibrated Singular Value Decomposition (ASVD) for this restoration setting, deriving low-rank factors 

entirely offline from pretrained weights and lightweight activation statistics gathered from only a few compressed calibration images. By weighting the decomposition with channel-wise activation magnitudes, our ASVD assigns larger penalties to errors on more active channels, requiring neither retraining nor online calibration and leaving the restoration pipeline intact. When instantiated on PromptCIR at rank 64, ASVD consistently improves PSNR-B over TSVD across four standard benchmarks under identical parameter and MAC budgets. At this operating point, the factorized model cuts parameters by 59% (34 .75 M → 14 .34 M) and computation by 

16 .1% (258 .79 → 217 .16 GMACs for a 256 × 256 input), enabling acceleration without retraining. 

Index Terms —Singular value decomposition, activation cali-bration, low-rank approximation, model compression, inference acceleration, blind compressed image restoration

A. Compressed Image Restoration 

Compressed image restoration (CIR) targets artifacts caused by quantization and transform loss. Early approaches relied on transform-domain priors or dual-domain modeling [7], using handcrafted assumptions to recover information lost to compression. CNN-based methods later pushed CIR performance further. ARCNN [8] demonstrated the effectiveness of convolutional networks for artifact reduction, while dual-domain designs such as D3 [9] and DMCNN [10] exploited complementary representations in the pixel and transform domains, following the residual learning paradigm [11]–[14]. Attention-enhanced CNN restorers further improved representation capacity through channel and non-local modeling [15], [16], while quantization-guided designs injected JPEG side information [17]. More recently, transformer-based architectures have become popular restoration backbones, owing to the global modeling capability of self-attention [18]. Representative models such as SwinIR [19], Uformer [2], Restormer [3], and HAT [4], [20] handle long-range dependencies and complex artifact patterns effectively, while Stripformer [21] and NAFNet [22] improve efficiency through structured attention or simplified operators. State-space restoration backbones such as MambaIR [23] offer another route to efficient long-range modeling. This progression has been evaluated across diverse restoration benchmarks and datasets [1], [24]–[27]. However, the gains of these backbones come largely from stacking high-dimensional projection and attention operators, whose cost grows rapidly with feature width and token length, making efficient deployment a recurring concern. The blind CIR setting poses additional challenges, because the compression artifacts are unknown and spatially varying. To improve robustness, prior work incorporates auxiliary cues or representation learning strategies, including quality-factor-aware modeling [28] and contrastive representation learning [29]. More recently, prompt-based conditioning has emerged as an effective way to handle diverse degradations, with prompt-conditioned transformers proving effective in both all-in-one and blind CIR settings, including Promp-tIR [30], PromptCIR [5], ProRes [31], PromptRR [32], Prompt-in-Prompt [33], DA-CLIP [34], and visual or multimodal prompt learning methods [35]–[37]. Related compressed-image restoration and super-resolution efforts, including UCIP [38], MambaCSR [39], and CODiff [40], further show the importance of compression-aware conditioning. Such prompt conditioning introduces additional projection pathways that interact with the backbone, further enlarging the operator budget that any acceleration method must address. 

B. Model Compression and Acceleration 

Model efficiency has been studied extensively through pruning, quantization, and low-rank approximation [41]–[44]. Among these, low-rank approximation offers a principled and training-free route to reducing computation, grounded in the classical Eckart–Young theorem [6]. Classical CNN acceleration methods showed that convolutional redundancy can be exploited through low-rank or tensor factorization [45]–[47]. Recent works revisit truncated SVD for large neural networks and propose truncation-aware [48] or importance-weighted and Fisher-weighted variants [49] to improve approximation fidelity. A shared insight is that raw weight-reconstruction error is an incomplete objective, since channels contribute unequally to the layer output; this motivates activation- or importance-aware reweighting of the decomposition objective. In parallel, training-free acceleration techniques for iterative generative models exploit feature reuse and operator-level ap-proximation. Representative examples include DeepCache [50], block-wise caching [51], and channel-aware SVD acceleration for diffusion transformers [52]. Other cache-based or training-free variants further explore layer caching, token-wise feature reuse, fast-forward caching, and delta updates [53]–[57]. III. M OTIVATION AND DESIGN RATIONALE 

We derive the projection-level SVD design from an empirical analysis of transformer-based blind CIR. The analysis is organized around three questions: which operators dominate the cost, whether their weights admit a compact low-rank approximation, and whether compressed-image activations justify channel-aware weighting. The answers determine our method design: projection operators define the replacement unit, TSVD supplies the training-free baseline, and ASVD supplies activation-aware weighting. 

A. Projection Operators as the Replacement Unit 

Prompt-based transformer restoration models contain many projection operators, including query-key-value projections, output projections, feed-forward expansions and reductions, point-wise convolutional projections, and prompt-conditioning projections. These modules are invoked repeatedly across the encoder, bottleneck, and decoder stages. They share a unified matrix representation: a linear layer is already a matrix, and a convolution kernel can be reshaped into a two-dimensional operator. This common representation localizes the replacement while preserving the architecture. The cost profile in Fig. 1(a) shows that this choice is not merely convenient algebraically but also meaningful for effi-ciency. In the profiled PromptCIR model, attention projections, feed-forward projections, and convolutional-projection layers together account for 99 .43% of parameters and 99 .89% of convolutional and linear MACs at 128 ×128 , so replacement can concentrate on these operators rather than on the non-dominant modules. Prompt-conditioning operators are far smaller in aggregate, yet we retain them within the unified formulation be-cause they govern degradation-conditioned feature modulation. Replacing selected projections is also far less intrusive than redesigning attention blocks. The surrounding normalization, nonlinearity, skip-connection, and prompt-conditioning logic can remain unchanged, while each dense projection is realized by two low-rank factors. This locality is essential in an offline replacement setting, where the compressed model must reproduce the pretrained restoration mapping as closely as possible. 

B. Spectral Evidence for SVD-Based Replacement 

After identifying the replacement unit, we examine whether SVD is a reasonable low-rank mechanism for these operators. SVD satisfies the deployment constraints imposed by the pretrained restoration model: it can be computed offline from pretrained weights, yields the optimal rank-r approximation under the standard Frobenius objective, and applies uniformly to linear projections and reshaped convolution kernels. It preserves the surrounding inference graph, since each original projection 3is replaced by two sequential low-rank projections with the same tensor interface. The singular-value analysis in Fig. 1(c) shows why SVD is an appropriate starting point but not a complete design. Representative projection operators exhibit measurable spectral concentration, but its degree varies across operator families: the mean rank needed to preserve 95% of the spectral energy, defined as the sum of squared singular values, ranges from 

47 .93% for prompt linear projections to 86 .47% for GDFN projections. This evidence supports SVD-based replacement while indicating that rank must be analyzed explicitly. First, TSVD is a principled baseline because it tests how much of the pretrained projection behavior a bounded-rank subspace can capture. Second, because uniform low-rank replacement is not equally effective for all projections, rank and operator selection should be evaluated explicitly rather than fixed globally. 

C. Channel Statistics Under Compressed Inputs 

TSVD provides the low-rank baseline, but its objective is uniform over channels. For blind CIR, this assumption deserves scrutiny because the network must preserve spatially precise structures while suppressing compression artifacts. As compressed images pass through a restoration transformer, different channels carry different amounts of artifact, texture, and prompt-conditioned information. We therefore inspect the activation statistics of high-cost projections using a small compressed calibration set. The observed activation distributions are markedly nonuni-form. For several window-attention, multi-Dconv head trans-posed attention (MDTA), gated-Dconv feed-forward network (GDFN), and output-projection families, 50% of the output activation energy, defined as the sum of squared per-channel activation magnitudes, concentrates in only 17 .39% –23 .76% 

of the channels, whereas prompt-related and some overlap-attention projections are less concentrated. Such heterogeneity means that a single unweighted objective treats all channels alike even when their activation magnitudes differ sharply under compressed inputs, which makes uniform channel treatment restrictive. If a high-activation channel is poorly approximated, the resulting error can propagate through the restoration features far more strongly than an error on a weakly activated channel. Our ASVD uses this observation as calibration information rather than as a training signal. The collected statistics neither optimize a restoration loss, nor rely on ground-truth restored images, nor modify the model online. Instead, they define diagonal scaling factors that give activation-dominant input and output channels greater influence within a deterministic offline replacement procedure. 

D. From Analysis to ASVD 

These observations define a two-step replacement strategy. Projection-level replacement targets the dominant cost centers while keeping the pretrained restoration pipeline intact. The spectral results support TSVD as a closed-form low-rank baseline for these matrix-representable operators. The activation results then motivate ASVD, our factor-generation refinement that introduces activation-derived channel weights so that channels more active under compressed inputs gain greater influence in the weighted approximation objective. At a fixed rank, TSVD and ASVD share the same inference-time structure, parameter count, and MACs; comparing them therefore isolates the effect of activation-calibrated factor generation under an identical deployment budget. IV. M ETHODOLOGY 

A. Overview 

The analysis in Sec. III leads to an operator-replacement formulation: selected projections are replaced by two low-rank factors generated offline from pretrained weights and, in our ASVD variant, lightweight activation statistics. The surrounding restoration pipeline is left unchanged, and no retraining, prompt redesign, or online updating is introduced. As illustrated in Fig. 2, TSVD directly decomposes the pre-trained projection weights as a closed-form baseline, whereas our ASVD first applies channel-wise scaling, decomposes the scaled weights, and then maps the factors back to the original operator space. At a fixed rank, the two variants share the same inference-time structure and complexity; only factor generation differs.

E. Implementation of Factorized Operators 

The low-rank factors are inserted as ordinary projection layers without intermediate nonlinearity or normalization. For a linear projection, Wb first maps the input feature from i

channels to reff channels, and Wa then maps the intermediate feature from reff channels to o channels. If the original projection has a bias term, it is retained after the second factor to preserve the affine form. For a convolutional projection, the reshaped factor Wb ∈

channels. The original spatial stride, padding, and dilation are attached to the first factor, while the second factor mixes the intermediate channels. This preserves the input-output tensor shape and leaves residual connections, attention reshaping, prompt fusion, and output heads unchanged. Because TSVD and ASVD produce the same two-factor graph at the same rank, their difference is attributable to factor generation rather than runtime architecture.

H. Instantiation on PromptCIR 

We instantiate both variants on PromptCIR [5], whose residual hybrid attention groups and transformer-style feed-forward blocks contain extensive projections across the en-coder, bottleneck, decoder, and prompt-conditioning pathways. Selected operators are replaced offline, while surrounding modules, prompts, normalization layers, nonlinearities, and inference procedures remain unchanged. V. E XPERIMENTS 

A. Experimental Setup 

We evaluate the framework on four blind compressed image restoration benchmarks: LIVE1 [58], BSDS500 [59], ICB [26], and DIV2K [25]. Low-quality inputs are generated by JPEG compression with quality factors uniformly sampled from 

[10 , 70] , which spans mild to severe degradation. We compare against JPEG, FBCNN [28], Contrastive JPEG [29], and prompt-based transformer frameworks including PromptIR [30] and PromptCIR [5]. All PromptCIR variants use identical ensemble inference and evaluation settings. ASVD uses 16 calibration images by default (all 14 images for ICB). Calibration inputs are 128 × 128 JPEG images generated from the corresponding benchmark with QFs sampled uniformly from 10 to 70. Only compressed-input activations are used for factor generation; no retraining is required. PSNR, SSIM, and PSNR-B are evaluated on RGB images and averaged over each dataset. PSNR uses the overall RGB mean squared error, whereas PSNR-B follows the channel-wise decibel averaging in SwinIR [19]. Parameter counts and MACs are reported per model for a 

256 × 256 input, including convolution, linear projections, and attention matrix multiplication.

The rank ablation in Table II further clarifies the role of activation calibration. At very small ranks, the factorized operators have limited capacity, and the choice of retained subspace determines most of the restoration error. The weighted objective favors preservation of active channels. ASVD achieves its largest DIV2K PSNR gain over TSVD at rank 32 and consistently improves PSNR-B at rank 64, with comparable PSNR and SSIM. As the rank grows, both decompositions recover a larger fraction of the pretrained operator, and the gap between TSVD and ASVD naturally narrows. This trend distinguishes ASVD from runtime augmentation: it improves the offline factor selection under the same rank and complexity budget. The feature visualizations in Fig. 4 provide representation-level evidence, with ASVD producing lower and more coherent errors on high-activation channels. The calibration-size study in Table III supports the use of a small compressed calibration set. The statistics used by our ASVD are per-channel activation magnitudes rather than task-loss gradients or reconstructed-image supervision; they simply measure which input and output channels respond more strongly when the pretrained model processes compressed images. The multi-seed results indicate that a small set can stabilize these coarse channel weights, with performance approximately plateauing around 8–16 images. The quality-range study also shows low sensitivity to the tested calibration distributions. This stability supports blind CIR, where the model restores compressed inputs without receiving their JPEG quality factors. The operator-level formulation in Fig. 2 keeps the accelerated model close to the original restoration backbone. Each selected projection is replaced locally by two factors, while attention re-shaping, prompt interaction, normalization, nonlinear activation, skip connections, and output reconstruction remain unchanged. This locality avoids conflating low-rank replacement with architectural redesign and enables a controlled comparison: at a fixed rank, TSVD and ASVD share the same parameter count, MACs, tensor interfaces, and inference graph. Consequently, their observed difference is attributable to activation-calibrated factor generation rather than topology or runtime computation. The choice of the rank-64 operating point follows the same evidence in Tables II and VII. It reduces parameters by 58 .75% 

and MACs by 16 .09% relative to the original PromptCIR backbone. Rank 96 reduces parameters by 46 .93% but MACs by only 1.70% ; rank 128 exceeds the original MAC count by 1.70% . Higher ranks are useful for demonstrating the convergence of TSVD and ASVD and for retaining restoration fidelity, but offer less arithmetic reduction. Rank 64 provides a compact model with measurable PSNR-B improvements over TSVD, while rank 96 offers restoration performance close to the original backbone. At rank 96, the PSNR difference from the original model ranges from −0.13 to +0 .03 dB, illustrating the complementary higher-fidelity operating regime. The method also offers a favorable deployment profile. All factor generation is performed offline, and the compressed model retains the original PromptCIR topology, prompts, normalization layers, and restoration head. ASVD is therefore complementary to pruning and quantization: it reduces the algebraic cost of dense projections while leaving precision reduction and sparsity-based acceleration available for deploy-ment. 9VII. C ONCLUSION 

We presented a training-free framework for accelerating transformer-based blind compressed image restoration. An empirical analysis of prompt-based restoration transformers shows that projection operators dominate the parameter and MAC cost, that their singular-value spectra exhibit operator-dependent low-rank structure, and that their compressed-image activations are strongly channel-imbalanced. These observations support TSVD as a principled training-free baseline and explain why a uniform low-rank approximation can fall short under aggressive rank budgets. Building on this rationale, we formulated projection-level replacement with TSVD as the closed-form baseline and designed ASVD as our activation-calibrated refinement that incorporates lightweight activation statistics into the decomposition objective. The resulting factors are generated entirely offline from compressed calibration inputs while preserving the original restoration architecture and inference flow. Experiments on four standard blind CIR benchmarks show that ASVD consistently improves PSNR-B over TSVD at rank 64 under identical parameter and MAC budgets, while maintaining comparable PSNR and SSIM. These results indicate that activation-aware factor generation can compress restoration transformers without retraining data, task-specific supervision, or online adaptation. The main evaluation uses a shared rank cap and one prompt-based backbone; a spectrum-based allocation study additionally examines non-uniform ranks under a matched MAC budget. Promising future directions include further optimization of operator-wise or layer-wise rank allocation guided by spectral and activation profiles, combining ASVD with quantization and pruning for further compression, and extending it to other transformer-based restoration tasks.
