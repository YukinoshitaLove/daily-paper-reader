Abstract—Incorrect exposure degrades both image brightness
and recoverable scene detail. Existing sRGB-based correction
methods are limited by the non-linear image signal processing
pipeline, whereas RAW-based methods are usually developed
for a single degradation type, such as low-light denoising or
high-light recovery. In this paper, we focus on joint exposure
correction and restoration directly in the RAW domain and
propose a unified RAW Image Exposure Correction and Restora-
tion (RIECR) framework for both under- and over-exposed
images. RIECR decomposes the problem into two coupled sub-
problems that follow the RAW imaging model: linear illumination
correction and residual degradation restoration. First, a Linear
Illumination Correction Network (LICN) estimates exposure-
aware linear gains by using channel-specific RAW cues. It exploits
the high signal-to-noise ratio of Green channels for robust
brightening and the relatively unsaturated Red/Blue responses
for high-light darkening. Second, a dual-branch Frequency-based
Enhancement Network (FEN) restores the remaining degrada-
tion with a Frequency Dynamic Convolution (FDC) module.
FDC decomposes convolutional responses into low- and high-
frequency components and adaptively suppresses amplified noise
or recovers clipped textures according to the exposure context.
We further build a real paired RAW benchmark containing
223 scenes and 1,784 pairs covering complex multi-exposure
errors. Experiments against sixteen state-of-the-art methods show
that RIECR achieves state-of-the-art performance on real RAW
exposure correction.

I. INTRODUCTION
Exposure correction improves visibility and preserves scene
detail for both photography and downstream tasks such as
segmentation and detection [1]–[5]. Yet exposure errors are
not a single degradation [6]. Limited photons in under-exposed
scenes produce photon shot noise, sensor noise [7], and color
distortion [8]; limited sensor range in over-exposed scenes
saturates and irreversibly clips bright structures [9]. A practical

valid linear gain and restore the direction-dependent residual
within one network.
To this end, we propose RAW Image Exposure Correction
and Restoration (RIECR), which explicitly decomposes the
task into two coupled sub-problems. First, the Linear Illu-
mination Correction Network (LICN) predicts the exposure
direction and estimates linear gains from complementary RAW
channels: Green offers robust high-SNR cues for low-light
brightening, while Red/Blue often remain less saturated and
guide high-light darkening. Second, the Frequency-based En-
hancement Network (FEN) restores the remaining degradation
through local convolution and global attention. Its Frequency
Dynamic Convolution (FDC) decomposes kernel responses
and adaptively suppresses amplified high-frequency noise or
emphasizes missing texture according to the exposure context.
Because existing RAW datasets usually cover only one
exposure subtask [9], [18], [22], we collect Complex Multi-
Exposure RAW (CMER), with 223 real scenes and 1,784
pairs spanning both directions, and synthesize additional pre-
training pairs from MIT-Adobe FiveK [23]. CMER supplies
pixel-aligned supervision for unified evaluation, while the syn-
thetic data tests whether broader exposure coverage benefits
real-data fine-tuning. Extensive experiments show consistent
improvements under both training protocols and exposure
directions. In summary, the main contributions are summarized
as follows:

II. RELATED WORK
Single Image High Dynamic Range Reconstruction: SI-
HDR expands local dynamic range through inverse tone
mapping [24], direct CNN/Transform reconstruction [25]–
[27], exposure-stack synthesis [28], or inverse-camera model-
ing [29], [30]. RawHDR further exploits high-bit-depth RAW
measurements [31]. These methods recover localized dynamic-
range loss from a nominally exposed input and usually assume
a usable global tone, whereas exposure correction must infer
the global rescaling.
Low-Light Image Enhancement: Classical enhancement ad-
justs histograms, suppresses noise, or decomposes illumination
and reflectance [32]–[34]. Learning-based methods employ
illumination decomposition, color spaces, frequency embed-
ding, transformers, and Mamba architectures [35]–[43]. RAW
restoration additionally models sensor noise for synthesis and
removal [7], [21], [44]–[46]. These methods are effective for
photon-limited inputs but typically assume a known exposure
ratio and do not address clipping-dominated over-exposure.
Image Exposure Correction: Classical exposure correc-
tion uses histogram, tone, or Retinex adjustment [32], [47],
[48], while high-light-specific networks recover brightness
and clipped content [9], [49], [50]. Deep learning has seen
rapid development recently [51]–[54], unified sRGB methods

B. Motivation and Framework Architecture
Fig. 2 illustrates the frequency behavior. It motivates two
coupled modules. LICN estimates r using channel-dependent
RAW responses: Green has higher sensitivity and SNR [31],
[63], [64], while Red/Blue often retain highlight responses.
FEN restores D by adapting low-/high-frequency responses to
amplified noise or missing texture.
The architecture of the proposed RAW Image Exposure
Correction and Restoration (RIECR) framework is illustrated
in Fig. 3. A normalized Bayer image Y ∈R2H×2W is packed
as Irgbg ∈RH×W ×4 and mapped by:
I = f(Irgbg; θ),
(5)
where f and θ denote RIECR and its parameters. LICN
produces a linearly corrected ˜I; local and global FEN branches
then predict the residual and output I.
C. Linear Illumination Correction Network (LICN)
LICN contains an exposure-label estimator and channel-

L = Sigmoid(Linear(Avg.(Convs.(Irgbg)))),

(9)

Frequency Dynamic Convolution. Fixed convolution cannot
simultaneously denoise and recover detail across spatially
varying degradation [56], [57]. FDC decomposes a base kernel
Ks in the spatial domain:
Kl = Avg(Ks),
Kh = Ks −Kl,
(12)
where Avg(·) is 3 × 3 pooling. Kl captures stable structure
and Kh captures residual detail. Pixel-wise gates Ml, Mh:
ˆF = Mh ⊙MC(Kh, F) + Ml ⊙MC(Kl, Avg(F)),
(13)
where MC is the primitive convolution and ⊙denotes multi-
plication. The gates attenuate noise-exposed high frequencies
or strengthen contextual texture near saturated structures.
Local Convolution Branch. A U-shaped CNN uses residual
blocks:
ˆF = {LReLU(Conv3×3(F))}×2 + Conv1×1(F),
(14)
where LReLU is the activation. Down/up-sampling and two
skip connections predict a residual added to ˜I, yielding Ilocal.
Global Attention Branch. A U-shaped transformer supplies
long-range context [36]. Its i-th attention block is:
ˆFi = CMCA(LN(Fi−1)) + Fi−1,
Fi = FFN(LN(ˆFi)) + ˆFi,
(15)
where CMCA, LN [68], and FFN denote channel attention,
layer normalization, and feed-forward layers. Multi-scale FDC
blocks let reliable regions guide distant degradation; convolu-
tion fuses Ilocal and Iglobal into I.

Complex Multiple Exposure RAW (CMER) Dataset: We
capture 223 static indoor/outdoor scenes with a Canon EOS
5D Mark IV and save 14-bit RAW images. The scenes include
dark smooth regions, strong light sources, reflective materials,
and fine structures. A tripod and remote control fix the
viewpoint; after acquiring a well-exposed reference, we fix
aperture and vary only exposure time. Captures with motion,
illumination variation, or misalignment are discarded, preserv-
ing pixel alignment. Daytime images use ISO 100; nighttime
inputs additionally use ISO 1,600–6,400 while references
remain at ISO 100. We record over-exposure ratios {2,4,8} and
under-exposure ratios {1/2,1/4,1/8,1/16,1/32} [70]. CMER
contains 1,784 pairs at 6720 × 4480: 669 over-exposed and
1,115 under-exposed. A scene-disjoint split uses 213 scenes
(1,704 pairs) for training and 10 scenes (80 pairs) for testing.

V. EXPERIMENTS
We evaluate RIECR against representative sRGB- and
RAW-domain methods, followed by component, domain, effi-
ciency, and cross-camera analyses.
A. Experimental Settings
Compared Methods. We compare eight sRGB methods:
MSEC [10], ECCM [71], ECLNet [12], ENC [72], FEC-
Net [11], Retinexformer (RF) [36], CSEC [16], and ES [15];
and eight RAW methods: SID [18], RED [73], ABF [74],
CGNet [9], RawHDR [31], SMG [75], ADU [76], and
RRM [39]. We adapt sRGB input/output layers to packed four-
channel RAW and retrain all methods on identical splits and
protocols using official implementations when available.
Evaluation Metrics. We report PSNR and SSIM [77] on
normalized RAW outputs for high-light, low-light, and their
average; higher values indicate better restoration. All visual-
izations use the same RAW-to-sRGB ISP.

D. Discussion
Evaluation between RAW and sRGB. We train RIECR
on CMER RAW data and sRGB counterparts generated by
the same ISP [21], and evaluate both through that ISP. Ta-
ble V shows that RAW improves average PSNR/SSIM from
28.35 dB/0.905 to 29.62 dB/0.919. The gain occurs in both
subsets because linear high-bit-depth RAW preserves high-
lights before tone mapping and enables physically consistent
low-light scaling and noise restoration. As shown in Fig. 9,
the visual example further shows that RAW input preserves
recoverable high-light details that are already compressed or
clipped in sRGB. This confirms that the rich detail, higher bit
depth, and linear response of RAW data are crucial for joint
exposure correction and restoration.
Evaluation on Cross-Camera Setting. Trained on Canon 5D
IV, RIECR transfers without retraining to the Sony IMX586
LRD dataset [80]. Fig. 10 shows corrected global tone and
recovered structure despite sensor and pipeline changes, sug-
gesting that the channel-aware decomposition is less device-
specific than a learned sRGB mapping.

When all RAW channels are clipped, the original signal cannot

recovery of the original details or colors. Future work could
incorporate generative priors to improve visual plausibility in
these severely degraded regions.
VI. CONCLUSION
In this paper, we proposed RIECR, a unified RAW-domain
framework that bridges the information loss of sRGB correc-
tion and the single-degradation focus of task-specific RAW
restoration. Following the physical RAW formation model,
RIECR decomposes joint under- and over-exposure restora-
tion into linear illumination correction and residual enhance-
ment. LICN estimates exposure-aware gains through channel-
specific guidance, using the high SNR of Green channels
for under-exposure and the less saturated Red/Blue responses
for over-exposure. FEN then addresses the divergent residual
degradations, while FDC separates low- and high-frequency
responses to adaptively suppress amplified noise and recover
weakened high-light structures. We further constructed CMER
with 223 real scenes and 1,784 paired RAW images and built
a synthetic dataset for pre-training. Comparisons with sixteen
methods, component ablations, RAW/sRGB evaluation, effi-
ciency analysis, and cross-camera tests validate the unified for-
mulation and its components. RIECR achieves 33.85 dB/0.951
after pre-training and fine-tuning with 2.56 M parameters.
Future work will extend CMER to dynamic scenes and broader
sensors and explore metadata-aware correction and stronger
priors for severely clipped regions.
