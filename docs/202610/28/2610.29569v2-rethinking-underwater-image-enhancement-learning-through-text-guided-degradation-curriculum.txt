Abstract 

Existing paired clean–degraded datasets have advanced learning-based underwater image enhance-ment, but they remain either limited in scale or difficult to acquire, and still fail to adequately cover the diverse degradations encountered in real-world underwater environments. Such scarce and insufficient data ultimately restrict the generalization of underwater image enhancement models, highlighting the need for realistic and controllable degradation modeling to better capture real underwater conditions and improve enhancement robustness. To this end, we propose a unified degradation-aware training framework for the underwater image enhancement task. A Text-Conditioned Degradation Augmen-tation Pipeline uses a text-guided diffusion model to synthesize diverse and semantically consistent degraded samples, providing paired data to support the subsequent curriculum learning strategy. The proposed Progressive Degradation Curriculum organizes training across multiple attributes and dif-ficulty levels to promote balanced learning, and a Transmission-Guided One-Step Diffusion Model leverages this progressive process to better address the wide range of degradation severity and restora-tion ambiguity in underwater images. Extensive experiments on multiple underwater benchmarks demonstrate that our proposed method achieves superior performance. Our code and models will be released upon the publication of this work. 

Keywords: Underwater image enhancement, diffusion models, curriculum learning, data augmentation

Unlike uniform degradations in com-mon image restoration tasks, underwater degra-dations are highly heterogeneous and scene-dependent, where they vary with water type, depth, illumination, and particle density, and often exhibit complex spatial coupling among these factors. Over the past decade, a wide range of enhance-ment approaches have been explored, spanning from physics-based restoration guided by analyt-ical priors [1–3] to deep learning-based methods trained on paired datasets [4–9]. While the for-mer provides physical interpretability, it fails to generalize across diverse water types and illumina-tion conditions. The latter achieves stronger visual quality through elaborately designed networks but relies heavily on limited paired data. Acquiring paired underwater data is inher-ently challenging because capturing the same scene under both degraded and clean conditions is almost impossible in dynamic environments. As illustrated in Figure 1-Top, such pairs are commonly obtained through (i) controlled water-tank capture [10], (ii) pseudo-reference generation by manually selecting outputs from multiple UIE methods [9, 11], or (iii) synthetic rendering with simplified underwater formation models [4, 12– 14]. The first two routes provide visually realistic supervision but are costly and difficult to scale, whereas the third enables scalable generation but often fails to reproduce the complex lighting and scattering effects of natural underwater scenes. These limitations reveal that the main bottle-neck of UIE lies not only in model architecture but also in the lack of realistic and controllable degra-dation modeling for generating diverse training variants. Moreover, underwater degradations vary drastically in strength and composition—ranging from light color cast to dense scattering and mixed turbidity. This motivates us to rethink UIE — not as direct restoration, but as learning from degrada-tion , where controllable, text-guided degradation modeling provides structured, degradation-aware supervision for enhancement learning. In addition, we argue that if degradations can be explicitly modeled and controlled, they can serve as a mean-ingful curriculum for enhancement, where the model gradually adapts to increasingly challeng-ing degradations across multiple attributes and severity levels, which encourages more generalized enhancement performance. To this end, we revisit underwater enhance-ment from a new perspective-leveraging the gen-erative capability of the diffusion model to explic-itly model and control underwater degradations (Figure 1-Medium). Diffusion-based generative models [15] provide an expressive latent space in which degradations can be synthesized and conditioned on semantic cues such as turbid-ity, illumination, or particle density. This text-conditioned formulation enables fine-grained con-trol over degradation attributes and supports large-scale data augmentation aligned with real underwater characteristics. The generated degra-dations further provide difficulty-aware cues that guide enhancement models to learn progressively under well-structured degradation distributions, yielding more stable optimization and visually coherent restoration across diverse underwater conditions. Moreover, we develop a Transmission-Guided One-Step Diffusion enhancer that injects 2a transmission prior into the latent space and per-forms a single deterministic reverse update, pro-viding transmission-guided restoration with lower sampling cost than multi-step diffusion meth-ods. Together, these designs establish a cohesive paradigm that unifies controllable degradation modeling and physically guided enhancement, offering a new perspective on the UIE task. Overall, our contributions can be summarized as follows: 

• We introduce a Text-Conditioned Degradation Augmentation (TCDA) pipeline that leverages a text-guided diffusion model to synthesize con-trollable degraded variants of clean images, enabling fine-grained attribute/severity control for task-aligned data augmentation. 

• We employ a progressive degradation curricu-lum learning that gradually increases training difficulty, stabilizing optimization and improv-ing the model’s robustness. 

• We design a Transmission-Guided One-step Dif-fusion model (TG-Diff) that embeds a trans-mission prior into the latent space, achieving high-quality restoration with greater inference efficiency than multi-step diffusion methods. 

• Extensive experiments on multiple underwa-ter benchmarks demonstrate that our method achieves state-of-the-art performance. 

# 2 Related Work 

2.1 Underwater Image Enhancement 

Traditional UIE methods are typically built upon the underwater image formation model that char-acterizes light scattering and absorption. Early works such as underwater dark channel prior [1], red channel prior [2], and blurriness-based esti-mation [3] estimate the transmission map and background light to remove haze and recover true colors. More recent prior-based methods further exploit illumination characteristics to handle non-uniform lighting and color degradation [16, 17]. However, their performance heavily depends on accurate estimation of water parameters and scene depth, limiting their robustness across varying underwater conditions [18]. Deep learning has revolutionized UIE by providing strong nonlin-ear modeling and robust feature representation capabilities. Early convolutional methods [4–8] employed encoder–decoder or multi-branch archi-tectures to learn direct mappings from degraded to clean images, achieving improved contrast and color fidelity through illumination modeling and multi-level feature learning. Adversarial and gen-erative frameworks [19, 20] further enhanced per-ceptual realism but often introduced instability and color over-saturation. Moreover, some works use contrastive learning [21], domain priors, and external knowledge to improve UIE generaliza-tion and controllability. PhaseFormer [22] intro-duces phase-based attention to preserve struc-tural cues, while CLIP-UIE [23] utilizes semantic guidance from vision-language models. Semantic-Guided Diffusion [24] leverages multimodal cues from LLaVA and Zip-CLIP to generate semantic heatmaps for diffusion guidance. Recent studies highlight the role of generative priors and degradation modeling in diffusion-based image restoration [25]. Underwater-specific approaches, including WF-Diff [26] and SLURPP [27], exploit diffusion models for under-water enhancement. Related diffusion-based restoration methods further explore degradation-model optimization [28], global brightness alignment [29], and blur-decoupled learning [30]. Despite these advances, the effectiveness of related UIE methods remains largely constrained by the availability and diversity of training data. 

2.2 Underwater Image Synthesis 

The scarcity of paired and diverse underwater data has motivated the research on underwa-ter image synthesis. Early physics-based methods simulate light attenuation using simplified water models but fail to reproduce complex scatter-ing [4, 14]. Alternatively, some datasets create pseudo-references by applying multiple UIE meth-ods to a degraded image and having a user select the best outcome as the “clean” target [9]. While visually plausible, this approach is funda-mentally limited as it provides no insight into the forward degradation process itself. It merely offers a single, fixed enhancement target for each input, failing to model the diverse and control-lable degradation attributes (e.g., water type, turbidity) that are crucial for robust general-ization. # 3 Method 

Our methodology addresses the challenges of underwater image enhancement through a two-stage framework. First, to overcome the bottle-neck of limited diversity and fixed degradation patterns in existing datasets, we introduce a Text-Conditioned Degradation Augmentation (TCDA) pipeline. This pipeline synthesizes a vast and diverse corpus of degraded images with control-lable degradation attributes specified by fine-grained text prompts. Second, leveraging this augmented data, we employ a progressive degra-dation curriculum to train a Transmission-Guided One-Step Diffusion (TG-Diff) model, designed for transmission-guided image restoration with greater inference efficiency than multi-step diffu-sion methods. 

3.1 Text-Conditioned Degradation Augmentation 

As illustrated in Figure 2, our TCDA pipeline establishes a controllable mapping between clean images, their degraded counterparts, and textual degradation prompts. 

3.1.1 Degradation Captioning via MLLMs 

We first collect a large-scale dataset of 12,483 degraded–clean image pairs from four representa-tive underwater datasets: UIEB [9], EUVP [12], LSUI [11], and 3D TURBID [10]. To ensure fair evaluation, only the training subsets of UIEB and LSUI are used. To create rich, structured textual represen-tations of the visual degradations, we employ a multimodal large language model (Gemini 2.5-Flash [33]) to generate fine-grained captions. These captions describe six core degradation attributes: turbidity level, color-cast type and severity, density of suspended particles, lighting conditions, degree of haze or backscatter, and extent of contrast reduction. 3.1.2 Flow-Based Degradation Modeling 

Building upon the constructed triplets, we develop a text-conditioned diffusion framework to learn the mapping from clean to degraded representa-tions. To model this directed transformation, we adopt a flow-based diffusion framework based on FLUX.1 Kontext [15]. Unlike traditional denoising-based models, flow-matching directly learns the vector field from a source to a target distribution, offering a more stable and efficient training objective for this task. Tdeg is encoded by frozen dual text encoders (T5-XXL [34] and CLIP-L [35]) to obtain an embed-ding c. The model learns a conditional velocity field vθ (zt, x 0, t, c ) that drives the transformation of x0 towards x1 under text guidance. We adopt a flow-matching objective as

3.1.3 Controllable Degradation Synthesis 

To fully leverage our text-conditioned degrada-tion model, we construct a structured prompt bank. Specifically, instead of relying on uncon-strained free-form prompts, we organize the prompt space around six degradation attributes, including turbidity, color cast, particles, light-ing, haze/backscatter, and contrast. For each attribute, we define phrases with different sever-ity levels and group them into three difficulty tiers, i.e., easy, moderate, and hard, so that the resulting prompts are both semantically coher-ent and explicitly controllable. Each prompt is a tuple Tdeg = (a1, . . . , a K , d ), detailing key attributes {ak} and a difficulty level d ∈{easy , moderate , hard }. During synthesis, given a clean image Iclean and any such prompt Tdeg ,our model generates a corresponding degraded image Ideg . This text-driven paradigm not only enables fine-grained semantic control over degra-dation types and intensities but also provides the multi-difficulty data crucial for our progressive enhancement training, as detailed next. Represen-tative prompt-conditioned degradation results are shown in Figure 4. Detailed design is described in Supplementary Sec. 2.2.

To address this, we introduce a progressive degradation curriculum that leverages the diffi-culty levels defined in our prompt bank. This curriculum gradually increases the degradation complexity during training, allowing the model to first master fundamental color and tone restora-tion before tackling more complex scattering and contrast loss. 3.3 Transmission-Guided One-Step Diffusion 

To bridge the gap between physics-agnostic dif-fusion models and restoration with greater infer-ence efficiency than multi-step diffusion methods, we propose Transmission-Guided One-Step Diffu-sion (TG-Diff). Our framework builds upon the single-step SD-Turbo architecture [40], which we adapt to the UIE task. Runtime and model-complexity comparisons are provided in Sup-plementary Sec. 3.1. We freeze the pre-trained weights and apply LoRA [36] with a rank of 32 to both the U-Net and the VAE encoder. To provide the diffusion model with a dense spatial prior on attenuation and scattering, we condition it on a transmission map T estimated via the UDCP method [1]. This strategy pro-vides the model with a physics-inspired cue about spatially varying water properties. To achieve this, the map is downsampled to the latent res-olution and concatenated with the image latent 

zdeg , forming an expanded 5-channel input x =[zdeg , Tlatent ] where new channel weights are zero-initialized. Furthermore, to preserve high-frequency details during the single-step process, we augment the VAE with multi-scale skip connections. In this process, an intermediate skip feature Si is first generated from the encoder feature Ei via a 1 × 1convolution. This feature is then added to the corresponding upsampled decoder feature Ui as:

# 4 Experiment 

4.1 Experimental Setup 

Implementation Details. In the TCDA pipeline, we utilize two NVIDIA A800 GPUs (80 GB) for training the degradation diffusion model. We adopt a LoRA fine-tuning strategy with a rank of 8. All the images are resized to 1024 ×1024 in the training process. The model is optimized using AdamW with a learning rate of 1 × 10 −4 and a per-GPU batch size of 1. We train the model for two epochs. During inference, we employ 28 denoising steps for text-conditioned degradation synthesis. For training the TG-Diff, we adopt the Stable Diffusion-Turbo [40] as our base model and fine-tune it with LoRA of rank 32. During the training process, the samples are randomly cropped into 256 × 256 patches from the original images. We train the model for 50,000 iterations using the AdamW optimizer (β1 = 0 .9, β 2 = 0 .999) with a constant learning rate of 1 × 10 −4 and a batch size of 12 per GPU. We optimize TG-Diff with the composite objec-tive Ltotal = LL1 + α LLPIPS , which combines the L1 reconstruction loss ( LL1 ) and the LPIPS perceptual loss ( LLPIPS ), with α = 3.3 in all experiments.

# 5 Conclusion 

In this work, we propose an explicit, controllable degradation modeling framework designed to alle-viate the scale and diversity limitations of paired UIE data. We introduce TCDA to synthesize diverse underwater degradations with fine-grained textual control, a progressive degradation curricu-lum to organize these samples across attributes and difficulty levels, and TG-Diff to incorporate a transmission prior into one-step restoration. Together, these components convert controllable degradation modeling into structured supervision for robust UIE. Experiments on multiple bench-marks demonstrate improvements across most restoration and reference-free metrics, while the ablation studies examine the contributions of the curriculum, data augmentation, and transmission guidance. Future work will extend the frame-work to temporally consistent underwater video enhancement and broader real-world degradation conditions.
