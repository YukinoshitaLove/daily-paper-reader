Abstract
Low-light image enhancement (LLIE) aims to recover well-
exposed and perceptually faithful images from severely de-
graded observations. Recent diffusion-based generative meth-
ods achieve strong perceptual quality by leveraging powerful
image priors, but their iterative sampling incurs substantial com-
putational cost, limiting practical deployment. Despite recent
efficiency gains in generative LLIE, high-quality enhancement
with a single function evaluation and low overall computation
remains challenging, particularly under diverse real-world low-
light conditions. We introduce GLOW-FM, a one-step latent
flow-matching framework for efficient generative LLIE. GLOW-
FM performs conditional transport in the latent space of a frozen
Tiny-VAE and leverages Terminal Velocity Matching (TVM) to
enable enhancement with a single function evaluation. Matched
comparisons with alternative one-step objectives support TVM
as an effective design choice, and, to the best of our knowledge,
this is its first adaptation to image restoration and specifically to
LLIE. Extensive experiments on challenging real-world bench-
marks demonstrate that GLOW-FM achieves competitive image
quality and state-of-the-art cross-dataset generalization while
requiring only a single function evaluation and 34 GFLOPs
for the small variant. These findings highlight the potential of
GLOW-FM to bring generative LLIE closer to practical deploy-
ment through single-step inference and compact latent model-
ing. Code is available at https://github.com/arm-
research/AAIR-GLOW-FM.

well-exposed and visually pleasing images from observations
captured under insufficient illumination. Real-world low-light
images often exhibit a complex combination of degradations,
including severe underexposure, sensor noise, color distortion,
reduced contrast, detail loss, and local saturation, which vary sig-
nificantly across cameras and imaging conditions, making LLIE
a challenging problem in real-world imaging [6, 8, 28]. Beyond

that supports reliable high-level vision applications [16, 40].
Regression-based LLIE methods provide strong distortion
performance and efficient inference [4, 5, 7, 45, 54, 58, 59, 61,
62, 68, 69]. These approaches typically optimize distortion-
oriented objectives and are effective at recovering visibility and
suppressing noise, but often struggle under severe degradation,
where substantial information loss makes enhancement inher-
ently ambiguous. Trained with pixel-wise objectives, they tend
to produce overly smooth results and suppress fine details [3].
To address this limitation, generative models [17, 19, 21, 22, 55,
65, 66] have emerged as a powerful LLIE paradigm, learning
the distribution of natural images and leveraging strong image

priors to recover realistic textures and structures that are diffi-
cult to reconstruct with deterministic networks. These methods
demonstrate substantial improvements in perceptual quality and
robustness under severe degradations. However, these gains
come at the cost of iterative sampling, resulting in high computa-

for high-resolution enhancement in resource-constrained appli-

To address this limitation, several efficient generative LLIE
frameworks have recently been proposed [20, 25, 70]. These
methods significantly reduce the required number of function
evaluations (NFEs) compared to conventional diffusion models.
Nevertheless, they still rely on iterative refinement procedures
and diffusion-inspired architectures whose computational
complexity remains substantial. For example, WCDM [20]
requires 10 NFEs and approximately 90 GFLOPs during
inference, while ReDDiT [25] reduces sampling to 2 NFEs
but still requires roughly 128 GFLOPs. Consequently, while
recent generative LLIE approaches have substantially reduced
sampling cost, achieving high-quality enhancement with a
single inference step and a low overall computational budget
remains an active challenge, particularly across diverse and
demanding real-world low-light conditions.
In this work, we address this challenge by introducing
an efficient Generative Low-Light image enhancement with
one-step latent Flow Matching (GLOW-FM). Our approach
leverages Terminal Velocity Matching (TVM) [71], which
enables direct prediction of the normal-light image, eliminating
the need for iterative sampling during inference. Comparisons
with standard Flow Matching [30, 31], Consistency Flow
Matching [63], and MeanFlow [12] establish Terminal Velocity
Matching (TVM) [71] as a deliberate design choice for
single-step LLIE. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first
adaptation of TVM to image restoration, and specifically to
LLIE. GLOW-FM learns a conditional transport that maps
degraded low-light representations directly to their well-exposed
counterparts in the latent space of a frozen Tiny-VAE [49].
Operating in this compact latent space substantially reduces
computational cost while preserving the expressive power of
generative modeling. Moreover, latent representations capture
semantic image structure while suppressing dataset-specific
variations, improving restoration and generalization [29, 48, 51].
Extensive experiments on challenging real-world low-light
benchmarks [45, 54] demonstrate that GLOW-FM provides
an effective generative prior for low-light enhancement while
operating under small inference budgets. Despite requiring
only a single NFE, our method achieves image quality
competitive with recent efficient generative LLIE approaches
and consistently exhibits strong cross-dataset generalization as
shown in Fig. 1. These results suggest that latent flow matching,
combined with one-step TVM estimation, offers a promising
direction toward practical, high-quality, and computationally
efficient low-light image enhancement.
Our main contributions are summarized as follows:
• We introduce GLOW-FM, an efficient generative framework
for low-light image enhancement (LLIE) based on latent
flow matching. By performing enhancement in a compact
latent space with a lightweight Tiny-VAE and a one-step flow
formulation, GLOW-FM achieves high restoration quality
at low computational cost.
• We establish Terminal Velocity Matching (TVM) as the
strongest overall one-step objective for LLIE through
matched comparisons with Rectified Flow, Consistency Flow
Matching, and MeanFlow. To the best of our knowledge,
this is the first adaptation of TVM to image restoration,
specifically LLIE.
• We achieve state-of-the-art cross-dataset generalization
compared to generative and non-generative baselines,
while maintaining competitive image quality and improved
efficiency over existing generative LLIE methods.
2. Related Work
2.1. Generative Low-Light Image Enhancement
A large body of LLIE research focuses on directly regress-
ing a normal-light image from a low-light input using Retinex
decomposition [26], illumination estimation, and deep neural
networks [4, 5, 45, 62]. While these approaches are compu-
tationally efficient, they often produce overly smooth results
and struggle to capture the diversity of plausible normal-light
reconstructions. The recent success of diffusion models [18] in
image generation has motivated their adoption for high-quality
low-light image enhancement [17, 19–21, 25, 65, 70]. Diff-
Retinex [65] formulates enhancement as Retinex decomposition
followed by conditional diffusion-based reconstruction of re-
flectance and illumination components. GSAD [19] introduces
a global structure-aware diffusion framework that combines
curvature-based regularization with uncertainty-guided optimiza-
tion to preserve scene structure and focus restoration on chal-
lenging regions. Subsequent work has largely aimed to reduce
the high computational cost of diffusion sampling. ReDDiT [25]
uses trajectory matching distillation in a reflectance-aware resid-
ual space to reduce the NFEs required at inference. PyDiff [70]
performs denoising in a coarse-to-fine resolution pyramid and
employs a global correction module to mitigate brightness and
color inconsistencies. WCDM [20] accelerates inference by con-
ducting diffusion in the wavelet domain on low-frequency sub-
bands while restoring high-frequency details through a dedicated
branch. Reti-Diff [17] generates compact latent Retinex priors
to guide a transformer-based restoration network, while Ligh-
tenDiffusion [21] combines latent Retinex decomposition with
diffusion for unsupervised LLIE. Related concurrent preprints,
IllumFlow [57] and Consist-Retinex [60], further explore one-
step generative LLIE through Retinex-based decomposition.

2.2. Generative Model Acceleration
Generative diffusion and flow models have achieved remarkable
success across a wide range of image-generation tasks. Their
strong priors have also motivated applications to image restora-
tion, where the goal is to recover a clean image from a degraded
observation. InDI [11] performs restoration through direct
iteration, while IR-SDE [35] models the restoration trajectory
using a mean-reverting stochastic process. Bridge-matching
methods [46] further formalize transport between degraded and
clean endpoint distributions, and flow-based approaches such
as FlowIE [73] and PnP-Flow [37] extend this formulation to
image restoration. Despite their strong restoration capabilities,
these methods commonly require iterative sampling, resulting
in substantial inference cost. Early acceleration approaches em-
ployed fast post-hoc samplers [23, 34, 47], reducing inference
from hundreds of steps to tens, often at the expense of quality
under aggressive acceleration.
More recently, flow-based
objectives have been developed specifically for efficient one-
and few-step generation. Rectified Flow [31] learns straighter
transport trajectories, while Consistency Flow Matching [63]
regularizes the velocity field for few-step inference. ELIR [9]
applies Consistency Flow Matching to image restoration in
the latent space of a Tiny AutoEncoder. MeanFlow [12] learns
mean transport trajectories, whereas TVM [71] learns finite
displacements between arbitrary timesteps for high-fidelity
one- and few-step generation. Despite these advances, efficient
flow-based generation remains relatively unexplored for
LLIE. Building upon TVM, we introduce a single-step latent
flow-matching framework for efficient low-light enhancement.
3. Method
We formulate low-light image enhancement as a conditional
flow-matching problem [30] between the low-light distribution
p1(x1) and the normally exposed distribution p0(x0). Given
a paired sample (x1,x0), where x1 ∼p1(x1) and x0 ∼p0(x0)
denote the low-light source and normal-light target, respectively,
we follow the TVM time convention [71]: the source is placed
at t = 1 and the target at t = 0, opposite to the original Flow
Matching convention. Enhancement therefore proceeds toward
earlier terminal times, from t=1 to t=0.
Rather than learning this transport directly in pixel space,
we perform it in the latent space of a frozen VAE with encoder
E and decoder D. We denote the corresponding latents as
z1 = E(x1) and z0 = E(x0), and define intermediate states zt
along the flow-path. To enable efficient inference, we train a
two-time conditioned model Fθ(zt,t,s) with Terminal Velocity
Matching (TVM) [71], which learns displacements along the
trajectory rather than relying only on numerical integration of
an instantaneous velocity field. This allows the same latent
flow model to support both one-step enhancement and few-step
refinement. Beyond its reported strong one-step performance,
we motivate TVM for restoration through theoretical analysis.
Under the TVM regularity conditions [71, Theorem 1] and
a Lipschitz assumption on the decoder, we derive a bound
on the pixel-space Wasserstein-2 distance [41] between the
restored-image and target normal-light distributions in terms of
the TVM objectives and autoencoder reconstruction error. This
links TVM training to pixel-space restoration; the complete
theorem and proof are provided in Appendix B. We next de-

3.3. Inference
At inference time, we encode the low-light input as the initial
latent z(M) = E(x1) at the source endpoint tM = 1. We use
the uniform time grid tm = m/M, m = 0,...,M, traversed in
decreasing order from tM =1 to t0=0. For each m, the interval
uses start time t = tm and terminal time s = tm−1, and the
model predicts the displacement from the current latent at tm
to the next latent at tm−1. Each interval requires one network
evaluation; hence, the number of intervals M equals the number
of NFEs. For one-step enhancement, M =1, so t=1 and s=0,
and the model directly transports the encoded low-light latent
to the enhanced endpoint. For few-step enhancement, the same
displacement model is applied over multiple shorter intervals.
The full inference procedure is summarized in Algorithm 2.
3.4. Efficient Latent Architecture
Compact Latent Representation. Operating in latent space is
central to the efficiency of GLOW-FM. Instead of applying the
flow model to full-resolution RGB images, the VAE encoder
maps the input into a lower-dimensional representation with
reduced spatial resolution. The learned transport therefore acts
on a compact latent grid, lowering the cost of each function
evaluation and making low-NFE inference more practical.
The latent space also provides a useful restoration prior.
Since the VAE is pretrained on a broad natural-image distribu-
tion, its representation captures reusable image structure while
suppressing some pixel-level noise and dataset-specific degra-
dation patterns. Prior work has similarly shown that pretrained
latent representations can support robust image restoration and
domain generalization [29, 48, 51]. As a result, the flow model

HDiT two-time flow model. We implement the two-time
conditioned flow model Fθ(zt,t,s) with an Hourglass Diffusion
Transformer (HDiT) backbone. HDiT combines the multi-scale
efficiency of U-Net-like architectures with the representation
capacity of diffusion transformers [10].
Unlike standard
DiT-style architectures [42], which apply global attention at
every resolution and incur O(n2) attention cost, HDiT restricts
global attention to low-resolution stages and uses fixed-window
neighborhood attention at higher resolutions, yielding linear
O(n) scaling with the number of latent image tokens. This
design is useful for GLOW-FM because latent restoration
requires both global exposure/color correction and preservation
of spatially aligned local content from the degraded input, while
keeping the two-time flow model efficient at inference.

4. Experiments
4.1. Experimental Setup
Datasets. We evaluate GLOW-FM on both paired and unpaired
real-world LLIE benchmarks.
Our primary benchmark is
LSD-DLL [45], a large-scale dataset designed to improve the
realism and diversity of LLIE evaluation. Unlike many earlier
low-light datasets [7, 15, 64], which are limited by synthetic
darkening, controlled capture settings, or restricted sensor cov-
erage, LSD-DLL [45] provides real low and normal-light image
pairs captured across diverse scenes and devices in the wild
using dynamic exposure calibration and multi-frame alignment
to obtain more realistic paired data. As a result, it better captures
phenomena such as sensor noise, spatially varying illumination,
and color distortions that are often underrepresented in existing
LLIE benchmarks. These properties motivated its use as the
benchmark dataset in [8]. LSD-DLL also provides the LSD-U
test split, which consists of unpaired low-light images captured
with unseen devices and enables evaluation of generalization
beyond the paired training distribution.
To complement
LSD-DLL, we additionally evaluate on UHD-LOL4K [54], a
high-resolution LLIE benchmark, and LOL-v1 [7], one of the
most widely used datasets in the LLIE literature.
Following [19, 20, 25], we further evaluate cross-dataset gen-
eralization by training on LOL-v2-Syn [64] and testing directly
on five widely used unpaired real-world low-light datasets:
DICM [27], LIME [14], MEF [36], NPE [53], and VV [50].

Since these datasets do not provide paired reference images,
they are evaluated using no-reference image quality metrics.
Evaluation metrics. For paired benchmarks, we evaluate
both distortion and perceptual quality. Distortion is measured
using PSNR and SSIM [56], while perceptual similarity is
assessed using LPIPS [67]. We additionally evaluate perceptual
image quality using the no-reference metrics NIQE [39] and
MUSIQ [24]. For unpaired benchmarks, where ground-truth
reference images are unavailable, we follow [20] and report
the no-reference perceptual quality metrics NIQE [39] and
BRISQUE [38]. Results are averaged over five evaluation
seeds; full mean and STD results are provided in Appendix F.
Implementation details. GLOW-FM employs a frozen Tiny-
VAE [49] to obtain 16-channel latent image representations,
while only the latent flow model is optimized during training.
Unless otherwise specified, experiments use the GLOW-FM-XL
configuration with a single NFE. We additionally evaluate
medium (M) and small (S) configurations, which differ only in
the capacity of the HDiT backbone. Source latents are perturbed
with Gaussian noise using σ=0.01. Models are trained for ap-
proximately 100k optimization steps on 8 NVIDIA H100 GPUs.
For LSD-DLL and UHD-LOL4K, we trained the baselines on
the respective training sets, whereas all other results use the au-
thors’ official code and checkpoints. Appendix D details these
baseline training protocols together with our implementation,
hyperparameters, and runtime and memory analyses.
4.2. Results on Paired Benchmarks
Tab. 1 compares GLOW-FM with representative non-generative
and recent efficient generative LLIE methods on the paired LSD-
DLL and UHD-LOL4K benchmarks. Across both datasets,
GLOW-FM achieves a favorable quality–efficiency trade-off
with only a single NFE during inference. On LSD-DLL, it ob-
tains the strongest overall performance across several distortion
and perceptual-quality metrics.
On UHD-LOL4K, non-
generative methods remain stronger on some distortion-oriented
metrics, while GLOW-FM maintains competitive image quality
with substantially lower inference cost than the generative
baselines.
The medium and small variants further reduce
computational cost while preserving strong image quality. Rep-
resentative qualitative results on LSD-DLL are shown in Fig. 3.
On LOL-v1, GLOW-FM maintains a strong quality-
efficiency tradeoff. As shown in Tab. 3, GLOW-FM achieves
the best NIQE score among the compared methods with
a single NFE. We hypothesize that its weaker distortion
performance arises from the limited training data available
in this benchmark.
A controlled data-scaling experiment
supporting this interpretation is presented in Fig. 5. Since paired
evaluation does not fully capture robustness to unseen devices
and low-light domains, we separately examine cross-dataset
and unseen-device generalization in Sec. 4.3.
4.3. Generalization to Unseen Low-Light Data
Tab. 4 reports results on five widely used unpaired real-world
low-light benchmarks:
DICM, LIME, MEF, NPE, and
VV. Following the protocol adopted in prior work [19, 25],
GLOW-FM is trained on LOL-v2-Syn [64] and evaluated
directly on the target datasets without additional fine-tuning.
GLOW-FM achieves the best generative NIQE and BRISQUE
results on nearly all evaluated benchmarks while operating
at substantially lower computational cost. It also obtains the
strongest mean performance across both metrics.
Tab. 2 reports results on the LSD-U subset from [8],
which evaluates generalization to low-light images captured
with unseen devices. Among all compared generative and
non-generative methods,
GLOW-FM achieves the best
NIQE and MUSIQ scores with a single NFE. Moreover, all
GLOW-FM variants achieve competitive no-reference image
quality despite substantially lower computational cost than
the competing generative methods. These results demonstrate
strong generalization beyond the training distribution, indicating
that the proposed latent flow formulation remains robust under
real-world distribution shifts. Together with the cross-dataset
results in Tab. 4, they demonstrate consistent generalization
across unseen devices and diverse low-light domains.

4.4. Ablation Study
Comparison of one-step objectives. Tab. 5 compares TVM
with MeanFlow [12], ConsistencyFM [63] and No-TVM
(standard Flow Matching [30, 31]), with all other experimental
settings held fixed.
We additionally evaluate No-TVM at
25 NFEs.
Among the matched TAESD one-step variants,
TVM achieves the strongest overall distortion and perceptual
performance.
MeanFlow forms its JVP target from the
initial-time velocity, whose sample-wise approximation can
destabilize training [71]; this may be particularly problematic
for LLIE, where the initial state is a degraded low-light image.
ConsistencyFM enforces a velocity-consistent straight flow,
which may restrict a single full-interval mapping for spatially
heterogeneous restoration [9, 63]. TVM instead regularizes the
displacement at its predicted terminal state, aligning the one-step
mapping with the velocity near the enhanced endpoint [71].
Effect of training-set size. Fig. 5 isolates the effect of data
availability by training GLOW-FM on progressively larger
LSD-DLL subsets while keeping the architecture and training
protocol fixed. Increasing the training set from approximately
0.2K to 3.0K image pairs improves PSNR monotonically from
16.8 to 20.3dB and MUSIQ from 34.5 to 36.0. This trend
supports our data-limited interpretation of the LOL-v1 distortion
results and is consistent with prior observations that flow-based
models benefit strongly from additional training data [72].

effect of the latent representation on GLOW-FM performance.

We compare the proposed TAESD latent representation against
a larger SD-VAE and a direct pixel-space variant while keeping
the velocity network fixed. TAESD achieves better PSNR,

significantly more computation, direct pixel-space modeling
produces substantially weaker distortion and perceptual quality.

is not only computationally advantageous but also improves
enhancement quality, suggesting that the compact TAESD
representation provides an effective latent space for LLIE.
5. Conclusion
Low-light image enhancement remains challenging because
noise, color shifts, and information loss make faithful restoration
ambiguous. Although recent generative LLIE methods have
reduced sampling cost, combining single-step inference, low
overall computation, and robust performance across real-world
conditions remains challenging.
In this work, we address this challenge with GLOW-FM,
an efficient generative LLIE framework based on one-step
latent flow matching. By combining efficiency-aware latent
flow matching with Terminal Velocity Matching, GLOW-FM
enables single-NFE enhancement while preserving generative
priors for realistic restoration. Experiments show competitive
quality, improved efficiency, and state-of-the-art cross-dataset
generalization, suggesting a practical direction for robust and
efficient generative low-light enhancement.

C. Additional Implementation Details
Training and inference.
Unless otherwise specified, all
experiments use a frozen Tiny-VAE encoder/decoder while
optimizing only the latent flow model. Models are trained on 8
NVIDIA H100 GPUs with a per-GPU batch size of 2 (effective
batch size 16) for approximately 100k optimization steps.
Optimization uses AdamW with a learning rate of 10−5, betas
(0.9,0.95), weight decay 0.01, gradient clipping at norm 1.0,
and EMA decay 0.9999. The learning rate follows a 5k-step
warmup with cosine decay to a minimum learning-rate ratio of
0.01. Training uses random 2048×2048 crops for LSD-DLL
and UHD-LOL4K, and 384×384 crops for LOL-v1. Validation
uses a batch size of 1 and is performed at the original image
resolution. Source latents are perturbed with Gaussian noise
using σ = 0.01 during training and σ = 0 during evaluation.
Unless otherwise specified, all reported results use a single NFE.
HDiT configurations.
Tab. 6 summarizes the implementation
details of the HDiT backbone variants used in our experiments.
Each HDiT backbone has two resolution levels: the first level
uses 7×7 neighborhood attention (NA), and the second level
uses global self-attention. In the table, Widths and Depths
denote the channel width and the number of transformer blocks
specified at each HDiT level, respectively; Map. width and Map.
depth denote the width and depth of the conditioning/mapping
network, and dhead denotes the attention head dimension.
Tiny-VAE latent representation.
We use the official made-
byollin/taesd3 [49] checkpoint as a frozen Tiny-VAE
image tokenizer. TAESD is a lightweight autoencoder distilled
from the Stable Diffusion VAE (SD-VAE) [44], providing a com-
pact alternative for encoding and decoding images. RGB images
are encoded into 16-channel latent representations, and the flow
model operates entirely in this latent space. The encoder and
decoder remain frozen throughout training, and no additional
latent normalization is applied. During inference, the predicted
latent representation is decoded using the same frozen Tiny-VAE
decoder before computing image-space evaluation metrics.

TFFormer.
We trained a separate TFFormer model from
scratch on each of LSD-DLL, UHD-LOL4K, and LOL-v1.
TFFormer was optimized using an Adam optimizer with hyper-
parameters β1 =0.9 and β2 =0.99. We set the initial learning

ReduceLROnPlateau scheduler [43]. We trained each model
for 300,000 steps on a single GPU using a batch size of 24
and 128×128 RGB patches. Additionally, we used a low-light
regularization coefficient of λR = 0.2 for the combined
reconstruction and luminance–chrominance objective.
HVI-CIDNet.
HVI-CIDNet was trained with the Adam
optimizer (β1 = 0.9, β2 = 0.999) and 256×256 input crops
using a single GPU for 1,000 epochs with a batch size of 4. The
initial learning rate was 10−4 and is then steadily decreased to
1×10−7 using a cosine annealing scheme [33] during training.
We used the default HVI-CIDNet data augmentation pipeline,
consisting of random cropping, random horizontal flipping, and
random vertical flipping.
Retinexformer.
We trained Retinexformer using the Adam
optimizer with β1=0.9 and β2=0.999, an initial learning rate
of 2×10−4 then steadily decreased to 1×10−6 by the cosine an-
nealing scheme [33]. Training is set for 2.5×105 iterations. We
train with batch size 8 on randomly cropped 128×128 patches,
and apply data augmentation of random rotation and flipping.
Multinex.
We trained Multinex using the Adam optimizer
with a cosine annealing learning-rate schedule [33] that decays
from 2×10−4 to 1×10−6. Multinex is trained from scratch
for 150K iterations with a batch size of 8 and patch size of
256×256. Training data are augmented via random cropping,
horizontal and vertical flipping, and random rotation.
PyDiff.
PyDiff was trained from scratch using random
192×288 crops, horizontal flipping, and brightness augmenta-
tion with a factor sampled from [0.5,1.5]. We used Adam with
an initial learning rate of 10−4 and a batch size of 8 per GPU.
The learning rate was multiplied by 0.5 after 50k, 75k, 100k,
150k, and 200k iterations. Training was conducted for 500k
iterations using the official 2,000-step linear diffusion schedule,
with variance endpoints of 10−6 and 10−2.
WCDM.
WCDM was trained from scratch using random
256 × 256 crops and a batch size of 12. The model was
optimized with Adam using a learning rate of 10−4 and zero
weight decay. The learning rate was multiplied by 0.8 every
5,000 iterations, and an exponential moving average with a
decay of 0.999 was maintained during training. Training was
conducted for 1,000 epochs, following the official training
schedule.
We used the official 200-step linear diffusion
schedule with variance endpoints of 10−4 and 2×10−2.
ReDDiT.
For each of LSD-DLL and UHD-LOL4K, we
followed the ReDDiT progressive-distillation procedure. We
first trained the required dataset-specific GSAD teacher from
scratch for 750 epochs in FP32 using random 96×96 crops.
Training was performed across eight GPUs with a batch size
of 8 per GPU, yielding an effective batch size of 64. We used
Adam with a learning rate of 10−4. The teacher objective
combined the ℓ1 reconstruction loss and the GSAD structural
loss with weights of 10 and 1, respectively, and employed a
500-step linear diffusion schedule.
Starting from the dataset-specific teacher, we first trained
an eight-step ReDDiT model and then progressively distilled it
into four-step and two-step students. Each distillation stage was
trained for 5,000 iterations using random 96×96 crops, a batch
size of 16, Adam with a learning rate of 10−4, and an exponen-
tial moving average with a decay of 0.9999. The ReDDiT stages
used a fine-grained diffusion-time discretization of T =512 with
a linear β-schedule whose endpoints were 10−4 and 2×10−2.

TVM Warmup Ablation.
Fig. 9 studies the effect of
gradually introducing the TVM objective during training. We
compare the proposed warmup-ramp schedule against a variant
trained without TVM warmup.
Without TVM warmup, the TVM objective is introduced
before the flow-matching model has learned a meaningful
low-to-normal-light transport. As a result, the displacement
objective is optimized with respect to an immature velocity
field, leading the optimization toward an inferior solution.
In contrast, the proposed warmup schedule allows the
flow-matching objective to first establish a reliable transport
before introducing the TVM objective, resulting in improved
PSNR and substantially better NIQE at convergence.
