# Abstract 

Real-time video generation, interactive world models, and on-device image synthesis are almost a reality. Diffusion Transformers (DiT) achieve excellent sample quality, shifting the bottleneck to the throughput of the models themselves. Natural images are inherently sparse, as large portions of an image are often background, contribute little to the scene content, and are easy to denoise. DiTs fail to exploit this sparsity, as they process image tokens statically and uniformly. We propose MoDDiT, a simple architecture mod-ification that learns to route tokens through both MLP and self-attention operations. By routing tokens through both blocks independently, MoD-DiT allows for dynamic compute allocation in MLPs that varies across tokens, images, and de-noising timesteps. Using MoDDiT to train image generation models from scratch significantly re-duces computational costs of training and infer-ence, processing as few as 12.5% of the tokens. By increasing sparsity and model depth, MoD-DiT reduces costs and improves generation per-formance over unrouted baselines. The learned allocation is interpretable, revealing how compu-tation varies with visual content and noise level. We also use MoDDiT to train video generation models, where allocation correlates with motion, and to distill a dense bidirectional video model into a sparse autoregressive student.

# 2. Background 

2.1. Diffusion Models 

Diffusion models (Sohl-Dickstein et al., 2015; Ho et al., 2020; Dhariwal & Nichol, 2021) generate images by itera-tively removing noise from samples drawn from a known noisy distribution. Specifically, they consist of two com-plementary Markov processes: a forward noising process and a reverse denoising process. In the forward process, an initial clean image x0 is progressively corrupted into a purely noisy image xT across a discrete sequence of timesteps t = 1 , . . . , T , following the Gaussian conditional distribution q(xt | xt−1) = N (xt; √1 − βt xt−1, β tI),where βt ∈ (0 , 1) defines the noise schedule at timestep 

t. Equivalently, this forward process can be expressed as xt = √¯αt x0 + √1 − ¯αt ϵ, with ϵ ∼ N (0 , I ) and 

¯αt = Qti=1 (1 − βi). The reverse process, parameterized by a neural network, gradually reconstructs the original clean image from xT to x0 by approximating the conditional dis-tributions pθ (xt−1 | xt) = N (xt−1; μθ (xt, t ), Σθ (xt, t )) .Model training involves minimizing the discrepancy be-tween the predicted and actual noise at each timestep, en-abling accurate modeling of the underlying data distribution. 2MoDDiT: Simple Token Routing in Diffusion Transformers 

2.2. Vision- and Diffusion Transformers 

Following their success in language modeling, transform-ers (Vaswani et al., 2017) have been successfully applied to vision tasks. Vision transformer (Dosovitskiy et al., 2020, ViT) achieves this by constructing a flattened token sequence from pixel patches. Similar to transformers, ViT models consist of alternating self-attention (Bahdanau et al., 2014) and multi-layer perceptron (MLP) blocks, with intermedi-ate layer normalization (Ba et al., 2016). In these models, self-attention distributes information across the sample, in this case passing information between image patches, while the MLP processes tokens independently. Diffusion trans-formers (DiT, Peebles & Xie, 2023) adapt ViT models for image diffusion with adaptive layer normalization (adaLN), which conditions the normalization operation on the diffu-sion timestep and image class. In addition to image generation, DiTs are the predominant architecture for video generation. Here, bidirectional video models such as Wan2.1 (Wan et al., 2025), which generate entire videos at once without causal constraints, are at the forefront of generation quality. To accelerate inference and allow for long-horizon generation, they are often finetuned into autoregressive models with fewer denoising steps (Yin et al., 2025; Huang et al., 2025; Zhu et al., 2026). MoDDiT is independent of, and compatible with these techniques. Outside of diffusion, various extensions to ViT have been proposed. Darcet et al. (2023) analyse the attention map of ViT models and find attention sinks: low-information image patches with anomalously high attention weights that pool global information. Therefore, the authors propose register tokens with learned embedding values that are appended to the image sequence and discarded at the end of the forward pass. We extend our models to use register tokens to im-prove compute allocation, inspired by Darcet et al. (2023), who demonstrate that removing the attention sinks from the image improves the performance and alignment of saliency maps with semantic features. 

2.3. Mixture-of-Experts Models 

Mixture-of-experts (MoE, Shazeer et al., 2017; Fedus et al., 2022) approaches improve transformer scaling by dividing the transformer’s MLP blocks into multiple expert MLPs, with each token typically routed to only a single expert, as determined by a routing module, a small MLP that computes scalar scores for each token-expert pair. These scores de-termine the assignment of tokens to experts via (potentially stochastic) top-K selection. While earlier approaches directly optimize stochastic routing decisions to minimize the expected loss using REINFORCE-style objectives (Bengio et al., 2015), recent work instead uses learned gating functions as the router, using the gate as a heuristic proxy for expert utility (Shazeer et al., 2017). (Raposo et al., 2024) use such a routing mech-anism for sparse token processing in language processing, and we propose combining it with dynamic MLP sparsity for image patches. 

# 3. Token Routing in Diffusion Transformers 

MoDDiT combines a token bypass (Krause et al., 2025) with separate routing for attention and MLPs (Zhao et al., 2025; 2026), allowing for dynamic allocation in MLPs without ragged batches (Shi et al., 2025; Yuan et al., 2025). 

3.1. Learning to Route Tokens 

At the core of our method is a simple routing mechanism (see fig. 2), similar to an MoE routing module as used in (Raposo et al., 2024; You et al., 2025). Each of our router blocks consists of a tiny, two-layer MLP Rϕ applied inde-pendently to each token ti with i ∈ 1, . . . , N to compute a scalar routing logit Rϕ(ti). During training, we apply stochastic Gumbel top-K (Jang et al., 2016; Kool et al., 2019) routing to encourage exploratory routing decisions. Specifically, for each token we sample gi ∼ Gumbel(0 , 1) 

and select the K tokens with the highest perturbed score,

3.2. Dynamic Allocation in MLP Routing 

MLPs process each token independently, allowing for a simple way to introduce dynamic sparsity. 

During training , instead of selecting the top-K tokens per sample, we can merge the batch and token dimensions and select the top-( B · K) tokens across the whole batch on each GPU (Shi et al., 2025; Yuan et al., 2025). Since samples in each batch belong to different classes and diffusion steps, the model can learn to dynamically allocate compute based on image class and diffusion step (section 4).

3.3. Fixed-Capacity Routing for Self-Attention 

In self-attention, the number of tokens in an example de-termines the dimensions of its attention matrix. For effi-cient parallel processing on GPUs, all examples in the same batch must use attention matrices with the same dimensions. Therefore, we cannot simply give each example a different number of tokens, because this would produce differently sized matrices and prevent efficient vectorized computation. We subsequently route a fixed number of K tokens for each example. Additionally, tokens have two distinct roles, each producing an output value and serving as context for other tokens to attend to. This opens up a design space for routing context (keys and values) and output (queries) independently, or routing based on relevance as context. We elaborate and show results for possible setups in appendix A.3.1, and find that the simple method in section 3.1 is among the best, so we select it for its simplicity. 

# 4. Results on ImageNet 

Experimental Setup We perform experiments on Ima-geNet (Deng et al., 2009), using the Face-blurred ILSVRC 2012–2017 variant (Yang et al., 2022) at 256 × 256 resolu-tion. We train all models from scratch for 1M steps using the DiT-L/2 configuration and all hyperparameters from Peebles & Xie (2023) without further tuning. This includes using the same pre-trained variational autoencoder (Kingma et al., 2013, VAE) from Stable Diffusion (Rombach et al., 2022), with an 8× downsample factor. To make MoDDiT comparable in FLOPs with DiT, we construct MoDDiT by replacing alternate DiT blocks with two MoDDiT blocks, approximately matching DiT FLOPs for a 50% capacity model with the same configuration. For more details, see appendix A.1. 

Evaluation Metrics We evaluate the performance of image generation using standard evaluation metrics of generative models: Fr´ echet Inception Distance (Heusel et al., 2017, FID), spatial FID (Nash et al., 2021, sFID), Inception Score (Salimans et al., 2016, IS), and Preci-sion/Recall (Kynk¨ a¨ anniemi et al., 2019), each using 50k samples. 

Results Figure 3 and appendix A.2 show the FLOPs-normalized performance of 50%, 25%, and 12.5% MoDDiT models, as well as a dense DiT baseline. The 50% MoDDiT model, which we constructed to match the baseline FLOPs, consistently outperforms the DiT baseline in FID, sFID, pre-cision, and IS. Notably, the 25% model achieves better FID, sFID, and IS, at a 22% reduction in training and inference FLOPs. Furthermore, the 12.5% model is competitive with DiT on all metrics, despite using 33% less FLOPs. 4MoDDiT: Simple Token Routing in Diffusion Transformers 

Ablations We experiment with multiple attention gating methods (see section 3.3), and present full results in ap-pendix A.3.1. Our evaluations show that no method uni-formly performs best. We thus settle for the simplest pos-sible attention gating setup, as visualized in fig. 2. Fur-thermore, appendix A.3.2 shows that learned token routing substantially improves over random token selection, while independent routing does not uniformly beat coupled rout-ing. Coupled routing has better FID, while the full method has better sFID. 

Compute Allocation over Noise Levels fig. 4 shows the compute allocation of samples over denoising steps, as well as the difference in diffusion loss of MoDDiT models with different capacity levels. Again, we show only the MLP routing proportion since the self-attention capacity is con-stant across samples. We see increased compute allocation at lower noise levels (smaller timesteps) for all models, as well as a greater difference in allocated capacity between models, validating the qualitative example in fig. 1. The difference in loss between MoDDiT models is roughly pro-portional to the difference in compute allocation between them, suggesting models spend their increased capacity at low noise levels to decrease loss. 

Compute Allocation over Classes We analyze compute allocation of MoDDiT models, and find that the variance within each image’s latent representation is a strong predic-tor of allocation (fig. 4). We hypothesize that classes with high-variance latent patches require more iterative refine-ment throughout the whole denoising process, for exam-ple, to update boundaries and relationships to surrounding patches. A uniform patch’s representation contains rela-tively less information, so additional computation adds little bonus. We find that lower-capacity models have weaker correlation, full results can be found in fig. 8. 

# 5. Results on 1X 

Experimental Setup We use the raw video dataset from the 1X World Modeling Challenge (1X, 2025), containing approximately 100 hours of humanoid robot POV video at 30 frames per second and 512 × 512 resolution, as well as continuous controller actions for the robot at every frame. To embed these videos for latent diffusion, we use the pre-trained Cosmos Tokenizer (NVIDIA, 2025), selecting the continuous variant with an 8× spatial and temporal down-sample factor. This embeds 8 contiguous video frames with dimension 8 × 512 × 512 × 3 into a latent frame with dimen-sion 1 × 64 × 64 × 16 . During training, we condition models on two latent context frames, equivalent to 16 image frames, as well as the next 8 robot actions (corresponding to 1 latent frame). We train models to denoise the next latent frame conditioned on this context, and autoregressively sample latent frames during evaluation. In order to support efficient training on video data, we also introduce spatio-temporal (ST) variants of DiT and MoDDiT, ST-DiT and ST-MoDDiT. These separate the self-attention operation into separate spatial and temporal at-tention operations, in which tokens attend to other tokens in the same frame or other tokens in the same position in 

previous frames, respectively. We compare ST-DiT and ST-MoDDiT using the DiT-L/2 configuration at 200,000 optimizer updates (appendix B.1). 

Results We evaluate the 200k EMA checkpoints on 400 paired validation clips, measuring PSNR at 0.5, 1.0 and 2.0

seconds after the observed context (table 1). ST-MoDDiT at 12.5% capacity improves PSNR by 0.677 , 0.376 and 

0.213 dB, respectively; all three pointwise 95% paired segment-bootstrap confidence intervals exclude zero. It uses 36.92% fewer counted denoiser FLOPs on the profiled clip. Sparse and dense models use training batches of 256 and 217, respectively, so equal optimizer-update counts do not imply equal training-data exposure. 

Compute Allocation over Temporal Pixel Difference 

Video generation models are conditioned on previous frames, making it trivial to denoise future tokens corre-sponding to static objects; they can just be copied over. We therefore hypothesise that dynamic objects with high optical flow should benefit from increased compute allocation in MoDDiT models. To evaluate this, we measure the alloca-tion percentage and absolute pixel difference in 8 consecu-tive frames (corresponding to the temporal downsampling factor of the video tokenizer). We visualise the result in fig. 5, which shows a strong positive correlation between a token’s frame difference and allocation frequency, confirm-ing that MoDDiT uses more FLOPs to generate frames with many dynamic objects (appendix B.2).

# 7. Related Work 

Transformers have become the predominant architecture for flow-based generative modeling, in both image generation with models such as DiT (Peebles & Xie, 2023), DALL-E 3 (Goh et al., 2023), and Latte (Ma et al., 2024), as well as video generation in GAIA-2 (Russell et al., 2025), Genie 2 (Parker-Holder et al., 2024), Mochi (GenmoAI, 2024), Sora (Brooks et al., 2024), MovieGen (Polyak et al., 2024), and Wan2.1 (Wan et al., 2025). This paradigm naturally invites adaptive compute allocation, since denoising diffi-culty varies across noise levels and spatio-temporal regions. Furthermore, many of the downstream applications of these models, such as large-scale image generation and real-time video generation, have strict constraints on latency that re-quire fast inference. Despite this, adaptive computation techniques have not yet been adopted in state-of-the-art models. However, interest in this research area has recently increased. Wang et al. ToCa (Zou et al., 2025) and RAS (Liu et al., 2026) reuse computation across denoising timesteps through cached token features and noise predictions for inactive regions, respectively. Dif-fCR (You et al., 2025) routes tokens through or around whole transformer blocks and learns layer- and timestep-specific capacities with an auxiliary budget loss, evaluated by fine-tuning pretrained diffusion models. More closely related, DyDiT (Zhao et al., 2025) and Dy-DiT++ (Zhao et al., 2026) combine timestep-conditioned width reduction with content-dependent token bypass in MLP blocks, using an auxiliary loss to control overall FLOPs. They require full self-attention during training, so sparsity is only introduced in inference. TREAD (Krause et al., 2025) accelerates training by randomly selecting to-kens to bypass sequences of transformer blocks, while re-taining dense computation at inference. Our method learns separate token routers for attention and MLP blocks jointly with the diffusion model from scratch, without auxiliary losses, while preserving tensor shapes for efficient batch inference with per-sample flexibility in the MLP blocks. Mixture-of-experts mechanisms (Shazeer et al., 2017; Fe-dus et al., 2022, MoE) allocate computation by routing tokens among multiple MLP experts, as in EC-DiT (Sun et al., 2025). DiffMoE (Shi et al., 2025) pools tokens across examples and learns a capacity predictor for adaptive in-ference; Expert Race (Yuan et al., 2025) combines global token–expert competition with tracked-threshold inference. MoDDiT shares these allocation principles but applies them to a single MLP branch, combines them with independently routed sparse attention, and trains the model from scratch without a separately supervised capacity predictor or auxil-iary routing objectives. Outside of diffusion modeling, adaptive computation has be-come increasingly prevalent in transformer models, in order to allocate resources efficiently based on input complexity. Xin et al. (2020) propose a language model architecture with adaptive depth via an early exiting mechanism. Inspiring this work, mixture-of-depths (Raposo et al., 2024) uses a routing mechanism to limit the capacity of transformer lay-ers in autoregressive language models. While this method shows improvements in the FLOPs-normalized performance frontier of such models, we find diffusion to be an even more suitable paradigm for this architecture, as the models avoid causal structure interfering with the top-K operation. 

# 8. Conclusion 

Summary MoDDiT represents a significant advancement in efficient generative modeling, effectively addressing a computational inefficiency inherent in existing diffusion models by dynamically allocating compute across tokens, samples, and noise levels. By employing a minimal yet pow-erful routing mechanism, MoDDiT demonstrates consider-able performance improvements at the same level of com-pute, on both image and video generation tasks. Our evalua-tion also provides an intuitive understanding of the learned routing mechanism, highlighting MoDDiT’s capability to align computational effort with semantic importance and temporal dynamics. Limitations and Future Work While our work provides an extensive evaluation, it is ultimately limited by our com-putational resources, providing the opportunity for further evaluation with alternate datasets, architectures, and gener-ative algorithms. Namely, image generation datasets such as ArtBench (Liao et al., 2022) and Food (Bossard et al., 2014), as well as video generation datasets such as Taichi-HD (Siarohin et al., 2019) and Minecraft VPT (Baker et al., 2022), would strengthen confidence in the performance of MoDDiT. While MoDDiT can substantially reduce training and infer-ence FLOPs, this reduction does not automatically trans-late into wall clock time savings. Even though we observe throughput gains at larger batch sizes 1, generating few images in parallel is slower. This is likely due to a combi-

# A. ImageNet experiments 

A.1. Training and evaluation Dataset We perform experiments on ImageNet (Deng et al., 2009), using the Face-blurred ILSVRC 2012–2017 vari-ant (Yang et al., 2022) at 256 × 256 resolution. 

Model Architecture We train latent diffusion models using the pre-trained variational autoencoder (Kingma et al., 2013, VAE) from Stable Diffusion (Rombach et al., 2022), with an 8× downsample factor, giving a latent embedding with dimensions 32 × 32 × 4. Our baseline is the DiT-L/2 architecture from Peebles & Xie (2023). To make MoDDiT comparable in FLOPs with DiT, we construct MoDDiT by replacing alternate DiT blocks with two MoDDiT blocks, approximately matching DiT FLOPs for a 50% capacity model with the same configuration. Finally, MoDDiT models append every latent image with 16 register tokens (Darcet et al., 2023), as we found this to help with aligning allocation masks with semantic features, giving a total embedding length of 272. We initialize the final router layer at zero, such that the output is constant 

0.5. We use a cosine decay for router Gumbel noise, decaying from 5 to 0 over training. 

Training We train all models from scratch for 1M steps (requiring approximately 6 days on 4 L40S GPUs) using hyperparameters from Peebles & Xie (2023) without further tuning. This includes using a global batch size of 256 with AdamW using a learning rate of 10 −4, β = (0 .9, 0.999) , and no weight decay. We use an EMA value of 0.9999 throughout training. 

Evaluation We evaluate all models using 50 , 000 generated images at 64 , 128 , 256 , and 512 denoising steps and a classifier-free guidance scale of 1.5. We measure FLOPs on the first 1024 generated images, including both classifier-free guidance branches, excluding the VAE decoder.

A.3. Routing design studies 

A.3.1. A TTENTION -ROUTING ALTERNATIVES 

Gating values serve as a proxy for token selection. But what form of gating produces the most useful proxy? Multiplying the attention output by the router score incentivises retaining tokens whose outputs reduce loss. However, there is no incentive to increase the scores of tokens that serve as useful context for others to attend to. To provide such a signal, we may additionally gate the attention values using the routing scores. Let A = softmax( QK T /√d) and R = diag( r). While standard output gating computes R attn( x) = RAV , additionally gating the attention value yields 

RARV = ( rr ⊤ ⊙ A)V where (rr ⊤ ⊙ A)ij = riaij rj .

This formulation introduces complementary incentives to increase the score Rϕ(ti) of a token ti:• Output gating: Does ti reduce downstream loss by attending to other tokens? • Value gating: Do other tokens decrease downstream loss by attending to ti?Disentangling both incentives by producing two routing scores opens a substantial design space. For example, consolidating both scores for top-K selection can be done as a sum, product, or harmonic mean. Additionally, instead of consolidating scores, one may select two different top-K subsets of tokens for Q and K/V , and compute cross-attention, or implement sparsity only in keys and values. We test four of these configurations: • Output-only : the default MoDDiT, gating only the self-attention output via RAV .• Shared value/output : gating the output and value by the same routing score via RARV .• Separate routers : two routers per attention operation, producing RO and RV , and selecting tokens independently, then gating as RO AR V V .• Sparse Q/dense KV : All tokens enter attention as context, but only selected tokens contribute to the output. # B. 1X video prediction 

B.1. Training and evaluation Dataset We use the 1X World Modeling Dataset (1X, 2025), with 512 × 512 video at 30 Hz and 25-dimensional robot states. 

Model architecture We use the 12 .5% -capacity DiT-L/2, block-replacement scheme and register count from appendix A.1. The frozen Cosmos CV8x8x8 tokenizer (NVIDIA, 2025) produces 64 × 64 × 16 latent frames. Each frame contains 1024 spatial tokens and eight state tokens in addition to the registers. We adopt spatiotemporal attention from (Russell et al., 2025), in which a token at a fixed spatial position and timestep attends to all tokens at the same timestep, and all tokens at the same spatial position in the past. 

Training We train from scratch with seed 0, using the AdamW settings and weight EMA from appendix A.1. Global batch sizes are 256 for ST-MoDDiT and 217 for ST-DiT. The learning rate warms up for 5,000 steps, then follows a cosine schedule towards 10 −5 at 1M steps. Routing uses Gumbel-sigmoid temperature 5. 

Inference We encode the first 17 frames, then autoregressively predict eight latents (64 frames), retaining three context latents and conditioning on the next eight recorded robot states. Sampling uses 128 DDPM steps per latent and the classifier-free guidance scale from appendix A.1. 

Evaluation For table 1, we evaluate both models’ EMA checkpoints at exactly 200,000 optimizer updates on 400 paired, non-overlapping 81-frame windows from 249 segments in one video from the raw validation v2.0 split. Clip identities and sampling seeds are shared across models. PSNR is computed against raw RGB frames at 0.5, 1 and 2 seconds after the observed context, averaging squared error across pixels and channels before conversion to dB. We report the clip-weighted mean and paired sparse-minus-dense gains. Pointwise 95% percentile confidence intervals use 20,000 paired bootstrap draws over segments, with seed 20261011, retaining all windows within each sampled segment. These intervals reflect evaluation variability, not training-seed variability. The temporal-change analysis in appendix B.2 uses its separately saved 300k, 100-clip cohort. We count denoiser FLOPs over the first clip’s complete eight-latent rollout (128 DDPM calls per latent, CFG 1.5), including both guidance branches and all dense and routed blocks. Registered matrix, convolution and attention operations count a multiply-add as two FLOPs; tokenizer, scalar/nonlinear, normalization, selection and I/O work are excluded. The table reports total rollout TFLOPs, not per-call GFLOPs or measured throughput.

# C. Self-Forcing adaptation 

C.1. Sparse architecture and adaptation 

We follow the chunk-wise Self-Forcing DMD protocol (Huang et al., 2025), including its prompt data, optimization settings, and inference procedure, with the following sparse adaptation details. 

Model architecture Starting from the released ODE checkpoint, we keep every third block dense and add separate routers of hidden width 256 for self-attention queries and MLP tokens in the remaining blocks. Keys, values, and text cross-attention remain dense, and we use straight-through gates to keep the output magnitude of the pretrained model. Attention uses fixed-count selection; MLP selection is fixed-count for Funnel20 and threshold-based for Funnel50 (appendix C.1). 

Capacity schedules We experiment with two MoDDiT models with capacity schedules we call Funnel20 and Funnel50 .Across the 30 blocks, target capacity decreases linearly from 100% at block 1 to 20% or 50% at block 30, respectively. Blocks 1, 4, 7, . . . , 28 override this schedule and remain fully dense. The names are chosen as the final-block targets, not the average model-wide capacity. Initial qualitative experiments showed that increasing capacity in early blocks could recover structure that was otherwise lost under aggressive sparsity. We also retain dense keys and values, including cached context, so selected queries can attend to all available tokens, including tokens that receive no attention update. 

Training We jointly adapt the student and routers for 3,000 ODE updates, reducing capacity to the target with a cosine schedule over the first 600 updates. Trajectories use 48 sampling steps and guidance scale 6, retaining five states each. We then run 25 critic warmup iterations and 1,000 DMD iterations, starting student EMA at iteration 200. 

Evaluation We compare the final student EMA with the released dense EMA checkpoint from Self Forcing. VBench and compute protocols are in appendix C.2; qualitative comparisons are in appendix C.3. 

C.2.
