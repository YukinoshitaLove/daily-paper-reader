# ABSTRACT 

High-quality image editing requires precise modification of target regions while preserving the content of unrelated regions. Existing editing models produce flat-tened outputs that limit precise local manipulation and layer reuse, while decom-position methods typically optimize layer recovery separately from instruction-driven editing. To this end, we introduce LayerEditor , a unified framework that couples instruction-guided image editing with controllable generation of reusable RGBA layers. At its core, In-Context RGB–Alpha Token Modeling jointly rep-resents the edited canvas and an ordered sequence of RGB–Alpha layers within a single token sequence, enabling semantic edits and explicit control over indi-vidual layer content. To retain the pretrained model’s editing capability during adaptation to layered outputs, we propose Compositional Edit-Prior Distilla-tion , which transfers its editing prior to both the edited canvas and the image composed from the predicted layers. To support training and evaluation, we con-struct LayerEditor-100K , a large-scale paired image-editing dataset with ordered RGBA layer annotations, and introduce dedicated benchmarks for layered editing. Extensive experiments demonstrate that LayerEditor maintains strong editing fi-delity while generating accurate RGBA layers, supporting both flexible layer-level manipulation and multi-layer decomposition. 

# 1 INTRODUCTION 

Instruction-driven image editing aims to modify specified content while preserving unrelated re-gions of the source image. Recent editing models (Wu et al., 2025; Super Intelligence Team, 2026) commonly employ multimodal diffusion transformers to jointly model textual instructions and im-ages within a unified token sequence, synthesizing a single flattened edited canvas in which text, objects, and background content are fused together. Such flattened outputs make it difficult to ma-nipulate fine-grained details or incorporate detailed user-provided elements, which is crucial for downstream applications such as e-commerce content production and advertising design. Subse-quent revisions therefore require repeated generation, increasing processing overhead and risking unintended changes to unrelated content and boundary artifacts. Image decomposition provides a natural way to recover explicit layers from such flattened re-sults (Yin et al., 2025; He et al., 2026; Tang et al., 2026). Decomposition and image editing can be combined into layered workflows, as illustrated by Agent Banana (Ye et al., 2026b). Yet edit-ing and decomposition are typically optimized as separate stages with different objectives. Editing models focus on producing visually faithful results without considering the desired layer structure, while decomposition models recover layers after editing without jointly optimizing the requested edit and target layer structure. Consequently, even visually plausible edits may yield incomplete or poorly organized layers that are difficult to reuse. Correcting these layer-level errors often requires repeated model calls and manual refinement, increasing labor costs while reducing the efficiency and

In this work, we introduce LayerEditor, a unified model that couples instruction-driven image edit-ing with controllable generation of reusable RGBA layers, as illustrated in Figure 1. We propose In-Context RGB–Alpha Token Modeling to jointly represent the edited canvas and ordered RGB–Alpha layers within one token sequence. A dedicated Alpha decoder further recovers continuous opacity for fine structures and semitransparent boundaries. Moreover, directly extending a pretrained editing model to layered outputs tends to degrade its original editing capability, i.e., the edited content ex-hibits quality drops or artifacts. To mitigate this degradation, we introduce on-policy Compositional Edit-Prior Distillation (C-EPD) which facilitates the model’s adaptation to layered outputs while maintaining its pre-trained generative editing capacity. C-EPD queries a frozen editing teacher at intermediate states along the student’s denoising trajectory, where the teacher supervises the direct edited canvas and the composition of layers predicted by the layered editing student. Extensive experiments demonstrate that LayerEditor preserves strong editing performance while supporting flexible layered outputs with variable layer counts and user-specified layer content. To further provide paired supervision for both editing outcomes and their underlying layer struc-tures, we construct LayerEditor-100K , a large-scale dataset tailored to instruction-driven layered image editing. Each example contains a source image, editing instruction, edited target, and ordered RGBA layer stack, covering diverse text, object, and appearance edits with variable layer counts. In addition, since existing benchmarks do not adequately evaluate instruction-driven layered editing, we construct Crello-Testset and ScaleEdit-Testset , with dedicated settings for layer-count control and user-specified layer content, enabling systematic evaluation of editing fidelity, layer quality, and layered controllability. • We introduce LayerEditor, a unified model for editing images into reusable RGBA lay-ers through In-Context RGB–Alpha Token Modeling. LayerEditor supports variable layer counts and user-specified layer content. • We construct LayerEditor-100K, a large-scale dataset tailored to layered editing, contain-ing annotated image pairs with ordered RGBA layers, together with two benchmarks, Crello-Testset and ScaleEdit-Testset, for evaluating layered editing quality and control-lability. • We propose C-EPD, an on-policy distillation strategy that transfers the pretrained editing prior to layered outputs, preserving editing fidelity during layered adaptation. 

# 2 RELATED WORK 

2.1 CONDITIONAL IMAGE EDITING 

Image editing aims to modify a source image according to textual instructions while preserving unrelated content. InstructPix2Pix (Brooks et al., 2023) established paired image–instruction su-2Preprint pervision for instruction-driven editing, while Qwen-Image (Wu et al., 2025) and FireRed-Image-Edit (Super Intelligence Team, 2026) further improved editing fidelity and instruction following with stronger generative priors. Related methods explored selective reconstruction, interactive control, and temporal consistency for more fine-grained manipulation (Wen et al., 2024; Xue et al., 2025; 2024). More recently, agentic systems extended image editing into multi-step planning and exe-cution workflows. Visual ChatGPT (Wu et al., 2023) coordinated specialized visual tools through language-based planning, PhotoAgent (Yao et al., 2026) performed iterative aesthetic editing with search and visual feedback, and Agent Banana (Ye et al., 2026b) incorporated hierarchical planning and layer decomposition for localized editing. Despite improved editing quality and control, these approaches either produce flattened outputs or rely on cascaded workflows to recover editable layers. 2.2 LAYERED IMAGE GENERATION 

Existing layered visual modeling mainly focuses on layered generation and structured editing. Lay-erDiff (Huang et al., 2024), PSDiffusion (Huang et al., 2025), LayeringDiff (Kang et al., 2025), and LASAGNA (Yang et al., 2026) generate coordinated layers from text or spatial conditions. For graphic design, COLE (Jia et al., 2023), CreatiPoster (Zhang et al., 2025), and Editable Visual Design (Ye et al., 2026a) organize generated assets into editable structures, while MiLDEdit (Lin et al., 2026) and MRT (Tang et al., 2026) extend generation and editing to layered inputs. More recently, LaDe (Lungu-Stan et al., 2026) jointly generates design canvases and RGBA layers, and UniWorld-Design (Li et al., 2026b) supports layered generation and prompt-controlled decompo-sition. These methods emphasize layered generation and decomposition rather than jointly editing a flattened source image and generating its edited layer stack. Image decomposition methods such as Qwen-Image-Layered (Yin et al., 2025) and LiWi (He et al., 2026) recover editable components from rendered images, with related transparency-aware methods improving opacity and boundary quality (Rhemann et al., 2009; Zhang & Agrawala, 2024; Yu et al., 2026). Decomposition is typi-cally optimized separately from instruction-driven editing. LayerEditor addresses this gap by jointly editing a flattened source image and generating the corresponding reusable RGBA layers. 2.3 ON-P OLICY DISTILLATION 

On-policy distillation (OPD) transfers knowledge from a teacher to a student by querying the teacher online on states actually visited by the student. By supervising the student’s own trajectories, OPD reduces the mismatch between teacher-generated training data and the student’s evolving state dis-tribution (Agarwal et al., 2024). Recent studies extended this idea to flow-based generative models. Flow-OPD (Fang et al., 2026) distilled specialized teachers through student rollouts and manifold anchoring, DiffusionOPD (Li et al., 2026a) studied continuous-state transition alignment, and STEP-OPD (Wei et al., 2026) further explored extrapolated targets and intermediate representation align-ment. Unlike progressive distillation (Salimans & Ho, 2022) and consistency models (Song et al., 2023), which mainly reduced the number of sampling steps, our goal is to preserve editing capability when extending a pretrained editor from single-image outputs to structured layered outputs.

# 4 EXPERIMENTS 

4.1 EXPERIMENT SETUPS 

Implementation Details LayerEditor is initialized from FireRed-Image-Edit-1.1 and adapted us-ing rank-256 LoRA, with a dedicated Alpha decoder for opacity reconstruction. Training begins with two epochs of Lflow , followed by joint optimization with C-EPD using AdamW at a learning rate of 2 × 10 −5 with a batch size of 64. Images retain their aspect ratios within a 1024 × 1024 pixel budget. Further details are provided in Appendix A. 

Competing Methods LayerEditor is compared with representative image editing and layer-decomposition baselines using their default or official inference settings. QIL, QIL-Decomp-FT, and RevealLayer use the requested layer count without content descriptions. QIL-Control, QIL-Edit-SFT, Seedream5-Pro, and LayerEditor additionally receive the same ordered layer-content specifications. Seedream5-Pro (ByteDance Seed, 2026) is evaluated through its official API un-der two settings: a sequential pipeline that performs editing followed by decomposition, and a joint setting that requests editing and layer decomposition in a single API call. We also construct cas-caded baselines by combining FireRed-Image-Edit-1.1 (FR) (Super Intelligence Team, 2026) with Qwen-Image-Layered (QIL) (Yin et al., 2025), QIL-Control (DiffSynth-Studio Team, 2026), or RevealLayer (Wang et al., 2026). Evaluation Protocols We evaluate layered editing performance primarily through the recomposed image C, since the requested edit should remain correct after the predicted RGBA layers are com-bined. Direct and Composite SSIM/FID (Wang et al., 2004; Heusel et al., 2017) measure similarity to the edited target, with the Direct output serving as an auxiliary canvas diagnostic. RGBA L1 and Alpha IoU assess reference-layer agreement after matching and ordered pairing of remaining layers, as detailed in Appendix C.2. To complement reference-based metrics, we use a unified five-dimensional rubric with VLM-assisted scoring for all methods. The rubric assesses edit consistency, visual quality, layer segmentation, layer composition, and Alpha accuracy from the recomposed out-put together with the corresponding target and reference layers. # 5 CONCLUSIONS 

In this work, we introduce LayerEditor, a unified model that couples instruction-driven image edit-ing with controllable generation of reusable RGBA layers. In-Context RGB–Alpha Token Modeling captures cross-layer dependencies and appearance–transparency interactions, while a dedicated Al-pha decoder refines opacity for fine structures. C-EPD transfers the pretrained editing prior to both 9Preprint the edited canvas and composed layer predictions, helping preserve editing fidelity during layered adaptation. We further construct LayerEditor-100K for paired training supervision and establish dedicated benchmarks for evaluating layered image editing. Extensive experiments demonstrate strong recomposition fidelity, accurate layer separation, and flexible control over layer counts and content. By generating independently editable layers alongside the edited canvas, LayerEditor en-ables fine-grained revisions and content reuse beyond the initial edit.

# B LAYER EDITOR -100K C ONSTRUCTION 

B.1 DATA CONSTRUCTION PIPELINE 

LayerEditor-100K combines two routes for obtaining paired editing supervision and reusable target layers (Figure 8). Every accepted record contains a source image, instruction, edited target, and or-dered full-canvas RGBA stack. Layer annotations provide RGBA supervision and aligned semantic descriptions for training the structured prompt interface (Appendix A.1). 

Route A: native layered designs. Crello and PrismLayersPro provide native assets with positions, stacking order, and transparency (Yamaguchi, 2021; Chen et al., 2025). We first render and consol-idate the assets into 2–10 ordered layers, then plan the edit while keeping key text, subjects, and target regions separate where possible. Layout changes, movement, and deletion are handled deter-ministically, while generative editing is used for text redrawing, object replacement, and appearance modification. The edited content is finally combined with preserved RGBA layers to form the target stack, followed by semantic and visual quality verification. 

Route B: flattened editing pairs. ScaleEdit source–target pairs (Chen et al., 2026) are screened for effective edits, readable content, and suitability for layered editing. A planner proposes layer roles and granularity, and QIL (Yin et al., 2025) and Seedream5-Pro (ByteDance Seed, 2026) gen-erate decomposition candidates. Candidates are standardized to full-canvas RGBA stacks with a complete bottom background and transparent foreground support. Repairable failures receive at most two rounds of local correction for text defects, missing content, leakage, or Alpha artifacts, followed by full-stack review. Severe failures are rejected, and repairs are retained only when they improve the candidate. The original target is preserved when reconstruction passes. A repaired com-position becomes the target only after independent verification of instruction execution and content preservation. Both versions and the revision reason are retained. 

Benchmark construction. Crello-Testset and ScaleEdit-Testset are constructed separately from the native-design and flattened-pair source groups, with no training-source overlap. Their main editing results are evaluated separately. To construct the two controllability settings, we combine all samples from both benchmarks, modify the editing prompt or requested layer count, and revise each target and reference stack to match the modified request. Count-only changes regroup compatible layers while preserving the intended edited appearance. Instruction changes update both edited content and its reference layers. Each derived case fixes its source, conditions, target, and reference stack before inference. Target pixels and reference RGBA arrays are withheld from generation. Their annotations provide the supplied layer-content specifications. 16 Preprint        

> Offline target-layer annotation and acceptance. Count and order: exactly Nnonempty layers in valid back-to-front order.
> Content isolation: complete intended components without duplication, cross-layer leakage, or unrelated background residue.

FR + QIL. FireRed edits the source, and pretrained QIL decomposes the result using the requested layer count (Super Intelligence Team, 2026; Yin et al., 2025). The FR stage uses 40 sampling steps, guidance of 4, and aspect-ratio-preserving dimensions aligned to multiples of 16 within a 1024 2

pixel budget. All FR-based cascades reuse these edited canvases within each setting, including the supplementary instruction-control experiment. 

FR + QIL-Control. QIL-Control replaces QIL in the decomposition stage (DiffSynth-Studio Team, 2026). It uses text-conditioned extraction with the same ordered layer-content descriptions supplied to LayerEditor, and the requested count specifies the size of the assembled stack. No task-specific fine-tuning is applied. 

QIL-Decomp-FT. QIL is fine-tuned on LayerEditor-100K targets and RGBA annotations follow-ing the official decomposition-training pipeline. It decomposes FR edits for layered editing or the source image for I2L, using the requested count without benchmark layer-content descriptions. 

QIL-Edit-SFT. This adapted QIL baseline learns canvas and layer generation directly from source images, editing instructions, requested counts, and layer-content descriptions. It uses the same con-tent specifications as LayerEditor, with paired targets and RGBA stacks as supervision. 

RevealLayer. RevealLayer (Wang et al., 2026) decomposes the FR edit without task-specific fine-tuning. Its bounding-box guidance uses the benchmark reference annotations. The requested count guides repeated extraction and stack assembly, without benchmark layer-content descriptions. Post-generation correspondence supports evaluation when the recovered organization differs from the reference. 

LayerD. LayerD (Suzuki et al., 2025) is included in the I2L comparison. We follow the official inference procedure and repeatedly invoke decomposition to assemble a stack with the requested target layer count. These sequential calls incur substantial time cost as more layers are extracted. They are part of the decomposition pipeline, not a single joint prediction of all layers. We use the pretrained model without task-specific fine-tuning. C.2 METRICS AND LAYER MATCHING 

Rendering and image metrics. Direct metrics compare the exported canvas D with the target. Composite metrics compare the target with C, recomputed from all exported layers in generated order on white. SSIM uses target-aligned images with the same size. Rendering uses the final exported stack without evaluation-time registration or reference-guided repair. Correspondence matching for layer metrics does not reorder the rendered stack. Differences between Direct and Composite SSIM compare their respective similarities to the target. They do not measure SSIM between D and C.

# E LIMITATIONS 

LayerEditor is trained and evaluated with at most ten RGBA layers, leaving larger stacks unexplored. Thin structures, complex transparency, and semitransparent boundaries can still produce artifacts during layer movement or recomposition. Text remains rasterized rather than represented as editable font or vector primitives, limiting downstream typography manipulation. The current results do not establish lossless source reconstruction or artifact-free downstream reuse. Quantitative evaluation measures layer isolation, opacity, and recomposition in the evaluated lay-outs. We do not systematically test reconstruction after layer manipulation or validate VLM scores against human judgments. Editing-prior retention is evaluated on the constructed benchmarks rather than an independent general-editing test. Automatically constructed annotations and model-based assessments may contain errors and model-specific biases.

# F ETHICS STATEMENT 

LayerEditor enables instruction-driven image editing and reusable layered representations. As with other image editing systems, it may be misused to alter visual content deceptively or modify copy-righted assets without authorization. Our work is intended for research on controllable image edit-ing and structured visual representations. LayerEditor-100K is constructed from existing research datasets and design resources, and we preserve source provenance throughout data processing. We do not intentionally collect additional private user information. Automated vision-language models are used for semantic verification and quality assessment, and we combine model-based evaluations with deterministic checks and structured quality-control procedures. Because layered outputs make subsequent visual manipulation easier, responsible deployment should consider content provenance, copyright, and safeguards against deceptive editing. 23 Preprint
