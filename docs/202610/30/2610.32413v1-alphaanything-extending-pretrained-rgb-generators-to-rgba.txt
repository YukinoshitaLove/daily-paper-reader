Abstract
Existing RGBA generation methods commonly learn a transparency-specific representation through reconstruction,
introducing an additional training stage and potentially altering the RGB decoding function inherited from pre-
trained models. We present ALPHAANYTHING, a lightweight framework that extends pretrained RGB generators
to RGBA directly in their original latent space, without RGBA reconstruction pretraining. ALPHAANYTHING
inserts LoRA modules into the generative transformer and attaches an alpha branch to the frozen RGB VAE
decoder. Unidirectional RGB-to-alpha feature sharing allows opacity supervision to shape the generative latent
while guaranteeing that, for any fixed latent, RGB decoding remains identical to that of the pretrained decoder.
We further introduce Endpoint-Aware Alpha Loss (EA Loss), which derives opacity supervision from the comple-
mentary foreground–background weights used in compositing. It supports exact opacity endpoints and continuous
partial transparency while emphasizing localized alpha errors. Experiments on text-to-RGBA and image-to-RGBA
tasks across multiple pretrained RGB backbones demonstrate competitive generation quality, and controlled
ablations validate the shared-latent training design and EA Loss.

INTRODUCTION
Pretrained image generators can synthesize high-quality visual content from text or image conditions (Rombach et al.,
2022; Wu et al., 2025; 2026). Many downstream applications, however, require assets that can be directly layered and
composited into new scenes, rather than RGB images tied to a fixed background (Fontanella et al., 2024; Yin et al.,
2026). RGBA images support this use case by representing appearance and opacity separately: the RGB channels
describe foreground color, while the alpha channel controls how the foreground is blended with its background (Xu
et al., 2017; Zhang & Agrawala, 2024). A practical RGBA extension should therefore add transparency generation while
reusing the conditioning and appearance priors of a pretrained RGB model.
Existing RGBA generation methods commonly introduce a transparency-specific representation through reconstruction
training. LayerDiffuse learns a transparency encoder and decoder with reconstruction supervision (Zhang & Agrawala,
2024), while AlphaVAE and Qwen-Image-Layered train dedicated four-channel autoencoders (Wang et al., 2025b; Yin
et al., 2026). Although effective, this strategy adds a separate representation-learning stage and couples subsequent
generative training to the reconstructed RGBA latent space. Updating an RGB autoencoder for RGBA reconstruction
can also change its pretrained RGB decoding function, with potential losses in color or high-frequency detail. Zippo (Xie
et al., 2024) shows that a dedicated RGBA VAE is not strictly necessary by encoding replicated alpha with a pretrained
RGB VAE, but it still models appearance and opacity with separate latent states. These observations motivate a simpler
question: can a pretrained RGB generator learn opacity directly in its existing latent space, using generative supervision
alone?
We answer this question with ALPHAANYTHING, a lightweight framework that augments a pretrained RGB generator
with a shared-latent alpha pathway. We insert LoRA modules (Hu et al., 2022) into the generative transformer and
attach a trainable alpha branch to the frozen RGB VAE decoder. During training, flow-matching supervision updates the
transformer LoRA, while opacity supervision passes through the frozen decoder features to shape the same predicted

Learning opacity also requires an objective that reflects its role in compositing. Alpha is not merely a bounded image
channel: a = 0 and a = 1 denote exact transparency and opacity, intermediate values encode partial visibility, and
(1 −a, a) gives the complementary background–foreground mixing weights. We therefore introduce Endpoint-Aware
Alpha Loss (EA Loss). EA Loss represents this compositing pair on the Bernoulli simplex and uses its Fisher–Rao
coordinate (Miyamoto et al., 2024) to parameterize the full opacity range. An endpoint-compatible Fenchel–Young
formulation represents exact endpoints with finite scores and retains corrective gradients when a prediction saturates at
the wrong endpoint. The same complementary weights further separate background- and foreground-associated errors
before root aggregation, increasing the relative contribution of localized defects such as thin structures, small holes, and
background leakage. We characterize these properties theoretically and evaluate the complete objective against standard
alpha L1 supervision.
The paper makes three contributions. (1) Shared-latent RGBA extension. We show that pretrained RGB generators
can acquire text- and image-conditioned RGBA generation without RGBA reconstruction pretraining. A unidirectional
RGB-to-alpha decoder branch lets opacity supervision shape the generative latent while preserving the pretrained RGB
decoding function for every fixed latent. (2) Endpoint-aware opacity supervision. We derive EA Loss from the
complementary compositing weights of alpha. The objective accommodates exact endpoints and continuous partial
transparency while emphasizing spatially localized alpha errors, and we establish its key optimization and error-control
properties. (3) Evaluation across tasks and backbones. We instantiate ALPHAANYTHING on multiple pretrained RGB
generators and evaluate it in both text-to-RGBA and image-to-RGBA settings, where it achieves competitive generation
quality without an RGBA autoencoder or a separate alpha latent.

RELATED WORK
RGB image generation. Modern diffusion and flow-based image generators learn powerful text- and image-conditioned
visual priors in pretrained RGB latent spaces (Rombach et al., 2022; Peebles & Xie, 2023; Wu et al., 2025; 2026). These
models provide strong appearance synthesis and editing capabilities, but their outputs remain conventional three-channel
images whose content is tied to an RGB background. Parameter-efficient training methods such as LoRA (Hu et al.,
2022) further show that new generative capabilities can be introduced while largely reusing a pretrained backbone. Our
work builds on this premise, extending pretrained RGB generators with opacity generation rather than replacing their
learned RGB representation.
RGBA generation and representations. Image matting estimates opacity for content already observed in an RGB
image, classically with auxiliary spatial guidance such as a trimap or mask (Xu et al., 2017). It therefore differs from
RGBA generation: matting operates on visible image evidence rather than semantic generation instructions, does not
jointly synthesize appearance and opacity, and typically recovers the visible matte rather than completing object layers
behind occlusions. Existing native RGBA methods adopt different transparency representations. LayerDiffuse learns
a latent transparency representation and decoder through reconstruction training (Zhang & Agrawala, 2024), while
AlphaVAE and Qwen-Image-Layered train dedicated four-channel autoencoders (Wang et al., 2025b; Yin et al., 2026).
These approaches introduce an additional reconstruction-trained representation to support transparency generation. Zippo
takes a different route by reusing a pretrained RGB VAE and representing alpha through replicated RGB channels (Xie
et al., 2024); however, color and matte are still modeled with separate latent states. These works show that transparency
can be incorporated through substantially different representation choices. ALPHAANYTHING instead learns RGBA
generation in the original RGB latent space while retaining the pretrained RGB autoencoder.
3
ALPHAANYTHING
Our central insight is that RGBA generation can be learned in the original RGB latent space. ALPHAANYTHING realizes
this with DiT LoRA modules and a lightweight alpha branch embedded in the frozen RGB VAE decoder. Within the
alpha branch, features are shared unidirectionally from RGB to alpha, preserving the original RGB decoding process for
any fixed latent, while gradients from the alpha loss propagate through the decoder to update the DiT LoRA parameters.
Our Endpoint-Aware Alpha Loss (EA Loss) further provides supervision for both exact endpoints and continuous
opacity. Figure 2 summarizes the framework.

PRELIMINARIES

CB(It) = Iα ⊙Ic + (1 −Iα) ⊙B = Ip + (1 −Iα) ⊙B,

where Ip = Iα ⊙Ic is the premultiplied foreground and opacity is broadcast over color channels. The endpoints Iα,i = 0
and 1 represent complete transparency and opacity; intermediate values represent partial transparency. For brevity, we
denote Iα by a below.
RGB training targets. As implied by the compositing equation in (1), a composite does not uniquely determine its
foreground color–opacity pair. In particular, when opacity vanishes, the stored RGB values are physically undefined
and do not affect the composite (Zhang & Agrawala, 2024). However, generative training in the pretrained RGB
latent space requires a well-defined RGB target over the entire image, including transparent regions; leaving these
values unconstrained would make the training target underdetermined. We therefore use the multiscale color-padding
convention of LayerDiffuse to define a dense RGB target:
eIc = Pad(Ic, a),
z0 = E(eIc).
(2)
The padding propagates foreground color from coarse to fine into transparent regions, providing a structured RGB target
from which the frozen RGB encoder E defines the target latent z0.
3.2
EXTENDING RGB GENERATION WITH OPACITY
Our goal is to endow a pretrained RGB generator with opacity generation without introducing a separate transparency
latent or altering its pretrained RGB decoding function. Our key insight is that opacity can be learned directly in the
original RGB latent space: alpha supervision is propagated back to the generator, so the sampled latent is jointly shaped
by appearance and opacity. Opacity is therefore learned as part of generation rather than estimated post hoc from a
completed RGB image.

3.3
ENDPOINT-AWARE ALPHA LOSS
Alpha determines foreground–background mixing through the complementary weights (a, 1 −a). Our central idea is to
construct both the pixelwise discrepancy and its spatial aggregation from this compositing pair. We use the Fisher–Rao

separate root reductions.

its interior. Its Fisher information is I(a) = 1/[a(1 −a)] for 0 < a < 1. The corresponding normalized Fisher–Rao

EXPERIMENTS
We evaluate whether pretrained RGB generators can acquire RGBA capability through generative training alone. All
ALPHAANYTHING models keep the RGB VAE encoder and decoder frozen and jointly train the transformer LoRA
and alpha head with flow matching and EA Loss, without any RGBA reconstruction training. For text-to-RGBA, we
construct and curate 8,963 RGBA training samples using an image generation model and evaluate ALPHAANYTHING
on FLUX.1-dev (Black Forest Labs, 2024), FLUX.2 Klein Base 9B (Black Forest Labs, 2025), Qwen-Image (Wu et al.,
2025), and LongCat-Image (Meituan LongCat Team et al., 2025). For image-to-RGBA, we use PrismLayersPro (Chen
et al., 2025) with corrected prompt annotations, and evaluate on FLUX.2 Klein Base 9B (Black Forest Labs, 2025) and
Qwen-Image-Edit (Wu et al., 2025).
All ALPHAANYTHING variants are trained for 20K optimizer updates with an effective global batch size of 16, a
learning rate of 10−4 for both the DiT LoRA and alpha branch, and DiT LoRA rank 64. Trainable parameter counts
for each backbone are reported in Appendix B.6; detailed training configurations, evaluation protocols, and metric
definitions are provided in Appendix B.

MAIN RESULTS
We compare ALPHAANYTHING with existing text-to-RGBA and image-to-RGBA methods. We evaluate on two
test sets constructed for this study: RGBA-Text, containing 294 text prompts, and RGBA-Image, containing 735
image-conditioned examples. We also evaluate text-to-RGBA generation on the 402 prompts in AlphaTest (Wang et al.,

Text-to-RGBA generation. We evaluate text-to-RGBA generation on RGBA-Text and AlphaTest using CLIPScore (Hes-

We compare four ALPHAANYTHING backbones with AlphaVAE (based on FLUX.1-dev), LayerDiffuse, OmniAlpha
(based on Qwen-Image-Edit), and Qwen-Image-2.1 (Qwen Team, 2026).
As shown in Tables 1 and 2, ALPHAANYTHING achieves improvements in visual quality and text–image alignment

4.2
PRESERVING RGB DETAILS
On the 295 source images in RGBA-Image, we find that RGBA-trained VAEs exhibit lower RGB reconstruction
fidelity than their corresponding pretrained backbone VAEs. Compared with the original Qwen-Image-Edit RGB VAE,
OmniAlpha’s RGBA VAE exhibits visible deviations in fine details, as illustrated in Figure 5. Quantitatively, LPIPS
increases from 0.03807 to 0.05197 and ∆E00 from 1.917 to 2.078. Figure 7 further shows that ALPHAANYTHING
better preserves RGB details during RGBA generation.
Figure 6 complements these decoder comparisons with a qualitative generation example using FLUX.1-dev. The original
RGB model and ALPHAANYTHING both render a volumetric nut with clear fabric texture and metallic highlights,
while AlphaVAE produces a flatter shape. This example illustrates that ALPHAANYTHING can retain fine material and
structural details exhibited by the original RGB backbone during RGBA generation.

CONCLUSIONS, LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE WORK
ALPHAANYTHING extends pretrained RGB generators to RGBA through generative training alone, without RGBA
reconstruction pretraining or a separate alpha latent. Alpha supervision guides RGBA generation in the original RGB
latent space, while unidirectional RGB-to-alpha feature sharing preserves the original RGB decoding process for
any fixed latent. EA Loss jointly handles exact opacity endpoints, soft transparency, and localized errors within a
single objective, outperforming standard alpha L1 loss in our experiments. Experiments across text-to-RGBA and
image-to-RGBA tasks and multiple pretrained backbones show competitive RGBA performance, with improvements in
aesthetic quality and text-image alignment.
High-quality RGBA data, widely accepted evaluation benchmarks and metrics are still scarce, which limits how broadly
we can evaluate the method. Broader datasets and more comprehensive metrics that jointly assess appearance, opacity,
and compositing quality are needed to further characterize data efficiency, generation quality, and generalization across
RGBA generation tasks.

EXPERIMENTAL PROTOCOL
This appendix specifies the implementation, training, evaluation, and ablation settings used in Section 4. All variants
use the objective in (12), with the stated substitution for the alpha-L1 ablation. Additional numerical results and visual
comparisons are reported in Appendices C and D.
B.1
IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS
RGB targets and native backbone interfaces.
The color-padding operation in Section 3.1 follows the multiscale
convention of LayerDiffuse (Zhang & Agrawala, 2024). We form a pyramid of premultiplied color Ip = aIc and alpha,
initialize the coarsest color from an alpha-weighted mean, and propagate color from coarse to fine. Each scale adds its
premultiplied color to the upsampled coarse color multiplied by remaining transparency. The procedure supplies a dense
three-channel training target without relying on arbitrary stored RGB values in fully transparent regions. The frozen
encoder uses its posterior mode for FLUX and Qwen, while LongCat follows its native training convention of posterior
sampling. Each backbone retains its native convolution dimensionality, feature widths, latent scaling, and token packing.
Conditioning tokens remain clean when required by its image-conditioning interface.

Alpha-branch configuration.
The five alpha stages use widths (256, 256, 192, 128, 96) from the bottleneck toward
the RGB output, with two residual blocks per stage. All RGB-to-alpha projections use rank rℓ= 64 and scaling
parameter ηℓ= 64. The learned stage gates are initialized to (1, 0.75, 0.5, 0.25, 0.25), and injection-count normalization

hidden channels. Dropout is zero throughout the alpha branch.

Initialization.
Alpha residual outputs and the bottleneck cross-attention output projection are zero initialized. Spatial
injection output projections and the final score convolution use random initialization with standard deviation 10−4. This
starts residual refinement from the identity while providing trainable feature and score paths.
B.2
TRAINING AND INFERENCE SETTINGS
Text-to-RGBA evaluation uses RGBA-Text, AIM-500 (Li et al., 2021), and AlphaTest from AlphaVAE (Wang et al.,
2025b). For AIM-500, we use the text prompts released with AlphaVAE. Image-to-RGBA evaluation uses RGBA-Image.
Prompt lists, source groups, aspect-ratio policy, conditioning protocol, and filtering rules are fixed within each backbone.
The text-to-RGBA comparison uses FLUX.1-dev, FLUX.2 Klein Base 9B, Qwen-Image-2512, and LongCat-Image; the
image-to-RGBA comparison uses FLUX.2 Klein Base 9B and Qwen-Image-Edit-2511. Each backbone keeps its native
conditioning interface, latent layout, encoding, scaling, token packing, and sampler.
Training data and preprocessing.
Text-to-RGBA training uses 8,963 curated RGBA assets generated using an image
generation model. Image-to-RGBA training uses ∼180K source-image-target-layer pairs from PrismLayersPro (Chen
et al., 2025), with corrected English extraction instructions. Images are processed at a nominal resolution of 1024 using
aspect-ratio buckets; the training examples in these runs occupy the 1024 × 1024 bucket. Center cropping and random
horizontal flipping are disabled. Image-conditioned source and target pairs receive aligned spatial preprocessing. The
RGB target uses color padding, while the original target alpha supplies opacity supervision.
Optimization.
All models are trained for 20,000 optimizer updates with a per-device batch size of one and one
gradient-accumulation step. The main experiments use 16 GPUs, giving an effective global batch size of 16. We
use AdamW (Loshchilov & Hutter, 2019) with learning rate 10−4 for both the transformer LoRA and alpha branch,
(β1, β2) = (0.9, 0.999), weight decay 0.01, and ϵ = 10−8. The learning rate follows cosine decay after 100 warmup
updates, and the global gradient norm is clipped at 1.0. Training uses bfloat16 mixed precision and transformer activation
checkpointing. The original transformer, RGB VAE, and text-encoder parameters remain frozen. The flow and alpha
terms both have coefficient 1. Alpha supervision uses the time weight in (12), with reference noise level 0.5 and
denominator stabilizer 10−4; the one-step clean-latent estimate is clipped to [−20, 20] before decoding.
Transformer LoRA modules.
All transformer LoRA modules use rank 64, scaling parameter 128, Gaussian initial-
ization, and zero dropout. Qwen and LongCat apply LoRA to the attention query, key, value, and output projections.
FLUX.1 additionally applies LoRA to the context-stream attention projections and the input and output feed-forward
projections in both image and context streams. FLUX.2 applies LoRA to the attention projections together with the
fused query/key/value–MLP input projection and output projection of its single-stream blocks.
Conditioning and noise sampling.
For text-to-RGBA, explicit and implicit caption variants are selected with equal
probability; within either form, the short caption is selected with probability 0.2. Qwen, FLUX.2, and LongCat drop
text conditioning with probability 0.1; image-conditioned training retains the reference image when its text is dropped.
FLUX.1 uses its native guidance embedding with training guidance 1.0. Qwen and FLUX.2 uniformly sample valid
entries of a 1,000-step flow schedule with shift 3, restricting scheduler timesteps to [1, 999]. FLUX.1 samples from its
native shifted schedule over the same timestep range. LongCat samples σ = sigmoid(ξ) with ξ ∼N(0, 1) and then
applies the backbone’s resolution-dependent time shift. All backbones use the linear flow path and velocity target in (10).
Controlled comparisons keep the sampler, guidance settings, inference seed, conditioning inputs, and sampling-step
count fixed.
Inference settings.
The text-to-RGBA comparisons use the corresponding 20,000-step ALPHAANYTHING check-
points. FLUX.1 uses 28 sampling steps and guidance 3.5; FLUX.2, Qwen, and LongCat use 50 steps and guidance 4.
The baselines use their recorded inference settings: 25 steps and guidance 7 for LayerDiffuse with SDXL (Podell et al.,
2024), 28 steps and guidance 3.5 for AlphaVAE, 50 steps and guidance 4 for OmniAlpha, and 40 steps and guidance 1.0

RGBA-Text.
RGBA-Text contains 294 English prompts covering nine categories, with the distribution in Table 7. We
follow the training set’s category taxonomy while composing new combinations of subjects, materials, colors, poses,
and visual styles. The set spans lettering and graphic assets, natural subjects with detailed boundaries, visual effects,
and transparent or translucent materials. Prompts are selected for content coverage, clarity, and distinctiveness, without
imposing equal category sizes or fixed description lengths. They range from 10 to 95 words, with a median of 53 words;
prompts involving visible lettering use English text.
We check both the main prompts and their short summaries against all nonempty training caption variants. The curation
combines normalized exact-text matching, shared 12-word sequence checks, and manual review of TF–IDF nearest
neighbors. Descriptions that too closely resemble training captions are revised. The final set contains no normalized
exact duplicates within the test prompts or against the training text, and no shared contiguous 12-word sequences with
training captions. RGBA-Text contains prompts without paired reference images, so we report reference-free scores on
this set.

RGBA-Image source images.
RGBA-Image contains 735 image-conditioned examples from 295 source images.
Each example includes a source image, a target RGBA layer, an English layer description, and an extraction instruction.
The source images come from two pools: selected GenPoster100K posters and images synthesized using an image
generation and editing model. Table 8 reports the final distribution by source and input-image category; multiple target
layers can share one source image.
For the GenPoster100K pool (Wang et al., 2025a), we first filter the released metadata for adequate resolution, valid
layouts, and usable text. Candidates must have a short side of at least 600 pixels, at least 0.7 megapixels, an aspect ratio
between 0.45 and 2.20, and 2–24 annotated layout elements. We reject candidates with malformed layouts, CJK text,
or template placeholders, and retain at most one candidate per original design file. Remaining candidates are ranked
using layout metadata, CLIP-based scores (Radford et al., 2021), LAION aesthetic scores, MUSIQ (Ke et al., 2021), and
image-quality statistics, followed by visual near-duplicate filtering and OCR screening. Full-resolution OCR removes
remaining CJK text and placeholder copy, leaving 143 posters for layer extraction.
For the synthetic-image pool, we generate 200 source images with the image generation and editing model: 100 stylized
design scenes and 100 photorealistic scenes. Scene prompts vary subject combinations, human presence, spatial layout,
camera viewpoint, palette, lighting, and rendering style. The final retained inputs comprise 99 stylized scenes and 86
photorealistic scenes, contributing 261 and 217 target layers, respectively.
Layer construction, annotation, and manual review.
We use the same image generation and editing model to
construct RGBA reference layers for both source pools. Posters are decomposed into at most six layers. For the
generated scenes, a vision-language model first identifies one to three visually unambiguous semantic targets per image,
and the image model extracts these targets jointly as separate layers. Reference layers are placed on the source image’s
canvas, preserving its dimensions and the targets’ spatial alignment. These are generated reference layers that undergo
manual filtering.
We use the vision-language model to annotate or re-annotate English descriptions and extraction instructions, followed
by manual review against the source images and target RGBA layers. Descriptions capture the target’s visible appearance
and spatial relationships; instructions identify the same target and its intended semantic grouping. Wording and length
vary with target complexity. Review corrects ambiguous targets, mismatched descriptions, and incomplete grouping
18


instructions, including text panels, frames, and attached objects when they belong to the accepted layer. Each candidate
layer is inspected alongside its source image and alpha matte for alpha accuracy, alignment with the description and
extraction instruction, and color fidelity. Layers with inaccurate opacity, prompt mismatches, or visible color deviations
are rejected. The final evaluation set contains 257 poster layers from 110 posters and 478 generated-scene layers from
185 images, for a total of 735 layers from 295 source images. We use these manually screened reference layers for
evaluation, although generative extraction does not guarantee exact pixelwise reconstruction of the source content.

B.4.3
RGB RECONSTRUCTION EVALUATION
We compare the RGBA VAEs of OmniAlpha and AlphaVAE with their original Qwen-Image-Edit-2509 and FLUX.1-dev
RGB VAEs, respectively. The evaluation uses all 295 distinct source images in RGBA-Image, counting each input once
regardless of how many target layers it has. Inputs are resized with LANCZOS to a maximum side length of 1024 pixels
while preserving aspect ratios, without upscaling. All models receive the same inputs, edge-padded to multiples of 64;
the padding is removed before scoring. We use deterministic float32 encode–decode with the posterior mode and supply
fully opaque alpha to the RGBA encoders. Decoded RGB values are clipped to [0, 1] and evaluated directly, discarding
the reconstructed alpha without compositing.
We evaluate RGB reconstruction using LPIPS (Zhang et al., 2018) and CIEDE2000 color difference, ∆E00 (Sharma
et al., 2005). LPIPS uses AlexNet v0.1 on floating-point reconstructions before PNG quantization, at the full evaluation
resolution. Both metrics are computed over complete images and averaged equally across inputs. The enlarged regions
in Figure 5 illustrate local details within these full-image reconstructions. Results are reported in Appendix C.2.
Computation of ∆E00.
