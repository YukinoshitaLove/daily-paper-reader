Abstract 

Generative models for microscopy can already produce realistic whole-image fields of view, but offer no principled way to control how each individual cell looks. We ad-dress this gap by recasting microscopy generation at the single-cell level and designing the input controls and the model’s internal representation jointly, so that both are orga-nized along the same biologically meaningful directions. On the input side, we aggregate a long list of standard cell measurements into a small set of semantic axes (size, elon-gation, brightness, texture, etc.) that are each named, measurable, and traversable. On the model side, we introduce BioVAE, an encoder-decoder whose latent space is ex-plicitly aligned with the same set of measurements. A key finding is that the benefit of biologically structuring the latent emerges only when conditioning is also moved to the semantic axes, showing that the two ends must be designed jointly. Experiments on LIVECell show that semantic-axis conditioning substantially improves generative fi-delity over high-dimensional conditioning, a blind study with biologists confirms the realism of generated cells, and per-axis traversals produce stable and interpretable phe-notypic changes.

# 1 Introduction 

Microscopy images are pictures of a set of individual cells captured under a microscope, and the way each cell looks; its size, shape, brightness, and internal texture patterns, is defined as the phenotype. Phenotypic differences across cells are the basis of almost every quantitative question in cell biology, from how cells respond to a drug to how diseased cells differ from healthy ones [20, 25]. Recent generative models can already generate realistic microscopy images [4, 5, 16, 17, 19, 29], but they produce the entire field-of-view in one shot rather than building it cell by cell (Fig. 1 (a)). As a result, current models do not provide direct control over interpretable phenotype attributes such as cell size or brightness. While cellular phenotype is fundamentally defined at the level of individual cells, current generative models typically operate at the level of entire microscopy scenes. These limitations motivate studying generation at the level of individual cells. Fine-grained control over cellular phenotype naturally suggests formulating generation at the level of individual cells. The remaining challenge is how to specify desired cell phenotypes in a form that is both biologically meaningful and amenable to reliable generation. Several existing methods can already generate realistic single-cell images [6, 14, 15, 28], but fine-grained control over phenotype remains challenging. Some methods condition gen-eration on binary shape masks and synthesize the corresponding cell appearance (Fig. 1 (b)); the user can specify the cell outline but has no control over its brightness or texture. Other methods take a long list of raw features measuring cell properties as input, but many of them describe overlapping aspects of the same cell, and many are not faithfully represented in the generated image. In both cases, precise control over individual phenotype attributes re-mains limited, making it difficult to manipulate generated cells along isolated biologically meaningful directions such as size or brightness. We address this challenge through a joint design of the conditioning space and latent rep-resentation, both organized around the same biologically meaningful phenotype directions (Fig. 1 (c)). On the input side, instead of asking the user for a shape mask or a long list of raw features, we provide a small set of intuitive controls, one for each interpretable aspect of how a cell looks, such as size, elongation, brightness, and texture. We call these the semantic axes. On the output side, the model internally compresses each cell image into a compact code before generating it, and we train this compression so that the code itself is organized TAKEZAKI ET AL.: RETHINKING MICROSCOPY GENERATION 3

along the same set of biologically meaningful directions. We call this component BioVAE. Aligning the conditioning variables and latent representation around the same phenotype di-rections promotes stable and predictable control over generated cells. To our knowledge, this joint design has not been studied for single-cell image generation. Our contribution is this joint design rather than single-cell synthesis itself, which has been demonstrated in prior work [14, 28]. We also see single-cell and whole-image gener-ation as complementary: whole-image models capture how cells are arranged together in a microscope picture, while controllable single-cell generation provides a fine-grained handle on how each individual cell looks. Together they form a more complete toolkit for studying cellular phenotype with generative models. In this work, we propose a controllable single-cell generation framework whose condi-tioning variables and latent representation are jointly organized around biologically mean-ingful phenotype directions (Fig. 2 (a)). We first extract standard measurements of cellular appearance [1] and aggregate them into a compact set of semantic axes that serve as in-terpretable conditioning variables (Fig. 2 (b)). To align generation with these controls, we introduce BioVAE, an encoder-decoder whose latent representation is explicitly structured around the same phenotype measurements (Fig. 2 (c)). A latent diffusion model [23] is then trained within this representation space and conditioned on the semantic axes, enabling controllable generation of individual cells (Fig. 2 (d)). Experiments on the LIVECell [3] dataset demonstrate that semantic-axis conditioning provides more effective control than conditioning on the raw set of measurements while maintaining high generation quality. A blind study with biologists further supports the re-alism of the generated cells, and traversal experiments show that individual semantic axes correspond to stable and interpretable phenotypic variations. Our contributions are summarized as follows: • Problem framing. We articulate controllable single-cell generation as a complemen-tary direction to whole-image microscopy generation, and identify the joint design of the user-facing controls and the model’s internal representation as the central method-ological question at this scale. • BioVAE. We propose an encoder-decoder whose compressed representation is explic-itly aligned with biological measurements of how cells look, via an auxiliary regres-sion objective and an anti-collapse entropy term. • Semantic-axis conditioning. We introduce a compact set of semantic axes, where each axis is named (corresponds to a recognizable phenotype), measurable (can be re-evaluated from the generated image), and traversable (can be varied independently), and show that this works better than conditioning on the raw list of measurements. • Empirical validation. On LIVECell, we report generation quality, a blind study with biologists, and per-axis controllability via traversal experiments.

2.2 Single-Cell Modeling and Synthesis 

A complementary line of work models individual cells, but each leaves a controllability gap that our framework targets. Early GAN-based methods such as CytoGAN [6] generate single-cell images without any conditioning, and so offer no control over how the generated cell looks. CP2Image [14] takes a different approach: the user provides a long vector of standard measurements of cell appearance (the CellProfiler [26] representation, on the order of several hundred numbers), and the model generates a matching cell image. In principle this allows the user to specify a target appearance, but in practice the measurements are highly correlated, and [14] reports that fewer than a third of them are faithfully reflected in the generated image. As a result, the user cannot reliably make the generated cell, for example, larger or brighter on demand. Cascaded diffusion for cell synthesis [28] takes yet another route: it samples a binary cell-shape mask and then fills it in with a fine-tuned Stable Diffusion model. The mask sampler is unconditional, so the user cannot specify the shape, and once a mask is given everything else (brightness, texture, internal structure) is left to the diffusion model; the pipeline is designed for generating training data for cell segmentation rather than for controlling phenotype. Unsupervised single-cell representation TAKEZAKI ET AL.: RETHINKING MICROSCOPY GENERATION 5

learning [15] learns compact representations of cell images, but does not generate. In all of these methods, the input the user can manipulate and the model’s internal representation are designed independently, with no shared set of interpretable directions tied to how cells look. We design both together so that a small set of intuitive controls (size, elongation, brightness, texture) drives both ends, making per-cell phenotype controllable in a stable and interpretable way. 

2.3 Controllable and Conditional Diffusion 

Conditional diffusion models steer generation through mechanisms such as cross-attention conditioning [23], feature-wise modulation (FiLM) [21], and classifier-free guidance [12]. These mechanisms determine how a conditioning signal is injected into the generator, but they do not address what that signal should be or how it should relate to the model’s internal representation. In the single-cell setting, the natural conditioning vocabulary is a long list of correlated handcrafted measurements, and we find empirically that feeding this redundant signal directly into a standard FiLM-conditioned diffusion model leads to unstable genera-tion and unreliable per-axis control (Sec. 4.4). Our semantic-axis design, together with the BioVAE latent it is aligned with, can be viewed as a domain-specific co-design of what to condition on and what the latent represents, which lets an otherwise standard conditional diffusion model behave stably and interpretably. 

2.4 Disentangled and Structured Latent Spaces 

A long line of work asks how a generative model can be made interpretable, in the sense that each direction in its latent space corresponds to one clean, isolated change in the output (for example, only the size of an object, or only its color). Some methods train the model so that different latent dimensions are statistically independent of one another [11]. Others take an already trained generator and search inside its latent space to find directions that happen to correspond to meaningful changes [8]. In both cases, the criterion for a good direction is statistical independence: moving along one direction should not affect the others. Our setting differs in two respects. First, our directions are not discovered by the model: each one is defined in advance from a standard measurement of how cells look (size, elonga-tion, brightness, texture), so the directions are interpretable by design. Second, our directions are intentionally not statistically independent. They are derived from groups of measure-ments that naturally overlap in real cells; for example, cell area, perimeter, and equivalent diameter all describe cell size and tend to move together. Forcing such directions to be in-dependent would erase patterns that actually exist in real cells. We therefore aim for each direction to produce a change that matches how real cells vary, rather than a strictly isolated one (Sec. 4.6). This positions our work between classical handcrafted descriptors of cell ap-pearance and unsupervised disentanglement: we prioritize matching real cell variation over strict statistical independence. 

2.5 Biological Descriptors in Computational Cell Biology 

Researchers studying cells have a long tradition of extracting numbers that summarize how each cell looks in a microscopy image, such as its size, shape, brightness, and the pattern of its internal texture. CellProfiler [26] is a widely used software that automatically computes hundreds of such measurements from a cell image, and the Cell Painting protocol [1] is a 6 TAKEZAKI ET AL.: RETHINKING MICROSCOPY GENERATION 

standardized way of staining cells with fluorescent dyes so that these measurements consis-tently capture the same set of cellular features across experiments. Together they are rou-tinely used in large-scale experiments that test many drugs or genetic conditions on cells at once. Subsequent work has shown that, although these measurements are high-dimensional, they can be summarized by a small number of meaningful patterns that capture the main ways in which cells differ from one another [2]. We adopt these measurements not as the end product of an analysis, but as supervision and conditioning signals for image generation, connecting this established way of describing cells quantitatively with modern generative models. 

# 3 Controllable Single-Cell Latent Diffusion Framework 

Fig. 2 (a) illustrates the core idea of our framework: we generate individual cell images and design the input controls and the internal representation together so that both are organized along the same set of biologically meaningful directions. The framework has two compo-nents. First, on the input side, we take the long list of measurements that describes how each cell looks and aggregate them into a small set of intuitive semantic axes (size, elonga-tion, brightness, texture, etc.); these axes are then used to condition a latent diffusion model (Fig. 2 (b,d)). Second, on the model side, we train an encoder-decoder, BioVAE, whose la-tent representation is explicitly aligned with the same set of measurements, so that the latent is organized along the same directions the user controls (Fig. 2 (c)). Because both ends share a common biological vocabulary, moving any control produces a stable and interpretable change in the generated cell. 

3.1 Single-Cell Extraction and Feature Aggregation 

We isolate individual cells from full microscopy images using the LIVECell [3] dataset and its segmentation masks. For each cell, we set the background to black, discard cells touching the image edge, and obtain a 64 × 64 single-cell crop. For each crop we then compute a long vector of standard measurements that summarize how the cell looks ( v ∈ R61 ), and aggregate these into a small set of intuitive semantic axes ( s ∈ R15 ) that we will use to control generation. 

Descriptor extraction. For each cell crop, we compute 61 standard measurements inside the segmentation mask. Following established practice in computational cell biology [1, 26], these measurements are organized into four categories that describe complementary aspects of how a cell looks: (i) Morphology (20): area, perimeter, equivalent diameter, major/minor axis length, aspect ratio, eccentricity, solidity, circularity, convex area, extent, and 9 Zernike moments (which describe the overall shape using a standard set of basis functions). (ii) Pixel intensity (10): mean, std., min, max, sum, median, and 5th/25th/75th/95th percentiles of intensities inside the mask. (iii) Texture (25): 13 Haralick features (which summarize the spatial pattern of intensities), a 10-bin LBP histogram (which captures local texture pat-terns), and gradient mean/std. (iv) Spatial distribution (6): a 6-bin radial intensity profile, which describes how brightness changes from the cell center outwards. Intensity-related measurements describe the cell body only, since the background is masked out. Definitions and exact formulas are provided in Supplementary Sec. A. Let v ∈ R61 denote the resulting measurement vector. TAKEZAKI ET AL.: RETHINKING MICROSCOPY GENERATION 7

From descriptors to semantic axes. Our goal here is not simply to reduce the 61 mea-surements to fewer numbers. Generic dimensionality reduction techniques such as PCA or an autoencoder would also produce a compact code, but the resulting axes would not cor-respond to anything that a biologist can name, manipulate, or measure independently, and so they could not serve as a control interface for phenotype. We instead manually design a small set of axes that satisfy three properties needed for interpretable control. First, each axis corresponds to a named phenotype that a biologist would recognize (e.g., cell size, elonga-tion, brightness, granularity). Second, each axis is measurable from a generated image using the same pipeline that defines it, so per-axis control can be verified quantitatively after gen-eration. Third, each axis supports one-axis traversal : the user can vary a single axis while keeping the others fixed and observe a focused change in the generated cell. These properties are what make the axes a control interface, not just a compressed representation. Concretely, we aggregate the 61 measurements into 15 semantic axes s ∈ R15 , each sum-marizing one coherent aspect of cell appearance: (i) Morphology (6 axes): cell size, elonga-tion, shape compactness, boundary irregularity, global shape complexity, and symmetry. (ii) Pixel intensity (3 axes): overall signal level, intensity contrast, and intensity skewness. (iii) Texture (4 axes): heterogeneity, smoothness, granularity, and edge strength. (iv) Spatial distribution (2 axes): central enrichment and radial uniformity. Each axis is computed by a simple interpretable function of the corresponding measurements (e.g., means of z-scored measurements and L1 norms of Zernike moments) and normalized using training-set statis-tics. The complete mapping appears in Supplementary Sec. B. The 15 axes remain partially correlated because real cells naturally vary in correlated ways (Supplementary Sec. B.5); we do not orthogonalize them, since that would discard real patterns in cell appearance. What matters is that each axis is named, measurable, and traversable, so that moving along any axis corresponds to a biologically meaningful change in the generated cell.

The second half of the co-design is the latent representation that the diffusion model op-erates on. We build upon a standard VAE, which consists of an encoder E and a decoder 

D: given an input image x, the encoder compresses it into a latent code z = E(x), and the decoder reconstructs it as ˜ x = D(z). Modern VAE architectures are trained by minimizing

A standard VAE has no reason to organize its latent space along any particular biological direction: it only knows how to reconstruct images. We therefore introduce BioVAE, which adds a simple auxiliary objective that forces the latent to encode the same measurements that define our user-facing controls. Concretely, a shallow neural network F takes the latent 

z and predicts the full measurement vector v ∈ Rd (d = 61), and we minimize the mean squared error LBio = ||F (z) − v|| 2/d. 3.3 Biological Conditioning for Latent Diffusion Models 

We now plug the BioVAE encoder into a Latent Diffusion Model (LDM) [23] to generate new single-cell images. The pre-trained BioVAE acts as a fixed encoder, projecting each image into a compact latent z. We compare two choices of conditioning signal: the full 61-dimensional measurement vector v, and the 15 aggregated semantic axes s. # 4 Experiments 

We evaluated our proposed single-cell generation pipeline on the LIVECell dataset [3] through three main experiments. First, we ablate the loss components of BioVAE to assess their im-pact on image reconstruction quality and on how well the latent space is organized along bi-ological measurements. Second, we evaluate the generative quality of the single-cell LDM, comparing conditioning on the raw 61-dimensional measurements against conditioning on the 15 semantic axes, and complement this with a blind study in which biologists assess the realism of generated cells. Finally, we evaluate per-axis controllability through traversal experiments, in which we vary a single semantic axis at a time and measure whether the generated cells change in the expected direction. 

4.1 Experimental Setup 

Dataset: We isolated individual cells using their respective annotation masks, setting back-grounds to black and removing edge fragments. At the instance level, the LIVECell dataset yields 64 × 64 grayscale crops partitioned into 928k training, 166k validation, and 411k test images. TAKEZAKI ET AL.: RETHINKING MICROSCOPY GENERATION 9

Evaluation Metrics: To evaluate reconstruction fidelity , we used MSE, PSNR, SSIM, and rFID. MSE and PSNR assessed pixel-level accuracy, SSIM evaluated structural consistency, and rFID (reconstruction FID [10]) captured perceptual realism using deep features. To assess latent space quality , we measured k-NN neighborhood preservation and linear SVM classification accuracy for cell types. For the generative performance of our diffusion model, we implemented domain-specialized FID [10], MMD [7], Precision, and Recall [24] for a quantitative measurement. 

BioVAE: Our foundational encoder was based on the Stable Diffusion VAE [22, 23, 27] architecture with a downscale factor of 8 and a latent shape of 8 × 8 × 4. Training used the Adam optimizer with learning rate of 1 .0 × 10 −4 for 500k iterations with a batch size of 64. Standard loss weights were set to λRecon = 1, λKL = 10 −6, and λGAN = 1. BioVAE additionally applied λBio = 1 and λEntropy = 1. 

Single-Cell Diffusion: We trained a conditional LDM for 500K iterations using a batch size of 64 and the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 1 × 10 −4. Biological conditioning (measurements v or semantic axes s) was integrated via FiLM [21]. At training, we used 1000 steps for the linear noise scheduler, and we synthesized synthetic single-cells using a DDPM [13] sampler. 

4.2 Implementation Details 

Boundary handling. Cells were cropped using their segmentation masks with the back-ground set to zero, then padded to 64 × 64. To prevent the model from latching onto sharp mask boundaries as a shortcut, we applied a 2-pixel Gaussian feathering along the mask edge during training. We did not observe artifacts at the boundary in generated samples (Fig. 3). 

Domain-specialized image quality evaluation. We used a DINOv2 ViT-S/14 backbone fine-tuned on LIVECell single-cell crops for 50 epochs with a self-supervised iBOT objec-tive, following the default setup adapted to low-resolution images [18]. The quantitative metrics were computed in the resulting 384-d embedding space using 10k real and 10k gen-erated cells per cell line, stratified.

4.4 Generated Quality 

We evaluated four single-cell LDMs combining BioVAE or baseline VAE encoders with the full high-dimensional morphodynamic descriptor vector v or semantic axes s, measuring image quality metrics (FID [10], MMD [7], Precision, and Recall [24]) using a DINOv2 model fine-tuned on single-cell data [18], as well as Morph. MSE on the LIVECell validation split (Table 3). Semantic axes consistently outperform morphodynamic descriptors, approximately halv-ing FID (0.93–0.97 vs 2.04–2.12), with BioVAE + semantic axes achieving the best per-formance (FID 0.93, MSE 0.925). Interestingly, while BioVAE underperforms the baseline VAE when conditioned on the highly correlated 61-dimensional vector, it demonstrates supe-rior fidelity when paired with the 15-dimensional semantic axes. This suggests that because BioVAE’s latent space already strongly encodes phenotypic structure, conditioning directly on the redundant 61-dimensional vector causes information interference. The compact, bi-ologically meaningful 15-dimensional semantic axes alleviate this redundancy, creating a favorable synergy with the biologically regularized latent space. 4.5 Blind Study with Biologists 

A blind study with six biologists evaluated 960 cell instances across eight cell types. Partic-ipants classified each cell as real or synthetic, predicted its cell type, and rated task difficulty on a 0–4 scale. Overall discrimination accuracy was 50.9% (AUC = 0.509), which was not significantly different from random guessing (binomial p = 0.58). As shown in Fig. 4 (a), synthetic samples were frequently perceived as real: only 20.2% of synthetic cells were cor-rectly identified as synthetic, whereas 81.7% of real cells were correctly classified as real. To quantify perceived realism at the sample level, we defined a realism score as the fraction of observers classifying a cell as real. Real and synthetic cells exhibited comparable realism score distributions, with no significant difference between groups (Mann–Whitney p = 0.41; Fig. 4 (b)). Observers generally rated the task as difficult (mean difficulty of 3.17/4), and their ratings were not significantly correlated with discrimination accuracy ( r = 0.14, p = 0.34). Together, these results indicate that generated single-cell images are visually realistic and preserve morphology consistent with the target phenotypes. 4.6 Controllability via Axis Traversal 

Beyond image quality, we explored the controllability properties of the BioVAE + semantic axes latent diffusion model. Unlike the biological feature representation, the semantic axes provide a compact and interpretable phenotype space that enables controllability analysis through biologically meaningful semantic traversals. For this experiment, we sampled 100 representative validation cells, referred to as anchors, and generated semantic traversals by modifying one semantic axis at a time while keeping the remaining axes fixed. The seman-tic axes were designed to prioritize biological interpretability rather than orthogonal latent decomposition; therefore, the controllability analysis should be interpreted as biologically consistent semantic modulation within a correlated phenotype manifold rather than fully disentangled control. To quantitatively evaluate semantic controllability, we measured three complementary properties: target controllability, control gain, and selectivity. Target controllability was estimated using the Spearman correlation between requested and measured semantic values. Control gain was estimated using the slope of a linear regression between requested and generated semantic values. Finally, selectivity measures the relative dominance of the target semantic response compared to the average response of the non-target semantic axes. Fig. 5 presents representative semantic traversals from a shared anchor cell. The quanti-tative results are summarized in the Supplementary Sec. C, with additional anchor examples. The traversal results reveal multiple controllability regimes across the semantic space. Cell size, global shape complexity, and symmetry exhibited high target controllability, strong control gain, and moderate selectivity, suggesting that the model can effectively manipulate global morphological structure. Elongation, boundary irregularity, and intensity contrast also presented high target controllability but lower control gain, indicating compressed se-mantic responses relative to the requested traversal range. We attribute this behavior to the model remaining close to the learned biological manifold and preserving biologically plausi-ble phenotype combinations during generation, consistent with the high precision and recall observed in Section 4.4. # 5 Discussion and Limitations 

The unit of generation and its scope. Whole-image and single-cell generation address complementary aspects of microscopy. Whole-image models capture spatial organization and cell-to-cell context, whereas single-cell models provide direct control over the appear-ance of individual cells. Our framework focuses on the latter and therefore does not model interactions between neighboring cells or larger tissue context. In addition, our evaluation is restricted to LIVECell, which contains phase-contrast images from eight cell lines. Extend-ing the framework to fluorescence, brightfield, 3D imaging, and multi-cellular scenes is an important direction for future work. 

Why the input and latent representation should be designed together. A central finding is that biologically structuring the latent representation alone is insufficient. BioVAE does 14 TAKEZAKI ET AL.: RETHINKING MICROSCOPY GENERATION 

not improve generation when paired with the full 61-dimensional measurement vector; its benefit emerges when conditioning is also organized through the 15 semantic axes (Sec. 4.4). This suggests that controllability is a property of the overall pipeline rather than of a single component. More broadly, our results indicate that simple biological priors can improve not only interpretability but also representation structure and conditional generation quality (Tables 2–3). 

Scope of the semantic axes. The semantic axes provide a compact set of named, measur-able, and traversable controls over cell appearance, but they are not a complete description of cellular phenotype. Fine-grained organelle structure, sub-cellular spatial patterns, and biological state transitions are not explicitly represented. Moreover, the axes are manually constructed from standard measurements, so their coverage depends on what those measure-ments capture. Data-driven extensions using richer assays such as Cell Painting may provide additional biologically meaningful control directions. 

External comparison. Our evaluation relies primarily on controlled internal ablations rather than direct comparison with external single-cell generators, because existing methods do not provide compatible single-cell-level metrics, feature extractors, or generation protocols on LIVECell. Establishing a common benchmark for controllable single-cell generation therefore remains an important community-level direction. 

# 6 Conclusion 

We presented a framework for microscopy generation that operates at the level of individual cells and designs the user-facing controls and the model’s internal representation together, so that both are organized along the same set of biologically meaningful directions. On the input side, we aggregate standard cell measurements into a small set of semantic axes that are each named, measurable, and traversable. On the model side, BioVAE aligns its latent space with the same measurements. On LIVECell, this co-design substantially improves genera-tion quality over conditioning on raw high-dimensional measurements, produces single cells that biologists cannot reliably distinguish from real ones in a blind study, and supports sta-ble per-axis control verified through traversal experiments. We see controllable single-cell generation as a complementary building block alongside whole-image approaches, one that connects classical quantitative cell biology to modern generative modeling and opens a re-search direction toward biologically grounded, controllable microscopy synthesis.
