## ABSTRACT 

Energy-Based Models (EBMs) are a flexible class of generative models that de-couple probabilistic modeling from the generation process. This decoupling di-rectly enables compositional generation and inference-time scaling, where sam-ple quality can be refined simply by increasing sampling steps. However, severe challenges in high-dimensional sampling and training instabilities with deep ar-chitectures have restricted EBMs to low-dimensional regimes. To resolve these bottlenecks, we introduce the Transformer Energy-Based Model (TEM), a scal-able transformer energy function operating entirely within a latent space. On top of this backbone, we extract energies at multiple layers and sum them, compos-ing complementary constraints from local texture to global semantics. Further-more, two components make this high-quality at scale: Positive Representation Alignment (PRA), which supervises TEM’s intermediate representations with a vision foundation model, and advanced sampling strategies that improve sample quality at inference. TEM-XL achieves an FID-50K of 3.82 on class-conditional ImageNet 256 ×256 without classifier-free guidance, showing that a contrastive EBM can generate high-fidelity images. On ImageNet 512 ×512, TEM achieves an FID-50K of 8.24, demonstrating that the approach scales to higher resolution. We further validate the core promises of EBMs: compositional generation, zero-shot inpainting, and out-of-distribution detection. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Energy-Based Models (EBMs) [9, 23] parameterize probability distributions via an energy function that assigns lower energies to more probable samples and higher energies to less probable sam-ples. EBMs provide a flexible and mathematically elegant framework that fundamentally separates explicit generation from probabilistic modeling. Unlike Diffusion [18, 38] and Flow [24] models, which are mathematically constrained to pre-defined generative trajectories governed by carefully designed timestep schedules, EBMs define the data distribution solely through an energy function, entirely decoupling the model from the generative process. This decoupling of the model from the generative process yields profound advantages. First, EBMs decouple learning from inference, allowing the sampling procedure to be chosen independently of model training. This enables flex-ible test-time compute scaling by increasing the MCMC steps, progressively refining the samples through a deeper exploration of the learned energy landscape. Second, this flexibility enables the composition of different distributions together [9, 11], which can enable zero-shot generation and generalization capabilities. However, despite their theoretical advantages and compositional properties, EBMs have struggled with widespread adoption due to several critical unresolved challenges. First, EBMs face severe vulnerability to the curse of dimensionality: MCMC sampling suffers from poor mixing in high-dimensional spaces, leading to slow convergence and mode collapse. Second, EBMs scale poorly with model capacity. As network depth increases, conventional architectures exhibit severe training instability, making robust optimization exceedingly difficult [9, 10]. ## 3 SCALABLE TRANSFORMER ENERGY -B ASED MODELS 

3.1 TRANSFORMER ENERGY -B ASED MODELS ARCHITECTURE 

TEM Architecture. We parameterize the energy function Eθ (x, c ) with a Vision Transformer (ViT) [8] backbone adapted for large-scale energy-based modeling. Given an input x, we tokenize it into spatial patch embeddings, which are processed through a stack of transformer blocks. The final patch tokens are aggregated via global average pooling and passed through a small MLP head to produce a scalar energy Eθ (x, c ).Our architecture adopts several modern design choices for stability and expressivity. We encode spatial structure with rotary positional embeddings [39], and apply query-key normalization [15] to stabilize attention logits at scale. Feed-forward layers use SwiGLU activations [36], and the condi-tioning signal is projected into feature-wise affine parameters in an AdaLN-style formulation [33] built on the same RMSNorm variant. Importantly, this design demonstrates stable and effective energy-based modeling at scale without requiring EBM specific components such as spectral normalization commonly used in prior energy-based models [9]. 

Multi-layer Energy Extraction. Energy-based models are naturally suited to composition, since combining distributions amounts to addition in energy space. Du et al. [10] demonstrate this by composing several separately trained models, each capturing a distinct concept. Composition of this kind has so far required multiple models. We show that it can instead be obtained within one, extracting energies through multiple heads at-tached to representations at different depths of a single Transformer. Its layers encode progressively more abstract features, from low-level structure in early layers to high-level semantics in deeper ones [34]. For L depth layer with k heads, we attach them at the blocks nearest to ( L/k , 2L/k , . . . , 

L). The resulting heads form a hierarchy of energy terms, each enforcing the structure visible at its depth. We train on the sum of these energies, allowing each layer to contribute at its own level of abstraction. This not only yields a better energy function but also decomposes the generative process across layers, opening the way to understanding each layer’s role and further applications. 3Preprint 3.2 TRAINING TRANSFORMER ENERGY -B ASED MODELS

This positive-only design also clarifies how PRA is both similar and different to representation align-ment in diffusion models, where the distinction lies in what each network receives as input. That line of work aligns the features of a noisy input xt with the VFM features of its clean counterpart x0; the clean target is dynamic and guides the denoising trajectory toward the data manifold. In our setting both the EBM and the VFM receive the same clean input x+, so PRA does not guide any sampling trajectory. Instead it shapes the geometry of the low-energy region of the landscape directly, PRA encourages semantically structured representations of positive samples.

At inference, we adjust the sampling step size while keeping the injected noise scale fixed: 

xt+1 = xt − η∇xEθ (xt) + σϵ, ϵ ∼ N (0 , I ). (8) 

η controls the balance between energy-driven refinement and stochastic exploration. It provides control over sample quality, without retraining or additional unconditional network evaluations. Increasing the step size η amplifies the deterministic energy-gradient update relative to the fixed noise. Similarly, extending sampling beyond the training horizon allows further refinement of inter-mediate samples. Empirically, both adjustments can sharpen individual samples and produce more class-typical images at the cost of mode coverage.

Inpainting. Inpainting is particularly simple in energy-based models. Our EBM defines a single energy over image latents, allowing refinement from any intermediate completion. We mask the input image before VAE encoding, preventing access to ground-truth content in the missing region. We then apply sampling updates to the missing latent region, restoring the observed-region latents to their initial encoded values after each step. ## 5 RELATED WORK 

Foundations of Energy-Based Models. Energy-based models assign an unnormalized scalar en-ergy to each input and define a Gibbs distribution over the data space, an idea with deep roots in statistical physics and undirected graphical models [17, 23]. Classical instantiations such as Re-stricted Boltzmann Machines relied on tractable architectures to make learning feasible, which in turn limited their representational capacity. Du & Mordatch [9] showed that short-run Langevin MCMC combined with a replay buffer yields a practical training loop, producing coherent samples on CIFAR-10. Follow-up work refined the training recipe through improved MCMC initialization, sampler matching terms, and regularization strategies [10, 29, 30], yet these methods operated at resolutions of 32 ×32 or less diverse dataset, leaving the scalability of the paradigm unresolved. 

Latent-Space Energy-Based Models. Pang et al. [32] proposed training an EBM as a prior over the latent codes of a variational autoencoder, while Hill et al. [16] explored energy-based priors for hierarchical latent variable models. Nie et al. [28] further demonstrated controllable generation by manipulating energies in a learned latent manifold. VAEBM [46] combines a VAE with an EBM refinement in data space, reparameterizing the MCMC chain through the VAE’s latent space to make sampling practical at higher resolutions. In contrast, we train a single scalar output Transformer energy function in the frozen latent space of a pretrained autoencoder [35], keeping the EBM as the sole generative component. 

Scaling Energy-Based Models. Several recent efforts have pushed EBMs toward higher resolution and stronger performance, though each introduces auxiliary mechanisms that depart from the pure energy-based formulation. EGC [14] hybridizes energy-based learning with diffusion modeling and can produce 256 ×256 ImageNet samples. However, its sampling procedure relies on the full diffusion denoising trajectory. On the adversarial side, Grathwohl et al. [13] reinterpret classifier logits as energies, and Yin et al. [47] scale this idea to ImageNet 256 ×256. However, their sampler is initialized from an auxiliary image dataset at both training and inference, and is never seeded from noise at this scale. Trained contrastively, TEM generates high-fidelity 256 ×256 images, enabling inference-time scal-ing and all EBM native applications. Appendix G provides an extended discussion of related work. 

## 6 LIMITATIONS AND CONCLUSION 

Limitations. TEM’s primary limitation is the computational cost of iterative sampling during both training and inference. One promising direction is to train a feed-forward proposal model to gen-erate candidates in low-energy regions, reducing the sampling needed to refine them. Extended sampling also introduces a fidelity–diversity trade-off: longer trajectories sharpen images but can reduce diversity and worsen FID. Adaptive noise schedules and proposals that enable movement between distinct modes could help preserve diversity while improving sample quality. 

Conclusion. In this work, we introduce the Transformer Energy-Based Model (TEM), an approach that addresses long-standing scalability issues in contrastive training of energy-based models. With a modern transformer backbone, multi-layer energy extraction, and representation-aware training, we demonstrate that a contrastively trained EBM can synthesize high-fidelity ImageNet images at 256 ×256 and 512 ×512, competitive with other generative models. We also show that the properties that motivate energy-based modeling in the first place, compositional generation, inpainting, and 9Preprint out-of-distribution detection, remain available at this resolution. We hope this work serves as a starting point for further scaling of contrastively trained EBMs.

## A THE SAMPLER -M ATCHING LOSS 

Maximum likelihood training of an EBM minimizes LNLL = EpD [Eθ (x)] + log Z(θ), whose gra-dient is given in Eq. 1: a positive phase over data and a negative phase over samples from pθ . The negative phase is the obstacle, since exact samples from pθ require MCMC run to convergence. In practice we replace pθ by the distribution qθ of the final state of a short Langevin chain initialized at N (0 , I ) or at a past sample. The resulting gradient, 

Lcontrast −only = EpD (x)[Eθ (x)] − Esg( qθ (x′)) [Eθ (x′)] , (9) is therefore biased by the gap between qθ and pθ , and the bias is largest exactly when the sampler fails to reach the model’s low-energy regions. Following Du et al. [10], we introduce a loss motivated by this gap that encourages low-energy, diverse sampler outputs: 

LKL = KL( qθ ∥ psg( θ)) + const = Eqθ (x)[Esg( θ)(x)] + Eqθ (x)[log qθ (x)] , (10) where the stop-gradient sg( ·) freezes the energy parameters so that gradients flow only through the sampler, and the constant − log Z(sg( θ)) does not depend on the differentiated parameters. ## B TRAINING & I NFERENCE DETAILS 

B.1 TRAINING DETAILS .The energy head consists of a two-layer MLP with SiLU activations. Details of the transformer ar-chitecture are provided in Table 3. All experiments operate in the latent space of a pre-trained Stable 14 Preprint Diffusion VAE [35], following Peebles & Xie [33] and are trained with bfloat16 mixed precision. The latent space is clipped by norm 100 to stabilize sampling for 256 ×256 and by norm 200 for 512 ×512, which is chosen as the threshold containing over 99 .99% of ImageNet training latents, which stabilizes sampling. A smaller threshold clips real data points, while a larger one expands the region the sampler has to search, which slightly degrades performance. For sampling, we use a step size of 500 and noise scale of 0.05. With a small step size, the network would have to produce very large input gradients to move samples meaningfully within a limited number of steps, and we found that this destabilizes training. With an excessively large step size, however, each update is coarse, and the model struggles to learn a fine-grained energy landscape. Following prior work [9, 10, 12], who likewise decouple the step size from the noise scale and use a step size much larger than the standard Langevin prescription, we select the step size empirically to balance these two effects. During training, 40-step sampling is employed. The replay buffer’s replay probability is set to 0.99 and capacity is 16000. Class labels are drawn independently for every chain, including chains initialized from the buffer. Buffer states therefore serve as class independent initializations near the data manifold, which the conditional energy re-fines toward the requested class. Once full, each newly added sample replaces a uniformly randomly selected buffer entry. For the sampler matching term, the distance with the nearest neighbor term is calculated based on the distance from 100 randomly sampled seeds to the replay buffer follow-ing [10]. We use DINOv2-large [31] as the VFM and implement the projection network Pϕ as a lightweight CNN with spatial normalization following Singh et al. [37] attached after the block nearest to one-third of the total network depth. We use λ = 1 for energy L2 regularization, λ1 = 1 

and λ2 = 0 .6 for overall objective’s coefficient at Equation 7 following Du et al. [10] and Singh et al. [37]. We optimize the model using Adam with a learning rate of 2 × 10 −4, momentum parameters 

(β1, β 2) = (0 , 0.999) , ϵ = 10 −8, and no weight decay. We keep an exponential moving average (EMA) of the weights with decay 0.9999 , updated after every optimizer step. All reported samples and metrics use the EMA weights. During training, we replace the class label with a null token with probability 0.15 , enabling unconditional energy evaluation for OOD detection.

## E COMPOSITIONAL GENERATION IN 256 X256 CLEVR D ATASET 

To quantitatively evaluate TEM’s compositional performance, we train TEM on a 256×256 location-conditioned CLEVR [20] dataset consisting of 70,000 images. Prior work on compositional gen-eration evaluates at 128 ×128 with a classifier trained at that resolution. We extend this setting to 256×256 to test whether the compositional advantage of energy-based models persists at higher res-olution, which is a harder generative setting. The following describes the training setup and the evaluation protocol used to compare against Composable Diffusion [25]. 

Training a location-conditioned TEM on CLEVR 256 ×256. We train our TEM-B model and evaluate it by providing object locations from real images and composing images according to the number of objects. We condition the energy network on a target object location p = ( x, y ) ∈ [0 , 1] 2

(normalized image coordinates) through two complementary pathways: a global coordinate code injected by adaptive layer normalization, and a spatial bias added directly to the patch-token grid. To condition TEM on location, we encode each coordinate with a NeRF-style Fourier feature encoding, 

γ(c) = c, sin(2 0πc ), cos(2 0πc ), . . . , sin(2 L−1πc ), cos(2 L−1πc ), (13) 17 Preprint which gives a richer positional signal than raw coordinates [27]. With L=8 frequency bands, (x, y )

maps to a 2(1+2 L)=34 -dimensional code, which is linearly projected, combined with the (optional) attribute embeddings, and injected into every transformer block via AdaLN. We train for 150k steps with batch size 64. 

Evaluation protocol. Composable Diffusion [25] operates at 128 ×128, and its evaluation classifier is trained at this resolution. We use their released 128 ×128 CLEVR checkpoint directly, and train the classifier ourselves following their training recipe with 128 ×128 CLEVR dataset. TEM gener-ates at 256 ×256, and we downsample its samples to 128 ×128 only for scoring, so both methods are judged by the same classifier at the same resolution. The classifier reaches over 99.9% detec-tion accuracy on real 128 ×128 CLEVR images, so the reported accuracies reflect generation quality rather than detector error. We report detection success rate over 1,000 scenes for 1-, 2-, and 3-object compositions. 

## F OUT -OF -D ISTRIBUTION DETECTION 

TEM as an OOD Detector. TEM provides a scalar energy that we evaluate as an OOD score, using lower energy to indicate more ID-like inputs. We evaluate this property as a secondary capability of TEM. Specifically, we use a TEM-XL model trained with class-label dropout: during training, the class condition is randomly replaced with a null token, allowing the model to learn an unconditional energy function Eθ (x) alongside the conditional one. At test time, we query the unconditional branch and use the scalar energy S(x) = −Eθ (x) as the OOD score in a single forward pass, with no class labels, post-hoc calibration, or OOD exposure.

All baselines use a single network evaluation, matching the single-forward-pass cost of TEM. For DiT, we use timestep t=350 . For SiT+REPA, we select the timestep that matches the same noise level. We include SiT-XL/2+REPA to separate two possible explanations for TEM’s performance. Since TEM is trained with PRA, its energy function inherits semantic structure from VFM, and one might attribute the gain to that supervision rather than to the energy score itself. SiT-XL/2+REPA receives the same DINOv2 alignment signal but is scored by its denoising residual, and its AUROC is close to that of SiT-XL/2 (Table 2). Alignment to a vision foundation model therefore does not by itself account for the gap. What distinguishes TEM is that the energy provides a single scalar that directly ranks inputs by fit to the learned distribution.

Parameter counts refer to the 256 ×256 configuration unless a resolution-specific model was released. G.2 SCALING ENERGY -BASED MODELS 

A parallel line of work attains scale by learning energy landscapes from predictive targets rather than from negative samples drawn from the model itself. Du et al. [11] assign a scalar energy to each diffusion timestep via denoising score matching, recovering MCMC-based composition but forgo-ing a single global energy. Equilibrium Matching [42] fits a time-invariant equilibrium gradient through a regression objective, defining the energy only implicitly through its gradient field. Its ex-plicit scalar-energy variants (EqM-E) substantially degrade generation quality (FID 73.40 and 75.53 in the B/4 setting). Energy-Based Transformers [12] learn an energy landscape by backpropagat-ing a reconstruction loss through unrolled inner-loop optimization. Energy Matching [1] explicitly parameterizes a scalar potential, but achieves its high-fidelity generation results by combining an optimal-transport objective with contrastive energy learning. Its image generation experiments are limited to 32 ×32 resolution (CIFAR-10 and unconditional ImageNet 32 ×32), so it is not included in Table 1. In contrast, TEM directly parameterizes a single scalar energy and trains it contrastively using only negatives produced by its own MCMC sampler, without a transport or flow-matching objective. Thus, while prior work demonstrates that energy parameterizations can scale when coupled with predictive, transport-based, or reconstruction-style objectives, our results show that an explicit, con-trastively trained EBM can itself scale to ImageNet 256 ×256. 19
