A FIRST-STEP BIAS RESTORES LOST DIVERSITY
IN DIFFUSION MODEL DISTILLATION
Wenwu Tang⋄, Dong Wang⋄, Yun Cheng⋆, Olga Saukh⋄,§
⋄Graz University of Technology, §Complexity Science Hub, Austria
⋆Swiss Data Science Center, Switzerland
{wenwu.tang, dong.wang, saukh}@tugraz.at, yun.cheng@sdsc.ethz.ch
ABSTRACT
Distribution-matching distillation enables high-quality image generation in only a few denoising steps.
However, distilled student models often map different initial noise samples to similar outputs under
the same prompt. To locate where diversity is lost, we compare the teacher and student along the
sampling trajectory. The largest measured reduction in the response to different initial noises occurs
during the first, high-noise step. When we replace the first step with the teacher’s corresponding
trajectory and leave the final three student steps unchanged, most of the output diversity returns. This
suggests that the first step is a key point for restoring diversity. Motivated by this finding, we introduce
ReSeed, a post-distillation method that adds a seed-response bias adapter only to the first student step
and aligns its velocity with the teacher’s first-interval secant velocity. All student weights and later
denoising steps remain unchanged. We evaluate ReSeed on three text-to-image models (SD3-Medium,
SD3.5-Medium, and FLUX.1-dev) and one text-to-video model (Wan2.1-T2V-1.3B). For a distilled
4-step FLUX.1-dev model, ReSeed requires only 0.00196% additional trainable parameters relative
to the base model and is trained on 8,000 prompt–noise pairs, while keeping the distilled student fully
frozen and requiring no target images, perceptual losses, or adversarial losses. It improves DINO
diversity by 48.3% while maintaining competitive visual quality. These results show that substantial
diversity can be recovered through a lightweight, data-efficient tuning of only the first denoising step,
without finetuning the full student model. Additional results are available on our Project Page.

Introduction
Diffusion and flow models generate high-quality images and videos, but often require many network evaluations [Ho
et al., 2020, Esser et al., 2024]. Distillation reduces this cost by training a student to generate samples in only a
few denoising steps [Salimans and Ho, 2022, Song et al., 2023, Yin et al., 2024b]. However, students trained with
distribution matching distillation (DMD) often produce similar outputs from different initial noises under the same
prompt. This loss of diversity reduces the range of results that users can obtain from a few-step model.
Previous works try to address this problem by aligning the student’s response to initial noise [Huang et al., 2026],
assigning different training objectives to early and late denoising steps [Wu et al., 2026], or using the base model for the
first sampling step [Gandikota and Bau, 2025]. These studies highlight the importance of preserving variation during
early generation. Further related work is discussed in 6. Building on these findings, we ask a practical question: can
we recover diversity in an already distilled student by changing only its first denoising step, while keeping its original
weights and later steps fixed?
We first examine this question through controlled interventions on SD3-Medium. We replace the first student step with
the teacher’s trajectory over the same time interval, then run the final three student steps without any changes. This
yields a higher DINOv3 diversity than that of the 28-step teacher. The result shows that the unchanged later steps can
preserve much of the diversity recovered by the teacher’s first interval. It supports our central hypothesis: a substantial
part of the measured diversity loss can be restored at the first step, without retraining the rest of the student.
Motivated by this finding, we introduce ReSeed, a post-distillation method that trains small bias adapters only for the
first student step (Figure 3). We add learned per-channel biases to transformer block outputs and keep all original
student weights frozen. Training matches the student’s first-step velocity to the teacher’s average velocity over the
same interval, using the same prompt and initial noise. Matching these velocities also matches the states reached at the
first student boundary. At inference, the biases are enabled only during the first step; later steps use the unchanged
student, with no teacher calls or additional denoising steps. The biases are shared across prompts and noise seeds, so
they do not provide new seed-specific information. Instead, the frozen nonlinear network turns the shared biases into
input-dependent corrections. Our analysis links diversity recovery to better alignment with the teacher’s seed-dependent
responses, rather than simply increasing response magnitudes. This explains why we study a small first-step correction
instead of changing the entire sampling process.
We evaluate ReSeed on SD3-Medium, SD3.5-Medium, and SenseFlow-FLUX, a 4-step student of FLUX.1-dev [Black
Forest Labs, 2024, Ge et al., 2026], and extend the evaluation to Wan2.1 text-to-video generation. For 4-step FLUX.1-
dev student, ReSeed trains only 233,472 parameters (0.00196% of the denoiser parameters) using 8,000 prompt–noise
pairs. It requires no target images, perceptual losses, or adversarial losses. DINOv3 diversity increases by 48.3%, while
FID decreases from 40.22 to 34.65. Experiments on the other image models and the video model also show diversity
gains, with trade-offs in some quality metrics. These results show that substantial diversity can be recovered with very
few trainable parameters after distillation.
Our main contributions are:
• Most diversity loss occurs in the first denoising step. We show that most of the diversity loss in few-step distilled
models occurs in the first denoising step, while the frozen suffix retains the ability to preserve diversity.
• A small first-step adapter can recover lost diversity. We introduce ReSeed, a first-step residual tuning that restores
lost diversity while keeping the pretrained distilled student frozen, requiring less than 0.005% additional trainable
parameters and approximately 8,000 prompt–noise pairs.
• Existing distilled models can be repaired without repeating distillation. Our results demonstrate that diversity
in an already distilled model can be substantially improved through a lightweight post-hoc tuning, without repeating
distillation or fine-tuning the whole original student. This provides a practical way to enhance the diversity of
existing few-step generative models after training.

Flow matching.

Diversity as the response to the initial noise.
For a fixed prompt c, the deterministic sampler maps initial noise z to
the output Fθ(z, c). We call the change in output caused by changing the initial noise the seed response. We write the
complete sampler as Fθ(z, c) = Hθ(Rθ(z, c), c), where Rθ maps the initial noise to the first-step state and Hθ denotes
the remaining steps. We use this decomposition to test whether the observed diversity loss can be corrected at the first
step while keeping the remaining steps unchanged.
3
First-step correction restores diversity
A first-step repair can restore output diversity only if the unchanged later steps still preserve differences between seeds.
We test this condition on the 4-step SD3-Medium student, then ask whether better first-step seed responses accompany
more diverse final images.
3.1
First-step replacement restores diversity
We replace the first student step with corresponding teacher steps covering the same time interval. The remaining three
steps use the unchanged student. Both samplers share the same prompts and initial noises, so the replacement changes
only how they reach the first student boundary. This replacement increases DINOv3 diversity from 0.2142 to 0.3117.
Replacing additional student intervals brings smaller further gains (Figure 2). The replacement requires extra teacher
evaluations and serves as a diagnostic test, rather than an equal-cost alternative to the student.
These results show that the unchanged final three steps can produce diverse images when given a different first-boundary
state. They identify the first step as an effective repair point in this setting, without requiring changes to the remaining
steps. These findings motivate matching the student’s first-step state to the teacher’s. For an Euler step, this is equivalent
to matching the student’s velocity to the teacher’s average velocity over the same interval, which forms the training
objective in Section 4.

3.2
Better seed-response alignment accompanies higher diversity
For a fixed prompt, a seed response is the difference between two first-step endpoints from different initial noises. On
5,000 COCO prompts with ten seeds each, we compare this response with the teacher’s for the frozen SD3-Medium
student and seven ReSeed checkpoints. Mean response error falls by a factor of 2.7 as final-image DINOv3 diversity
rises from 0.213 to 0.290 (Figure 4). Across checkpoints, lower error accompanies higher diversity for 90% of prompts.
This association supports correcting the first endpoint while keeping later steps unchanged.

ReSeed: a seed-response bias for the first step

steps share backbone parameters, so changing an original bias would affect them all. Instead, we freeze the backbone
and disable the adapters in steps 2–4. The adapters contain no seed or prompt information; their input-dependent effect
arises through the frozen network.

Bias adapter parameterisation.
For block b in the model, we add constant per-channel vectors βb ∈RN to the
block output of both streams, each βb is an N-dimensional channel vector. The bias is broadcast over the batch
5


and token dimensions and initialized to zero, so an untrained adapter reproduces the original student model exactly.
For SD3-Medium, Nbias = 72,192 parameters or 0.00356% of the 2,028,328,000 student parameters. Unlike BitFit
[Ben Zaken et al., 2022], ReSeed does not unfreeze existing model biases; it adds new zero-initialised residual vectors
whose activity is gated by the denoising interval.

Experiments and Results
5.1
Experimental setup
Models and datasets.
We evaluate 4-step DMD students of SD3-Medium and SD3.5-Medium [Esser et al., 2024, Yin
et al., 2024b], the 4-step SenseFlow student of FLUX.1-dev [Black Forest Labs, 2024, Ge et al., 2026], and the 4-step
Wan2.1-T2V-1.3B DMD2 student [Wan Team, 2025, Yin et al., 2024a] released with Data-Forcing Distillation (DFD)
[Chen et al., 2026]. Image adapters use JourneyDB prompts [Sun et al., 2023]; the video adapter uses a pool of 4,000
ViPE-Wild-1M captions [Huang et al., 2025]. Training requires only prompts and initial noise.
Training.
We train only first-step bias adapters, keeping the teachers and student backbones frozen. We use three
training seeds for the main image results and one for video. Reported checkpoints, training budgets, and sampling
settings are given in Appendix A.
Evaluation.
We evaluate image quality on 5,000 images generated from COCO-2017 validation captions [Lin et al.,
2014], using CLIP score [Radford et al., 2021], PickScore [Kirstain et al., 2023], HPSv2.1 [Wu et al., 2023], the LAION
aesthetic score [Schuhmann et al., 2022], and FID [Heusel et al., 2017]. Image diversity uses a separate set of 100
prompts with R = 10 independent initial noises per prompt. For a feature extractor ϕ, we define same-prompt diversity

5.4
Training and inference overhead
ReSeed trains only 0.002–0.004% of the student denoiser’s parameters (46,080–233,472; Table 4), excluding text
encoders and autoencoders. The teacher is used only over the first student interval, and the loss is computed on the
first-step velocity, so training needs no later denoising steps, no decoding, no target images, and no discriminator or
perceptual network. The reported image checkpoints use 1,900–2,100 updates of four prompt–noise pairs, and Wan2.1
uses 300; most of the gain appears within the first few hundred updates (Appendix A). At inference, the bias is added
only at step 1, so the number of function evaluations is unchanged. Per-sample latency rises by 0.14–0.41% and peak
activation memory by at most 1 MiB.

Related work
Few-step distillation.
Prior approaches progressively halve sampler steps [Salimans and Ho, 2022], learn consistency
maps to trajectory endpoints [Song et al., 2023, Luo et al., 2023], match output distributions [Yin et al., 2024b,a], match
trajectory distributions [Luo et al., 2025], or add adversarial objectives [Sauer et al., 2023]. We study diversity loss in
already distilled students.
Diversity-aware diffusion model distillation.
Reduced sample diversity is a known cost of these methods. Score-
identity distillation studies guidance-dependent diversity and introduces zero- and anti-guidance objectives [Zhou
et al., 2025]; GAD matches initial-noise Jacobian–vector products during training [Huang et al., 2026]. At the sampler
level, Gandikota and Bau [2025] trace part of the loss to premature structural commitment and recover diversity by
using the multi-step base model for the first denoising step. At the training-schedule level, DP-DMD gives that step
a teacher-derived target-prediction objective while retaining DMD later [Wu et al., 2026], and 1.x-Distill separates
coarse distribution matching from detail refinement [Li et al., 2026]. Data-Forcing Distillation instead performs a short
post-training correction using teacher scores on real video samples [Chen et al., 2026]. CADS improves diversity at
inference by adding noise to the conditioning signal and gradually reducing it during sampling [Sadat et al., 2024].
ReSeed differs in both question and mechanism: it diagnoses an already distilled, frozen image generator and changes
only the first step of the frozen student without target images, adversarial training, or a teacher call at inference. We use
finite differences only for diagnosis. Vendi is an alternative set-level diversity summary [Friedman and Dieng, 2023].
Parameter-efficient adaptation.
LoRA adds low-rank updates to frozen weights [Hu et al., 2022] and BitFit tunes
biases [Ben Zaken et al., 2022]. TimeStep Master assigns LoRA experts to different timestep intervals and mixes them
with time-dependent gating [Zhuang et al., 2025]. Our residual bias is substantially simpler: it is active only during the
first solver interval, adds no expert router, and serves as both intervention and diagnostic.
7
Discussion & Conclusion
ReSeed repairs the first denoising step of a distilled student while leaving the later steps unchanged. It adds small
residual biases to the frozen denoiser and trains them to match the teacher’s average velocity over the first high-noise
interval. At inference, the biases act only in step 1, so steps 2–4 use the original student. Training needs no target
images or videos. On SD3-Medium, SD3.5-Medium, and SenseFlow-FLUX, the adapters add only 0.002–0.004%
denoiser parameters and raise DINOv3 diversity by 37.1%, 39.3%, and 48.3%, respectively, while lowering FID.
On Wan2.1-T2V-1.3B DMD2, 300 updates raise video-level DINOv2 diversity by 27.1%, recovering 56.2% of the
measured teacher–student gap. ReSeed thus provides a low-cost post-distillation correction without retraining the
student.
8
Limitations
ReSeed training requires multi-step teacher trajectories. Our experiments use flow-matching transformers; we have
not tested non-flow-matching diffusion models or U-Net backbones, so it remains unclear whether the same first-step
behavior holds for those models.

The teacher uses guidance scale 7 with the shifted-time threshold a = 0.96: below the

A.1
Video training data and evaluation protocol
Training data and budget.
We use 4,000 captions extracted from the first 40 shards of the long split of the released
ViPE dataset csusupergear/vipe_fliter_complete. Only caption text and its precomputed UMT5 embeddings
enter ReSeed training; the associated videos are not used as targets. Each update samples four prompt–noise pairs on
four H100 GPUs. The 300-update checkpoint therefore uses 1,200 prompt–noise pairs. The full training trajectory
contains 2,000 updates (8,000 prompt–noise pairs), with checkpoints saved every 100 updates.
Target and optimization.
The teacher starts from the same noise as the student and takes 13 boundary-aligned Euler
microsteps from t0 = 0.999 to t1 = 0.937, with CFG 5 and the released negative prompt. The microstep grid is
uniform in the unshifted time coordinate, then shifted and aligned to the exact student boundaries. The target is the
secant velocity over this interval, not the teacher’s instantaneous prediction or a decoded video. We fit zero-initialized
FP32 bias adapters with AdamW (learning rate 10−3, momentum coefficients 0.9 and 0.999, zero weight decay, and
gradient-norm clipping at 1). All 30 blocks receive one 1,536-channel bias shared over spatial and temporal tokens, for
46,080 trainable parameters; the frozen backbone is evaluated in BF16. Biases are enabled only on the first student step,
with no change to the remaining three steps. The 13-step Euler training target differs from the 50-step UniPC sampler
used to evaluate the teacher, as described above.
Evaluation prompts and counts.
We take the first 40 unique prompts from each of the eight VBench category files:
animal, architecture, food, human, lifestyle, plant, scenery, and vehicles. The source is Vchitect/VBench. A plant
prompt already present in lifestyle is skipped and replaced by the next prompt, leaving 320 unique prompts. For each of
the teacher, DMD2, DFD, and DMD2 with ReSeed, seeds 0–7 produce 2,560 videos.

Theoretical analysis
This appendix explains what first-step regression controls and how a shared bias can change responses to different seeds.
We first prove Proposition 1, then explain the gap between the bound and the measured error, including the correction
for a finite seed bank. We next relate these measurements to final-image diversity and finish with a local analysis of the
bias. The mathematical results concern endpoint responses; the connection to output diversity is tested empirically.

E.3
Empirical experiments and the relation to output diversity
Evaluation design.
We evaluate both sides of Equation 7 on 5,000 COCO5K prompts with ten seeds each, using
the frozen SD3-Medium student and seven checkpoints from one ReSeed run (100–1,900 updates). This gives 40,000
prompt–checkpoint comparisons. Table 9 reports the endpoint statistics and final-image feature diversity. These are
measured in different spaces and answer different questions.
Consistency and tightness of the bound.
All measured pairwise errors satisfy the finite-sample bound in Equation 13
with n = 10. This confirms that the two errors were computed consistently. The median ratio bP/ bB rises from 0.68 to
0.92 during training, as the estimated seed-shared share of endpoint error falls from 0.39 to 0.17 (Table 9). We next
examine whether the reduction in pairwise response error accompanies a change in final-image diversity.
Association with final-image diversity.
Mean pairwise response error decreases by a factor of 2.7, with a decrease
on every prompt. Mean DINOv3 diversity rises from 0.213 to 0.290 (+36.5%), with gains on 92% of prompts; as the
bound’s right-hand side falls, this diversity rises (Table 9).
Figure 4 shows the relation between pairwise response error and final-image diversity. Across the frozen student and
seven trained checkpoints, lower mean error consistently accompanies higher mean diversity (Spearman ρ = −1).
Comparing checkpoints within each prompt, halving the response error is associated with a 0.056 increase in DINOv3
diversity (95% CI [0.054, 0.057], prompt-clustered bootstrap). The within-prompt Spearman correlation is −0.54 (CI
[−0.55, −0.53]), and the association is negative for 90% of prompts. These results show an association, not a causal
effect of response error on final diversity.

Interpretation and limits.
The same bias is used for every seed, but its output correction need not be the same
because Jβ(z) depends on the prompt, noise and activation state. In a jointly affine map this Jacobian is constant and
the bias cancels exactly from every seed difference. In the nonlinear student, the leading correction is instead potentially
first order in β and is controlled by the difference of two input-specific Jacobians. If two inputs are close, that Jacobian
difference can be described locally by a mixed derivative, but independent Gaussian seeds need not be close; the result
instead expands in the small parameter change β, not in the distance between seeds. It does not establish that the local
approximation remains accurate at the final trained bias, or that its direction will reduce response error. Those are
empirical questions.
In summary, Proposition 1 identifies what the regression objective controls, and Proposition 2 explains how shared
internal biases can change seed differences. The observed link to final-image diversity comes from the matched
evaluations above and the teacher-prefix intervention, not from either proposition alone.
