Abstract
Image generation models have rapidly advanced, with many models now support-
ing generation conditioned on multiple reference images and textual instructions.
Such models enable a wide range of applications, including entity fusion and
attribute transfer across images. However, existing benchmarks for multi-reference
image generation rely on limited evaluation dimensions and fail to capture the
diverse challenges of the task. To address this limitation, we propose MDMR-
Bench, a Multi-Dimensional benchmark for Multi-Reference image generation.
MDMR-Bench is organized around representative capability settings and consists
of multiple evaluation subsets spanning diverse dimensions of multi-reference
image generation, including (1) common entity fusion and attribute transfer, (2)
multi-element extraction from a single image, (3) infographic manipulation, and
(4) visual reasoning-intensive generation. This design enables systematic analysis
of model capabilities and failure modes across different challenge dimensions.
Furthermore, to achieve more reliable and interpretable evaluation, we introduce
a checklist-based evaluation protocol that decomposes generation quality into

Introduction
Text-to-image [23, 46, 7, 11, 43] and instruction-based single-image editing models [44, 27, 2, 41, 42]
have achieved remarkable progress. They support high-quality image generation from text and fine-
grained, controllable edits on a single image. However, text alone is often insufficient to precisely
specify complex visual attributes, such as the exact appearance of clothing, or abstract properties like
hairstyle, pose, and artistic style. In practice, users naturally rely on multiple reference images to
convey such details, leading to a growing demand for multi-reference image generation. In response,
recent models, such as Qwen-Image-Edit [46] and FLUX.2 [3], as well as proprietary models like
GPT-Image-1.5 [30], Seedream [38, 5], and Nano-Banana Pro [13], support multi-image inputs and
demonstrate promising capabilities in integrating information from them.
While benchmarks for text-to-image generation (e.g., GenEval [12, 20], DPGbench [16]) and
instruction-based image editing (e.g.GEdit [27, 19], ImgEdit [52]) have become increasingly rich
and comprehensive, benchmarks for multi-reference image generation remain scarce. Representative
efforts have begun to explore this setting, with several benchmarks recently introduced. However, as
shown in Table 1, existing benchmarks remain limited in several aspects. First, some existing bench-
marks are limited in scale, often containing only a few hundred cases [48, 47], and provide relatively
narrow task coverage. Many primarily focus on simple fusion or transfer scenarios, while some are
restricted to specific applications, such as virtual try-on in VTEdit-Bench [25]. Second, reference
images are often overly simple and contain many visually unnatural samples, typically dominated by
salient foreground objects, and thus do not reflect the complexity of real-world scenes [47, 45, 48, 33].
Most importantly, existing benchmarks lack a multi-dimensional evaluation settings, typically orga-
nizing tasks along only a single aspect, such as the number of references or coarse task categories.
While some benchmarks vary reference properties (e.g., photo fused with anime) [33], such varia-
tions mainly reflect superficial input styles rather than the diverse capabilities required in real-world
multi-reference image generation.

answered by VLMs to assess specific aspects of the output, demonstrating better alignment with
human judgment. To further evaluate reliability, we compare checklist-based and score-based
evaluation using multiple evaluator models on MDMR-Bench. As shown in Figure 2, checklist-
based evaluation achieves substantially higher inter-judge agreement indicating more consistent
assessment across judges. Building on this insight, we adopt a checklist-based evaluation protocol,
where the checklist includes both global-level and atomic-level questions. Global-level questions
evaluate whether the generated image satisfies the overall prompt requirements, while atomic-level
questions focus on fine-grained details. We evaluate MDMR-Bench on eleven representative open-

models exhibit a large gap between open-source and proprietary systems, perform substantially better
on entity fusion than on infographic manipulation, and obtain lower scores on subsets with more
reference images. In summary, our key contributions are as follows:
• We introduce MDMR-Bench, a capability-diverse benchmark for multi-reference image generation
with 2,390 cases, emphasizing broader dedicated task coverage rather than a larger number of
cases.
• Our benchmark covers diverse challenge settings, including entity fusion, attribute transfer, multi-
element extraction, infographic manipulation, and reasoning-intensive generation.
• To the best of our knowledge, we are the first to adopt a checklist-based evaluation protocol
for multi-reference image generation, enabling fine-grained diagnosis of model capabilities and
failures.

Related Works
2.1
From Text and Single-Reference to Multi-Reference Image Generation
Text-to-image generation has achieved remarkable progress since the emergence of stable diffu-
sion [34]. Subsequent works such as Imagen [36], Flux [23], Qwen-Image [46] and Z-Image [43]
have significantly improved generation quality and text alignment. Building on these advances,
research has further explored single-reference image generation. These approaches mainly fall
into two categories: subject-driven generation, which preserves the identity of a reference subject
(e.g., DreamBooth [35] and OmniControl [50]), and editing-based generation, which modifies an
input image according to instructions (e.g., SeedEdit3 [44], FLUX-Kontext [2], Emu-Edit [39], and
DreamOmni [49]). However, textual instructions alone are often insufficient to precisely describe
complex visual attributes, such as hairstyles, poses, artistic styles, or specific identities. To address
this limitation, recent works have begun to incorporate multiple reference images as complementary
visual guidance. Representative models include Qwen-Image-Edit [46], DreamOmni2 [48], the GPT-
Image series [29, 30], the NanoBanana series [14, 13], and the Seedream series [38, 5]. However, it
remains unclear how well these models perform on multi-reference image generation, due to the lack
of reliable and fine-grained evaluation benchmarks.
2.2
Benchmarks for Reference-Based Image Generation
Existing benchmarks can be broadly categorized into single-reference and multi-reference settings.
3


Single-Reference Benchmarks
Early benchmarks such as MagicBrush [54] and EMU-Edit [39]
introduce large-scale, instruction-driven image editing datasets covering diverse tasks, but suffer from
relatively low-quality input images and limited coverage of task types. Subsequent works [52, 53, 27,
28, 19] focus on improving realism and coverage. For example, GEdit-Bench [27] proposes a general-
purpose benchmark grounded in real user instructions, while its follow-up version GEdit-v2 [19]
further improves human-centric coverage and consistency evaluation. Reason-Edit [18] focuses on
reasoning-intensive instruction-based image editing, which we also incorporate as a visual reasoning
dimension in our multi-reference benchmark.
Multi-Reference Benchmarks
OmniContext [47] introduces a multi-reference benchmark with
around 400 cases focusing on scene, character, and object fusion, while MICo-Bench [45] covers 897
cases across object-centric, human-centric, human-object interaction, and De&Re tasks; however,
both lack coverage of fine-grained attribute transfer. DreamOmni2-Bench [48] convers a relatively
comprehensive set of tasks, but contains only 319 cases, limiting its suitability for large-scale and
fine-grained evaluation, and lacks a systematic evaluation protocol. MultiBanana [33] extends the
number of reference images up to eight and systematically explores variations such as domain
mismatch (e.g., photo vs. anime), scale mismatch, rare concepts. However, it primarily varies the
properties of input references and lacks explicit task-level difficulty modeling. In addition, its visual
styles are relatively homogeneous, limiting the realism and diversity of evaluation scenarios.
2.3
Evaluation Metrics and Protocols
Many early single-image editing benchmarks [53, 54, 39, 18] provide ground-truth images, enabling
the use of similarity-based metrics such as L1 distance, CLIP [15], and DINO [6] for evaluation.
However, the target output is often not uniquely defined. As VLMs become increasingly powerful,
evaluation can move beyond traditional similarity-based metrics toward more semantic and instruction-
aware assessment. Recent works adopt VLM-based evaluators with score-based approaches, such as
VIEScore [22], ImgEdit-Bench [52], and GEdit-Bench [27], which produce one or a few scalar scores
(e.g., per-dimension ratings), yet remain coarse-grained and unstable cross-evaluation consistency.
More recently, an increasingly popular remedy in T2I evaluation is checklist-based evaluation, which
decomposes a prompt into atomic yes/no questions answered by VLMs. This paradigm is pioneered
by TIFA [17], and some following works [26, 24, 40, 8] have been shown to yield finer granularity,
better interpretability, and stronger alignment with human judgment than holistic scoring. However,
in multi-reference image generation, where fidelity must be verified against multiple heterogeneous
references simultaneously, checklist-based evaluation remains largely unexplored. We therefore adopt
a checklist-based evaluation philosophy for multi-reference image generation, which provides more
fine-grained, interpretable, and stable assessment than score-based evaluation.
3
MDMR-Bench
In this section, we introduce MDMR-Bench as a multi-dimensional benchmark for multi-reference
image generation, covering diverse and realistic generation scenarios such as entity fusion, attribute
transfer, multi-element extraction, infographic manipulation, and reasoning-intensive generation.
3.1
Challenging Dimensions
We organize the benchmark around representative capability settings in multi-reference image
generation. Entity Fusion and Attribute Transfer are established settings in prior benchmarks, while
Multi-Element Extraction tests selecting several elements from one source, Infographic Manipulation
tests structured content and layout editing, and Visual Reasoning tests implicit relationships. These
settings cover complementary capabilities. Specifically, we construct several challenge settings
spanning different aspects of multi-reference generation, as described below.
• Entity Fusion and Attribute Transfer. We include common multi-reference generation scenarios
involving entity fusion and attribute transfer to evaluate models’ ability to accurately combine
identities, attributes, and visual concepts from multiple reference images. In this subset, we vary the
number of reference images from 2 to 4 to further assess multi-reference composition capability.
• Multi-Element Extraction. Existing benchmarks [33, 48] often assume that each reference image
provides only a single relevant element. In contrast, we include scenarios where multiple relevant

• Infographic Manipulation. Recent models [13, 30] have demonstrated strong capabilities in

3.2
Sample Construction
The samples in MDMR-Bench are primarily constructed via an automatic and scalable pipeline,
consisting of three stages: (1) Image Gallery Construction, (2) Task Bucket Filling, and (3) Sample
Validation and Refinement, as illustrated in Figure 3. Although most samples are generated through
this unified pipeline, certain settings require specialized construction procedures, which are introduced
in their corresponding subsections.
Image Gallery Construction. In the first stage, we construct a large-scale image gallery that serves
as the foundation for subsequent benchmark construction. Images are primarily collected from the
LAION [37] with resolution above 1024. To complement web-collected images, we additionally
generate synthetic images using Nano Banana Pro and GPT-Image-1.5. Specifically, captions are
first extracted from real images and then used as prompts for image generation, aiming to retain the
depicted content while obtaining cleaner images. In the final benchmark, 48.2% of cases contain at
least one synthetic reference image, and 29% of reference images are synthetic. A paired real-versus-
synthetic experiment is reported in Appendix A.5. Each image is automatically annotated using
Gemini-3-Pro [9], which assigns structured semantic tags, including high-level content categories
(e.g., people, animals, objects, scenes, and posters), fine-grained visual attributes (e.g., hairstyle,
texture, facial expression, filter, and painting style), and auxiliary metadata such as the number of
individuals, age, body framing (e.g., full-body or half-body), and facing direction. The resulting
image pool contains approximately 500K images.
Task Bucket Filling. In the second stage, we define a set of task buckets for sample construction. Each
bucket specifies a task type together with the corresponding reference image sampling specifications
and requirements. For example, a three-image fusion bucket requires three reference images sampled
according to predefined entity and attribute constraints. To ensure diversity within each task type (e.g.,
three-image fusion), we divide tasks into multiple buckets based on variations in image categories
and compositions. Each bucket is populated with 10–50 samples. We organize sampling using two
categories of annotations from the image gallery: entity tags, including person, animal, object, scene,
text, outfit, and decoration, and attribute tags, including artistic style, filter, era, texture, color, hair,
expression, makeup, and pose. To further improve diversity, we incorporate auxiliary tags during
sampling, such as the number of people in an image and the amount of text in infographic images.

Sample Validation and Refinement. In the final stage, we perform sample validation and refinement
through a multi-step process. First, given the task description, we use Gemini-3-Pro [9] to verify
whether the sampled image combinations are valid and consistent with the intended task. Next,
Gemini-3-Pro is used to generate corresponding instructions in both English and Chinese. We
then conduct human verification to filter out low-quality or ambiguous cases, including those that
violate task constraints, contain low-quality images (e.g., heavy cropping or artifacts), or result in
semantically or visually inconsistent compositions.
With the above pipeline, we construct 936 entity fusion samples and 982 attribute transfer samples,
each containing 2 to 4 reference images. We further construct 101 multi-element extraction cases,
where multiple entities or attributes are extracted from one reference image and applied to another. For
infographic manipulation, we construct 278 cases (155 Chinese and 123 English) covering operations
such as addition, replacement, color modification, font change, texture/material editing, and style
transfer. For addition and replacement tasks, target elements are extracted from or generated based
on the original image to construct realistic and controllable editing scenarios. For visual reasoning,
we construct cases spanning temporal (e.g., time and season), stylistic, state, spatial, and layout
transformations, as well as operations such as addition, replacement, removal, and extraction. We first
use VLMs to generate candidate reasoning formulations, which are then instantiated into concrete
cases using GPT and Nano Banana Pro. The resulting cases are further divided into two categories:
analogical reasoning, where reference images demonstrate the target transformation pattern, and
logical reasoning, where the target output must be inferred from multiple reference images. We
provide key statistics of MDMR-Bench, with additional analysis included in the appendix. Figure 4a
shows the distribution of different challenge settings in the benchmark. Figure 4b reports the image
type distribution, organized into normal entities, global attributes, and local attributes.
3.3
Evaluation Metrics
We adopt a binary checklist-based protocol rather than asking a judge model for a single holistic
score. Each generated image is evaluated along five dimensions: Following, whether the output
strictly follows the prompt instruction; Consistency, whether reference elements and fine-grained
details are faithfully preserved; Composition, whether generated elements are arranged and interact
in a plausible way; Minimality, whether the output avoids unrequested modifications; and Quality,
whether the image is realistic, aesthetically coherent, and free of obvious artifacts.

Evaluation Results
4.1
Experimental Setup
Baseline Models We evaluate leading proprietary systems that support multi-reference generation,
including GPT-Image-2 [32], Nano Banana 2 [14], Nano Banana Pro [13], GPT-Image-1.5 [30],
Seedream 4.0 [38], and Seedream 5.0-Lite [5]. These models represent current high-performing
commercial image generation systems with strong instruction-following and multi-image condi-
tioning capabilities. We also include prevalent open-source baselines, including BAGEL [10],
DreamOmni2 [48], OmniGen2 [47], Qwen-Image-Edit [46], and FLUX.2 [3]. These models cover a
diverse set of unified multimodal generation and editing architectures. Evaluating them alongside
proprietary systems allows us to compare the capability gap between closed and open models, and to
identify which difficulty dimensions remain challenging for the broader research community.
Output Resolution. Each sample is annotated with a target output resolution. During evaluation,
we select the closest supported resolution for each model according to its native resolution settings,
using the same selection rule across systems.
4.2
Experimental Results

4.3
Task-Level Results
Table 3 breaks down performance by task type and reveals that different tasks stress different
capabilities. We have the following findings: 1) Proprietary models exhibit relatively balanced
performance across all task types, while open-source models show substantial performance variation
across different settings. This suggests that current proprietary systems have developed more
comprehensive multi-reference generation capabilities, whereas open-source models still rely more
heavily on task-specific strengths. 2) Interestingly, Multi-Element Extraction, which can be viewed
as a special case of Entity Fusion and Attribute Transfer, is often easier for current models than
using multiple separate reference images. A possible explanation is that consolidating multiple
reference elements within a single image reduces the effective multi-reference complexity, whereas
increasing the number of reference images introduces longer visual token sequences and greater
compositional difficulty, leading to degraded model performance. 3) Except for Nano Banana
Pro, all models achieve noticeably lower performance on the Infographic Manipulation subset,
suggesting that precise infographic editing remains a major challenge for current multi-reference
image generation systems. 4) For reasoning-intensive generation, models with stronger vision-
language understanding capabilities achieve substantially better performance. In particular, Nano
Banana Pro and Seedream 4 outperform most other systems on reasoning-related tasks. Interestingly,
BAGEL also attains relatively strong reasoning performance despite weaker overall image quality.
As a unified mixture-of-transformers architecture that jointly models visual understanding and
generation, BAGEL suggests that effective multi-reference reasoning depends heavily on integrated
visual comprehension capabilities.
4.4
Reliability of VLM Judge
To validate our checklist-based evaluation protocol, we compare VLM judge decisions with human
judgments. As shown in Table 4, the three proprietary judges achieve similar agreement ratios with
humans, with Gemini-3-Pro [9], GPT-5.4 [31], and Doubao-1.8 [4] reaching 0.853, 0.854, and 0.849,
respectively. Their rank correlations are also strong, especially Gemini-3-Pro [9], which obtains the
highest Spearman correlation (0.862) and Cohen’s κ (0.642). These results support the use of VLM
judges as scalable proxies for human evaluation in MDMR-Bench.

4.5
Diagnostic Analysis
We conduct two diagnostic analyses on the Attribute Transfer subset: (1) the impact of different
reference content types, and (2) observed performance across subsets with different numbers of input
images.
Impact of Reference Content Types. We observe as shown in Figure 6 that current models perform
substantially worse on human-related attribute transfer tasks, such as facial expression, makeup, outfit,
pose, and hairstyle, likely because these tasks require precise manipulation of human appearance
and identity. The dimension-wise heatmaps also reveal trade-offs hidden by the aggregate score.
For example, GPT-Image-1.5 achieves strong Following, indicating that it can satisfy the requested
attribute-transfer instructions across content types, but this comes with a much lower Minimality
score. In other words, it often follows the requested transfer by changing content that should have
remained untouched, which limits its Overall score.
Impact of the Number of Reference Images. Figure 7 shows lower performance on Attribute
Transfer subsets containing more reference images. From two to four references, average Overall is
lower by roughly 0.106, and Composition by roughly 0.122, on the plotted 0–1 scale.
5
Conclusion
We introduced MDMR-Bench, a 2,390-case, multi-dimensional benchmark for evaluating multi-
reference image generation. MDMR-Bench covers realistic multi-reference scenarios across entity
fusion, attribute transfer, multi-element extraction, infographic manipulation, and visual reasoning-
aware generation. To move beyond coarse holistic scores, we further adopt a checklist-based
evaluation protocol that decomposes each generation result into interpretable and visually verifiable
judgments. GPT-Image-2 leads the primary leaderboard evaluated by Gemini-3-Pro. Our evaluation
of representative proprietary and open-source models shows that multi-reference image generation
remains challenging. Proprietary systems substantially outperform open-source models, but even
strong models exhibit non-trivial failures in reference consistency, composition, and minimality.

A.1
Inter-Judge Agreement Analysis Setting
To evaluate the reliability of different evaluation protocols, we conduct an inter-judge agreement
analysis using four evaluator models: GPT-5.4 [31], Doubao-Seed-1.8 [4], Qwen3-VL-32B [1], and
Gemini-3-Pro [9]. We apply these evaluator models to assess outputs generated by four representative
image generation systems, including Nano Banana Pro [13], FLUX.2 [3], Seedream5 [5], and
Qwen-Image-Edit [46]. For score-based evaluation, we adopt a scoring prompt similar to the
evaluation protocol used in MultiBanana [33], where evaluator models assign scalar scores for each
evaluation aspect. For our checklist-based evaluation, evaluator models answer structured global-
level and atomic-level questions. We compute Krippendorff’s alpha [21] to measure inter-judge
agreement across different evaluator models. As shown in Figure 2, checklist-based evaluation
consistently achieves substantially higher agreement than conventional score-based evaluation across
most evaluation aspects, indicating improved evaluation consistency across judges. Separately,
Gemini score-based evaluation achieves Krippendorff’s α = 0.5896 against human scores using the
interval metric.
A.2
Cross-Judge Evaluation
The primary leaderboard in Table 2 uses Gemini-3-Pro alone. However, evaluator independence
is important. Therefore we conducted an additional non-Gemini judge analysis to directly test
whether our conclusions depend on the Gemini judge. This supplementary analysis is not required to
compute the benchmark score. Benchmark cases, generated outputs, Gemini-generated checklists,
and hierarchical aggregation are held fixed; only the final evaluator changes. Table 5 reports GPT-5.4
scores. GPT-Image-2, Nano Banana Pro, and Nano Banana 2 remain the top three under both judges,
while Seedream 4.0, GPT-Image-1.5, and Seedream 5.0 reorder. Together, these results indicate that
the main conclusions are stable under a non-Gemini evaluator and are not explained by same-family
coupling. Empirically, the coupling between the VLM evaluator and the image generator appears
limited: model-quality differences dominate over generator–judge family membership.

A.3
Human Evaluation Protocol
The human evaluation uses 200 benchmark cases and four generators: Nano Banana Pro, Seedream
5.0, FLUX.2, and Qwen-Image-Edit. Each generator contributes one output per case, for 800 gener-
ated outputs. The evaluation set contains 5,924 checklist decisions. Five annotators independently
judge each evaluated checklist item. They inspect the textual instruction, all reference images, and
the generated output, and answer “Y” or “N” according to whether the checklist item is satisfied,
using the same evaluation instructions as the VLM judges. Generator names and identities are hidden,
and the order of model outputs is randomized. The final human label is the majority vote of the five
annotators. Human inter-annotator agreement is Krippendorff’s α = 0.88.

A.7
Limitations
Although MDMR-Bench covers diverse multi-reference generation settings, constructing realistic
and semantically coherent multi-reference cases remains challenging for certain tasks, especially
reasoning-intensive and infographic-oriented scenarios. As a result, some challenge settings may still
have limited coverage compared with common entity fusion and attribute transfer tasks.
In addition, except for Entity Fusion, Attribute Transfer, and Analogical Reasoning, most subsets in
MDMR-Bench are constructed using only two reference images. This design choice is intentional:
rather than maximizing the number of reference inputs, we focus on evaluating the atomic capabilities
of current models, such as fine-grained attribute extraction, symbolic editing, and reasoning-aware
generation, under controlled settings.
The benchmark construction pipeline also relies on substantial manual filtering. Current multimodal
models still make mistakes when verifying subtle visual differences, distinguishing source-specific
details across multiple references, or assessing fine-grained attributes. Manual filtering improves the
reliability and authenticity of the benchmark, but also limits the scalability of dataset construction.
Finally, although our checklist-based evaluation improves evaluation consistency and interpretability,
automatic evaluation cannot fully replace human judgment. Certain aspects of image quality, such as
artistic preference, creativity, and cultural nuance, remain difficult to evaluate using deterministic
yes/no questions alone.
A.8
Compute Resources
MDMR-Bench primarily evaluates model inference rather than training new generative models. The
local computational cost therefore comes mainly from generating outputs for open-source baselines
and organizing the benchmark artifacts. For open-source models, inference was conducted on GPUs
with 80GB of memory to support high-resolution image generation and editing. The exact memory
footprint varies across models because the evaluated systems differ in parameter count, precision, and
reference-image handling. For proprietary generation models, we accessed the official APIs provided
by the model developers; the underlying hardware, memory allocation, and serving details are not
disclosed by the providers.
Checklist generation and checklist-based judging were also performed through API calls to the judge
model rather than through locally hosted judge inference. As a result, these two stages do not require
additional local GPU resources; their practical cost is dominated by API latency, rate limits, and the
number of multimodal requests. The evaluation pipeline is embarrassingly parallel across benchmark
samples and models. In practice, wall-clock runtime can be reduced by distributing open-source
model inference across multiple GPU workers and distributing API-based stages across available
API workers.
The benchmark itself does not require model training or hyperparameter search. Reproducing the
main evaluation requires storing the benchmark images, generated model outputs, checklist files,
and judge responses. We will release scripts that record model identifiers, prompts, input image
order, generation parameters when available, and judge outputs, so that future users can audit both
the generated images and the checklist-level decisions.

A.9
Broader Impacts
MDMR-Bench can have positive societal impact by improving the transparency and reliability of
multimodal image generation systems. Multi-reference image generation is increasingly used in
design, education, advertising, entertainment, and personal creative workflows. A benchmark that
diagnoses whether models preserve identities, transfer attributes correctly, follow instructions, render
text accurately, and compose references coherently can help developers understand failure modes
before deploying such systems in user-facing settings. More granular evaluation can also reduce
over-reliance on single holistic scores, which may hide important errors.
The benchmark may also benefit the research community by making model capabilities easier to com-
pare. Because MDMR-Bench decomposes evaluation into interpretable checklist items, researchers
can identify whether a model fails due to reference neglect, poor text rendering, inconsistent style
transfer, weak visual reasoning, or low-level artifacts. This can guide safer and more targeted model
improvement, and can help users choose appropriate systems for different creative tasks.
At the same time, better evaluation of image generation models can indirectly accelerate progress
in systems that may be misused. More capable multi-reference generation could be used to create
misleading media, impersonation content, deceptive advertisements, or other synthetic images that
are difficult for viewers to verify. Our work does not introduce a new generative model, and it does
not provide tools for bypassing safety filters. Nevertheless, evaluation benchmarks can influence
model development, so we encourage responsible use of MDMR-Bench for research, transparency,
and safety-oriented model assessment.
Potential mitigations include responsible dataset release, clear documentation of intended use, exclu-
sion of unsafe or inappropriate content, and encouraging model providers to pair improved generation
capabilities with provenance tools, watermarking, content moderation, and misuse monitoring. We
also encourage future work to study fairness and cultural coverage in multi-reference generation, since
reference-conditioned tasks may behave differently across identities, languages, artistic traditions,
and visual domains.
A.10
New Asset Documentation
MDMR-Bench introduces a new benchmark asset consisting of task metadata, input images, natural-
language instructions, generated checklist items, and evaluation annotations. Each sample records
the task family, subtask setting, image order, image roles, content types, and other metadata. This
documentation is intended to make the benchmark auditable and to reduce ambiguity in multi-
reference settings, where the ordering and role of input images are critical for reproducible evaluation.
The released benchmark will include data-format documentation, field descriptions, and scripts
for running checklist generation and checklist-based evaluation. We will also document known
limitations, including the reliance on manual filtering, the scope of task categories, and the fact that
proprietary model outputs may depend on API versions and availability. The dataset is intended for
academic evaluation and diagnostic analysis of image generation systems, not for identity verification,
surveillance, or deployment as an automated moderation tool.
Before release, we apply filtering to remove unsafe, inappropriate, or clearly low-quality samples.
The benchmark is constructed to avoid collecting private user data, and the annotation process is
conducted internally by the authors rather than through public crowdsourcing. We will provide the
benchmark with usage guidelines and license information appropriate for research use.
The code is publicly available at GitHub, and the benchmark is publicly available at Hugging Face.
A.11
Prompt Templates for Checklist Generation and Evaluation
MDMR-Bench follows a two-stage checklist-based evaluation protocol. First, a checklist generation
prompt asks a multimodal judge to produce atomic, visually verifiable questions for each benchmark
sample. Second, an evaluation prompt asks the judge to answer each checklist item for a model output.
This design follows the same motivation as binary checklist evaluation: complex multi-reference
requirements are decomposed into independent yes/no judgments, making model failures easier to
localize.
