Abstract 

Complex text-to-image requests remain diffi-cult to satisfy reliably. Verifying and repair-ing an image can fix an individual output, but a successful repair does not necessarily carry over to other requests. We present ImageRSI ,a framework that keeps the image generator frozen and combines three components: five-dimensional verification and repair within each task, an experience library shared across tasks, and offline updating of the judge. Candidate ex-periences enter the library only after paired A/B validation, and the judge is updated with super-vised fine-tuning and GRPO on accumulated image–judgment records and evaluated before reuse. We also introduce ImageReq-Bench , a benchmark of 961 requests built around hard cases in real-world image-generation requests, each paired with an evaluation checklist. Im-ageRSI achieves a Checklist Strict Accuracy (CSA) of 52.6% on MindBench (with Nano Banana Pro as the generator) and 65.66% on ImageReq-Bench, and we compare system out-comes across judge-training configurations. 

1 Introduction 

Text-to-image models such as GPT-Image and Wan2.7-Image (Mao et al., 2026) generate high-quality images with fine-grained style control and strong instruction following. Yet requests that com-bine multiple constraints still reveal failures in fac-tual knowledge, exact text rendering, object counts, attribute binding, and spatial relations (Huang et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2025; Sun et al., 2026). Re-quests may also leave some requirements implicit. For example, a request for a labeled diagram of a solar eclipse implicitly requires the Moon to appear between the Sun and Earth, with the three bodies aligned and legible labels identifying each body. Reliable generation therefore requires inferring the intended constraints, verifying that the resulting image satisfies them, and applying targeted repair when needed. Beyond fixing the current image, verification and repair leave trajectories that record the errors, the changes made to the generation conditions, and the resulting outcomes (Ye et al., 2025b; Yang et al., 2024; Zhu et al., 2025); these trajectories can pro-vide candidate strategies for future tasks. How-ever, a single successful repair does not establish that a strategy is reliable or transferable: stochastic generation can produce improvements by chance, redundant trajectories can overstate the evidence, and judge errors can reinforce incorrect diagnoses. Candidate experience therefore requires validation, explicit conditions of applicability, and criteria for revising or retiring it. Existing agentic systems address these issues only in part. Some apply verification and itera-tive repair to individual requests (Shen et al., 2026; Chen et al., 2025; Ouyang et al., 2026; He et al., 2026a; Feng et al., 2026a), and additionally incor-porate cross-task memory (Zhang et al., 2026c; Xiong et al., 2026). Retaining feedback, however, does not determine when the resulting experience is actually useful. Evaluation faces a related limi-tation: fixed-prompt benchmarks (Niu et al., 2025; He et al., 2026b) measures final-output quality, but it cannot by itself establish whether an agent im-proves as it accumulates experience across tasks. We propose ImageRSI , a verification-driven re-cursive self-improvement framework that couples within-task repair, cross-task experience reuse, and offline judger self-evolution, where the judger is the verification model that scores images. Within each task, the agent parses the request, retrieves rele-vant experience and, when needed, external knowl-edge, generates an image, and evaluates it for Align-ment, Faithfulness, Quality, Aesthetic, and Physics. Defect feedback then guides targeted prompt re-1arXiv:2610.32120v1 [cs.CV] 23 Oct 2026 pair while leaving the original requirements un-changed. Across tasks, an experience library stores candidate strategies, each recording when it ap-plies and when it does not, the prompt change it makes, and its observed effects and side effects; paired A/B validation decides which experiences are admitted for reuse. For judger self-evolution, selected trajectory images are paired with model-generated score-and-reason annotations for super-vised fine-tuning (SFT), followed by GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) to optimize scoring behavior. After evaluation, a candidate judge can guide verification and repair in a later collection round, yielding new records for further updates. Experience admission updates inference-time knowledge, whereas judger self-evolution updates the judge’s parameters of-fline; the image generator stays frozen throughout. Figure 1 gives a schematic example of task-level repair and cross-task reuse, and Figure 2 illustrates the three processes in detail. Our contributions are as follows: • We propose ImageRSI, a verification-driven recursive self-improvement framework for agentic image generation with a frozen image generator. Within each task, five-dimensional verification localizes unmet requirements, and repair rewrites the prompt to target these de-fects while locking the parsed requirements, so that satisfied requirements are preserved across rounds. • We formulate two complementary uses of re-pair trajectories: an experience library records when each strategy applies and requires paired A/B validation before reuse, while accumu-lated image–judgment records support offline judge post-training and evaluation before re-deployment. • We introduce ImageReq-Bench, a benchmark of 961 complex image-generation requests built around hard cases in real-world image-generation requests. Each request is linked to its source records and an evaluation check-list for assessing whether its requirements are jointly satisfied. • ImageRSI achieves state-of-the-art CSA on both benchmarks, reaching 52.6% on Mind-Bench and 65.66% on ImageReq-Bench and surpassing the strongest baselines by 10.2 and 9.28 pp, respectively. 2 Related Work 

Agentic image generation. Diffusion and multi-modal generators have substantially advanced im-age synthesis (Rombach et al., 2022; Podell et al., 2024; Esser et al., 2024; Sun et al., 2024; Team, 2024). However, satisfying factual, textual, spa-tial, and physical requirements that interact with one another remains challenging, as examined in MindBench and WISE (He et al., 2026b; Niu et al., 2025). Agentic approaches handle complex re-quests through planning, external tools, and itera-tive evaluation (Xue et al., 2024; He et al., 2026b; Zhang et al., 2026c), and Qwen-Image-Agent al-ready reuse memory across tasks (Zhang et al., 2026c). ImageRSI additionally specifies an explicit validation protocol and applicability conditions for candidate experience. 

Experience-driven improvement. Prior work has used verification and search to improve reason-ing (Hosseini et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2024; Cao et al., 2025; Fan et al., 2025), distilled lessons from agent trajectories (Zhao et al., 2024), and explored iterative improvement of programs or agent con-text (Zelikman et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2026a,b; Yang et al., 2026; Wei et al., 2026; Wu et al., 2026; Zheng et al., 2026). ImageRSI brings these ideas to image generation in two ways: it admits reusable experience through A/B testing, and it supports judger self-evolution through offline post-training on versioned verification records. Experience vali-dation checks whether a piece of experience applies before it is activated, and the judge-training loop turns newly collected failures and successes into supervision for later versions. 23 Method 

3.1 Overview 

ImageRSI uses image verification to repair the cur-rent output, maintain experience for later tasks, and accumulate training records for offline judger self-evolution. These processes address three con-nected challenges: identifying unmet requirements, deciding when a repair strategy can be reused, and adapting the judge to observed failures. For task t, let qt denote the request, it an op-tional input image, and Mt−1 the available expe-rience library. Given a frozen image generator G

and the judge J(k) from training iteration k, the task-level workflow produces an image xt and a trajectory τt that records the generation conditions, verification results, and repair actions. As shown in Figure 2, this trajectory provides both candidate experience for M and supervision for a later judge version. Experience updates the inference-time context, whereas judger self-evolution updates the judge’s parameters between data-collection rounds. The generator stays frozen throughout each run. 

3.2 Online Generation and Verification Requirement-preserving generation. The workflow first parses qt into a set of requirements 

Ct = {ct, 1, . . . , c t,m }, covering entities, attributes, counts, spatial relations, exact text, layout, and image-to-image (I2I) preservation or exclusion conditions. Holding Ct fixed across repair rounds gives a stable target: a revision must satisfy an unmet requirement rather than drop it from the evaluation criteria. Generation context combines factual and visual evidence, gathered through bounded ReAct-style retrieval (Yao et al., 2022), with procedural advice drawn from active experience. A language model selects the relevant entries from a compact meta-data index and adapts their actions to the current request (Appendix C.1). At round r, the prompt combines the request, the fixed requirements, the available evidence, the selected experience, and feedback from earlier rounds. After a failed round, experience selection is rerun with the observed defects as additional con-text, and the image generator produces one image from the resulting conditions. Appendix C.3 gives the prompt interfaces for each stage. 

Verification and targeted repair. The judge evaluates five core dimensions: Alignment (sat-isfaction of explicit instructions), Faithfulness 

(correct referents and factual content), Quality 

(visible defects), Aesthetic (visual presentation), and Physics (physical or spatial coherence). For each dimension, the verification interface returns a score, defect evidence, and a confidence value. For trained judges, which do not output confidence, an inference adapter supplies constant values (Ap-pendix D.1). Deterministic rules convert these judg-ments into an acceptance decision. 3.4 Judger Self-Evolution 

Judger self-evolution updates the judger, i.e., the verification model that serves as the in-loop judge, using records accumulated during generation and repair. The judge remains fixed within a task; pa-rameter updates take place offline between col-lection rounds. This process converts task-level observations into supervision for later judge ver-sions, whose five-dimensional scores and evidence-based explanations support acceptance decisions and prompt repair. 

From trajectories to training records. Genera-tion and repair under judge J(k) produce trajecto-ries T (k). Selected images are paired with score-and-reason annotations to form a versioned training set D(k). Each input z = ( q, C, E, i, x ) contains 4the request, parsed requirements, permitted evi-dence, optional task input image, and generated image; its target y contains a score and an evidence-based reason for each dimension. Targets come from model-generated annotations; acceptance by 

J(k) is not treated as ground truth. For example, an image that omits requested text can receive a low Alignment score with an explanation of the omis-sion. Each data version combines retained records with additional or oversampled cases addressing observed scoring weaknesses (Appendix D.1). 

Post-training the judge. SFT trains the judge to predict y from z, with loss computed only on response tokens (Appendix D.2). The GRPO stage, trained on requests disjoint from the SFT data, fur-ther optimizes scoring behavior while preserving the score-and-reason output format. 2 Its reward combines pointwise assessment of the five scores and their rationales with a within-group ranking adjustment; severe false accepts and critical fabri-cations or omissions place the reward in a lower band that accurate assessments on other dimensions cannot offset (Appendix D.2). Section 4.4 evalu-ates these stages through both scoring error and CSA. 

Returning the updated judge to the workflow. 

After evaluation, a candidate J(k+1) can replace the in-loop judge in Section 3.2: its scores enter Equa-tion 1, and its reasons guide prompt repair. Subse-quent runs then produce records for another offline update. Collection, post-training, evaluation, and redeployment define the proposed self-evolution cycle. Training annotation, in-loop verification, and external CSA evaluation serve distinct roles; external test verdicts are not fed back into gener-ation. The reported judge configurations evaluate the post-training stages, while repeated collection– training cycles remain a proposed extension.

4.1 Main Results 4.1.1 Experimental Setup Benchmarks. MindBench (He et al., 2026b) contains 500 image-generation requests requiring world knowledge or reasoning across ten cate-gories; three categories (Life, GU, and Math) in-volve image-to-image (I2I) tasks with an input im-age. ImageReq-Bench, constructed in this work, contains 961 requests across four reporting cate-gories: Illustration, Scene, Infographic, and Design & Art. Each request is paired with an evaluation checklist, yielding 8,165 check items in total. We use a category-stratified split into an experience set (530 requests), used to construct the experience library, and a disjoint test set (431 requests) for evaluation. Table 2 reports results on the test set. 

Metric. We adopt Checklist Strict Accuracy (CSA): a request counts as passed only if the de-livered image satisfies every item on its checklist. Requests without a generated image or with an eval-uator error count as failures, so the denominator includes every request in the evaluation set. The external evaluator is Gemini 3.1 Pro; it is indepen-dent of the system’s internal judge, and its verdict on a request is never fed back into the generation of that request. 

Implementation details. The generator is al-ways frozen: Qwen-Image-2.0 for the main Mind-Bench results and the judge experiments (§4.4), and Wan2.7-Image elsewhere. The internal judge scores each image along five dimensions: Align-ment, Faithfulness, Quality, Aesthetic, and Physics (§3.2). An image is accepted if its weighted score is at least 4.5 and no veto is triggered; otherwise, the system repairs the localized defects, using zero, one, or two repair rounds per request. For each baseline, we report the higher of our reproduction and the value claimed in its original paper. 

4.1.2 Results on MindBench 

As shown in Table 1, ImageRSI achieves an over-all CSA of 48.2%, outperforming all baselines. It improves over direct generation with its own gener-ator, Qwen-Image-2.0 (22.4%), by 25.8 percentage points (pp), and over the strongest agent baseline, GEMS (He et al., 2026c)(42.4%), by 5.8 pp; with Wan2.7-Image, ImageRSI reaches 47.4%. Per cate-gory, ImageRSI ranks first on IP (30%) and leads 5most clearly on Math (80% vs. 46% for Nano Ba-nana Pro). Replacing the generator with Nano Banana Pro raises the overall CSA of ImageRSI to 52.6% (263/500), 4.4 pp higher than the Qwen-Image-2.0 version; the difference comes mainly from SE (70% vs. 48%) and SL (72% vs. 56%). 

4.1.3 Results on ImageReq-Bench 

As shown in Table 2, ImageRSI achieves the high-est overall CSA, 65.66%. It improves over di-rect generation with Wan2.7-Image (38.28%) by 27.38 pp; over the strongest generative model, Nano Banana (56.38%), and the strongest agent baseline, GEMS (55.68%), by 9.28 and 9.98 pp, respectively; and over MemoGen R2 (47.56%) and SCOPE (Ren et al., 2026) (46.87%) by 18.10 and 18.79 pp. Per category, ImageRSI ranks first on Il-lustration, Scene, and Infographic. On Illustration and Scene, which together account for 75.2% of the test set, it leads the runner-up, GEMS, by 9.17 and 11.58 pp. 

4.2 Effectiveness of Verification and Repair 

We first test whether the gain from verification and repair comes only from generating one additional image, by comparing second-round strategies un-der the same generation budget. 

Experimental settings. The repair and experience-reuse ablations (§4.2 and §4.3) use the test set defined in §4.1.1, with Wan2.7-Image for generation and Gemini 3.1 Pro for evaluation. 

Compared strategies. All strategies share the same first-round image. If the judge accepts it, the image is delivered directly; otherwise, one of the following strategies generates a second image. Each request therefore receives at most two images, and all strategies have the same generation budget. (i) Best-of-2 resampling: regenerate with the same prompt and let the judge select the better image; (ii) Generic-feedback repair: ask the rewriter to revise the original prompt without specifying the defects; (iii) Localized repair (ImageRSI): spec-ify the localized defects in the rewrite instruction and lock the parsed requirements (§3.2) so that sat-isfied requirements are preserved. We also ablate requirement locking by removing it from localized repair, keeping all else unchanged. 

Results. As shown in Figure 3a, localized re-pair raises CSA from 53.4% to 65.7% (+12.3 pp), 10.7 pp above Best-of-2 resampling, which gains only 1.6 pp. The gain therefore does not come from the additional generation alone. Generic-feedback repair gains only 3.2 pp, indicating that signaling failure alone is of limited help. Without require-ment locking, CSA drops to 57.8%, 7.9 pp below full localized repair, suggesting that locking con-tributes substantially to the gain. Figure 4 presents two cases. Case (a) requires a desert roadside gas station to display a complete, legible Shell emblem, comprising the scallop-shell graphic and the brand name “Shell,” on both the canopy front and the fuel-pump casing. In the first-round image, both emblems show only the graphic without the text, failing the three text-related check items (6/9); the judge correctly localizes the miss-ing brand name. Case (b) requires the main charac-ter to present measurement results to two assistants in a radiation laboratory, with detailed constraints on her appearance and clothing and on how the two assistants differ. In the first-round image, she faces forward while operating an instrument, without in-teracting with the assistants, who are also not stand-ing behind the instruments; two items fail (8/10), and the judge identifies the missing interaction with the assistants. In both cases, the second images from Best-of-2 resampling and generic-feedback repair fail to fix these errors; in case (b), the generic-feedback image even contains three assistants and loses two previously satisfied requirements (6/10). Localized repair instead states the defect in the rewritten prompt and excludes it in the negative prompt (“graphic only, no text”; “facing forward and operating an instrument, no interaction”), after which all items pass in both cases.

4.4 Recursive Self-Improvement 

ImageRSI improves itself at two levels. Within a task, it revises its output round by round based on judge feedback; across tasks, it distills trajectories into experience and updates the judge with accu-mulated judgment records. This section evaluates the judge update (§3.4).

5 Conclusion 

ImageRSI couples five-dimensional verification and targeted repair with a validated cross-task ex-perience library and offline judger self-evolution under a frozen image generator. It reaches 52.6% CSA on MindBench with Nano Banana Pro and 65.66% on ImageReq-Bench. Under a matched generation budget, localized repair with require-ment locking outperforms resampling and generic feedback, and CSA increases with the size of the ex-perience library; post-training the judge on accumu-lated judgment records further improves end-to-end CSA. The gains from repeated collection–training cycles remain to be evaluated. 

Limitations 

We evaluate ImageRSI with several base image generators. Our focus is on improving generation quality for a given generator through verification, repair, and cross-task experience reuse. A related application arises when the base generator is re-placed. Here, existing experience could be reused to reduce the cost of accumulating it again. For ex-ample, one could study which experiences transfer directly and which need to be adapted to the charac-teristics of the new generator. Such optimizations could be added to the existing pipeline as an op-tional extension of the experience library, and they are independent of the contributions of our method.

A Implementation Details 

A.1 Generation and Delivery 

Retrieval is limited to four passes per request. Each round generates one image, and a request receives an initial round followed by at most two repair rounds; the direct-generation baseline uses a sin-gle round. In each round, the workflow selects experience, builds the prompt from the fixed re-quirements and earlier feedback, generates an im-age, and scores it. The first accepted image is returned. If no round is accepted within the budget, the highest-scoring judged image is returned as a fallback; it is not counted as accepted. Failed gener-ation or judging calls are recorded in the trajectory. 

A.2 Scoring and Acceptance 

Each dimension is scored on an integer scale from 1 to 5. For T2I requests, the base weights of Align-ment, Faithfulness, Quality, Aesthetic, and Physics are 0.40 , 0.20 , 0.25 , 0.20 , and 0.15 . Following Equation 1, the weight of a low-confidence dimen-sion is multiplied by 0.7, and the weights are then normalized over the active dimensions. I2I requests additionally activate a reference-consistency check. An image is accepted if its aggregate score is at least 4.5 and no veto applies. A Quality score of at most 3 vetoes acceptance; so does an Alignment score of at most 3, unless the requirement parser reports low confidence. 

A.3 Constraint-Aware Verification 

In this optional variant, an extractor splits the re-quest into atomic requirements, each linked to its source phrase in the request. The verifier labels ev-ery requirement pass , fail , or unknown and cites visible evidence; counts that are occluded or rela-tions that cannot be seen remain unknown . Early acceptance requires every requirement to pass. The repair prompt keeps the clauses of satisfied require-ments and rewrites only those for failed or un-known ones; satisfied requirements are rechecked after repair. 

A.4 Experience Admission 

A candidate needs at least two independent source trajectories. Each validation trial applies only the candidate’s action, with the seed and all upstream inputs shared between the two arms, and the im-age pair is scored blind. Calibration and holdout use separate splits. Calibration requires at least three trials, at least two positive differences ∆e(q),no negative difference, and a mean difference of at least 0.05 . Holdout requires at least two tri-als, at least one positive difference, no negative difference, and a nonnegative mean. A candidate becomes verified after calibration and active 

after holdout; any hard regression rejects it. 

B Datasets and Evaluation 

B.1 ImageReq-Bench 

ImageReq-Bench is built around hard cases in real-world text-to-image requests, i.e., requests whose requirements current generators often fail to satisfy jointly. Benchmark scope and the experience/test split are described in §4.1.1. During curation, we deduplicated candidate requests and removed or replaced identified personal information. Each re-quest is linked to its source records. All methods and judges are evaluated on the same fixed list of test case IDs. We consolidate the 12 content categories into four: Illustration (511 requests), Scene (213), In-fographic (144), and Design & Art (93). These categories are organizational labels rather than a validated difficulty taxonomy. Language or vision–language models were used to draft and refine a checklist for each request, re-moving unsupported requirements, merging dupli-cate items, and expressing the remaining require-ments as visually checkable statements. Checklists contain 2 to 18 items per request, and all meth-ods are scored against the same checklist version. The benchmark assesses whether multiple require-ments within a request are jointly satisfied and uses individual checks to identify unmet requirements, providing a common basis for evaluating image generation, targeted repair, and cross-task experi-ence reuse. The three MindBench I2I categories (Life, GU, and Math) comprise 150 requests.

D Judge Training 

D.1 Training Data Training requests. We assemble approximately 2,000 requests for judge training, combining 530 requests from the experience split of ImageReq-Bench with approximately 1,500 additional re-quests. The latter are constructed using WISE’s taxonomy and prompt design as guidance, together with MindBench’s knowledge- and reasoning-driven task categories (Niu et al., 2025; He et al., 2026b). WISE provides sampled coverage of world knowledge, while MindBench contains 50 evalu-ation examples per category. Although both offer useful evaluation resources, neither directly sup-plies the five-dimensional score-and-reason super-vision required by our judge. We therefore expand the request pool along their task categories and pair selected images from generation and repair trajec-tories with dimension-specific scores and evidence-grounded explanations. 

Inputs and targets. Each training input contains the request, the parsed requirements, permitted ev-idence, any task input image, and the generated image. The target consists of five lines, one per dimension in the order Alignment, Faithfulness, Quality, Physics, and Aesthetic; each line gives an integer score from 1 to 5 and a reason grounded in the image. Images that violate the request are kept, since their targets describe the violation. Training covers only scores and reasons. The confidence values used in Equation 1 are set to constants by an inference adapter when a trained judge is deployed.
