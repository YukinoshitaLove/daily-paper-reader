## ABSTRACT 

Many real-world signals are corrupted by localized, structured perturbations whose distribution is unknown: an intermittent background noise in a music recording, a watermark stamped on a photograph, a cast shadow. We study the task of recovering a clean signal x from an observation y containing such a localized out-of-distribution (OOD) corruption. We propose Support-guided OOD Removal with Diffusion, SORD , an iterative algorithm to recover x using only two ingredients: a binary map of the corruption support and a single diffusion model pretrained on clean signals. SORD does not require any paired data or any statistical model of the corruption. We evaluate SORD on natural images and human faces with spatially localized corruptions including handwritten digits, other images, watermarks, and shadows, as well as audio recordings of different musical instruments with temporally localized corruptions including other instruments and human speech. 

SORD is competitive with, and often outperforms, posterior-sampling inpainting given the same diffusion prior and the same mask, source-separation baselines that require a separate diffusion model for each source, and, on several benchmarks, supervised networks trained on paired clean and corrupted signals. The code is released here: https://github.com/voilalab/SORD. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Figure 1: SORD removes diverse localized OOD corruptions without prior knowledge of the corruption distribution. Examples span semantic image patches, watermarks, shadows, and localized audio interference. Rows show the corrupted observation (top), our reconstruction (middle), and the clean target (bottom). Localized corruptions arise in many restoration problems: a limited region of an image may be affected by structured noise, a watermark may cover part of an image, a cast shadow may alter a region, or an interfering sound may overlap a musical recording for a short time. These corruptions can be highly structured and semantically meaningful, rendering simple (e.g., Gaussian) noise models inadequate. At the same time, collecting paired examples or training a generative model for every possible corruption type can be impractical in the face of unexpected corruptions. Existing approaches for removing localized corruptions generally fall into three cate-gories. Supervised restoration methods learn a direct mapping from corrupted to clean sig-nals (Liu et al., 2021; Liang et al., 2021; Guo et al., 2023b); these methods can perform well on specific tasks, but require paired clean and corrupted training data and are then restricted to re-moving corruptions similar to those seen during training. Posterior-sampling approaches com-bine a pretrained generative prior over clean signals with a known measurement model or like-1arXiv:2610.33194v1 [cs.CV] 23 Oct 2026 Preprint lihood (Kawar et al., 2022; Chung et al., 2023). While these methods have achieved strong per-formance on many inverse problems (Lugmayr et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2023; Song et al., 2023), including inpainting a masked region, they generally require the corruption statistics to be known (Legin et al., 2023), often assuming relatively simple likelihood models such as Gaussian or Poisson noise (Chung et al., 2023) rather than corruptions with complex spatial, semantic, or temporal structure. Finally, source-separation approaches explicitly learn distributions for both the clean signal class and the corruption class, for example using separate generative models (Stevens et al., 2025; Mariani et al., 2024). These approaches can represent substantially richer cor-ruption types, but require training data or learned generative priors for the anticipated corruption classes. In many applications, these requirements can be restrictive or impractical. Paired clean and corrupted examples may be unavailable, and the corruption distribution may be too complex or unexpected to model accurately. We therefore consider a more weakly specified setting in which a strong generative prior is available for the clean signal class and the corruption is localized to a known support Ω; we do not assume access to any paired corruption data, parametric corruption distribution, or generative model of the corruption class. Our key idea is to replace statistical prior knowledge of the corruption distribution with structural prior knowledge of where or when the corruption occurs. In many applications, the corruption is localized: it affects only a spatial subset of an image, as with watermarks, shadows, or localized artifacts, or only a temporal segment of an audio signal, as when an interfering sound briefly overlaps with a recording. The corruption within this region may have complex and unknown structure, but identifying its support is often substantially easier than modeling its distribution. A broad class of anomaly-detection and segmentation methods have already been proposed to estimate the support of such localized corrup-tions (Defard et al., 2021; Roth et al., 2022; Wyatt et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2023; Kheirandish et al., 2026) .Rather than attempting to model the corruption distribution, we treat localized corruptions as out-of-distribution (OOD) relative to a generative prior over clean signals. The observations outside the corrupted region Ω are regarded as clean and are preserved, while the region inside Ω is reconstructed using the clean-signal generative prior. Unlike standard inpainting, however, the corrupted observa-tions inside Ω may still carry information about the underlying clean signal. Our algorithm, SORD ,therefore alternates between explaining the observed corrupted region and reconstructing a signal that is consistent with both the learned clean-data distribution and the trusted observations outside Ω.At each iteration, SORD uses its estimated uncertainty to adapt its effective step size and evaluate an adaptive stopping criterion. Empirically, we find that knowledge of the clean-signal prior and corruption support alone can be sufficient to match or outperform approaches that rely on paired training data or explicit corruption models. We evaluate SORD across localized structured corruptions in both images and audio, as shown in Figure 1. For images, we consider both controlled additive corruptions to face images as well as specific applications in watermark removal and shadow removal in which the corruptions are not purely additive. For audio, we consider temporally localized additive audio interference in which a musical recording of one instrument is corrupted by another instrument or by human speech. Across these settings, we compare SORD with posterior-sampling (inpainting) methods using the same clean-signal prior and corruption support, supervised restoration models trained for specific corruption types, and multi-source diffusion models that explicitly model possible interfering sources. The results show that support information can substantially reduce the need for an explicit corruption model, while corruption-specific supervision remains advantageous when the test corruption is well represented by its training distribution. To summarize, our main contributions are: 1. We introduce SORD , an iterative algorithm for removing localized, highly structured OOD corruptions without requiring prior knowledge, training examples, or a generative model of the corruption. Instead, our method requires only a mask specifying the corruption support and a diffusion model trained on the clean signal distribution. 2. We demonstrate the generality of our approach across both image and audio restoration tasks, where corruptions are localized spatially or temporally. Across a diverse range of structured corruption types, SORD is competitive with approaches with substantially stronger modeling or training requirements, including supervised methods that require paired clean and corrupted data and source-separation methods that require an additional generative model trained on the corruption class. 2Preprint 

## 2 RELATED WORK 

Our work lies at the intersection of diffusion-based inverse problems, corruption-specific restoration, source separation, and localized anomaly detection. 

Diffusion priors for inverse problems. Diffusion models have been widely used as learned priors for inverse problems (Song et al., 2023; Chung et al., 2023; Zhu et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2025), in-cluding inpainting a masked region (Lugmayr et al., 2022; Kawar et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2023). These methods typically assume a specified observation or measurement model together with a known or modeled noise distribution such as Gaussian noise, Poisson noise, and other non-Gaussian likelihoods (Chung et al., 2023; Legin et al., 2023). In contrast, localized structured corruptions need not follow a known parametric noise model or likelihood, motivating restoration under support information alone. 

Corruption-specific image restoration. A complementary line of work trains supervised or gener-ative restoration models for a particular family of corruptions. For example, specialized methods have been proposed for image watermark removal (Cun & Pun, 2021; Liu et al., 2021; Liang et al., 2021) and shadow removal (Guo et al., 2023b;a), using either classical supervised learning (Cun & Pun, 2021; Liu et al., 2021; Liang et al., 2021), conditional diffusion (Guo et al., 2023b) or an attention-based model (Guo et al., 2023a). However, these approaches all require paired clean and corrupted training images and are specialized to specific types of corruption, whereas we seek a single general-purpose algorithm that can remove any structured localized corruption without retraining for each corruption class. 

Generative priors for source separation. A broad family of methods focuses on source separation, treating a single observation as a mixture of multiple constituent sources and using learned generative priors to recover the individual components. For images, Stevens et al. (2025) model an observation as the combination of a clean image and structured noise, using separate diffusion priors for the signal and noise distributions and jointly inferring the two components. For audio, Multi-Source Diffusion Models (MSDM) (Mariani et al., 2024) learns the joint distribution of a fixed set of musical sources, such as different instruments, and uses the resulting model for both generation and source separation. Related diffusion-based approaches learn source distributions for speech (Scheibler et al., 2023) or separately trained priors for constituent communication signals (Jayashankar et al., 2023). These approaches rely on training data that represent the possible constituent source and corruption classes. In contrast, we assume a generative prior only for the desired clean signal; the localized corruption may come from an unknown class, may vary across observations, and need not be anticipated during training. 

Localized anomaly and OOD detection. Localized anomaly and out-of-distribution (OOD) detection methods aim to identify spatial regions that deviate from the distribution of inlier data. A broad range of methods estimate such localized supports using feature-space de-viations (Defard et al., 2021; Roth et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2023) or learned anomaly representa-tions (Zavrtanik et al., 2021). More recently, diffusion models have been used as expressive repre-sentations of the inlier distribution, enabling anomalies to be localized from discrepancies produced during denoising or reconstruction (Wyatt et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2023; He et al., 2024). These works demonstrate that the support of an anomalous or out-of-distribution component can often be estimated without explicitly modeling its distribution; our work assumes access to this corruption support and uses it towards recovering the underlying clean signal. 

## 3 METHOD 

We propose SORD , a diffusion-based method for removing localized structured corruptions without modeling their distribution. We assume access only to the support Ω identifying where or when the corruption occurs, and a diffusion prior pretrained on clean signals. A natural strategy under these assumptions is diffusion inpainting: discard the measurements inside Ω and generate the missing content conditioned on the uncorrupted observations outside Ω. However, such a strategy also discards potentially useful information. In our setting, the values inside Ω are not missing; rather, they contain a superposition of the clean signal and an unknown structured corruption. Our key insight is that these measurements can be progressively reused to improve an initial inpainting estimate.

SORD terminates adaptively once the uncertainty estimate σ⋆ stops decreasing much. When this condition is satisfied, independent clean-signal hypotheses agree sufficiently within the corrupted support, and SORD returns their mean. SORD continues its alternating updates until either this condition is satisfied or it reaches a maximum of M iterations. 

## 4 EXPERIMENTS 

We evaluate SORD on localized structured corruption removal tasks in the image and audio domains. For images, we consider additive localized structured corruptions, visible-watermark removal, and shadow removal; for audio, we consider temporally localized additive interference removal. We report peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR) over the corrupted support Ω for image experiments, since full-image metrics can be dominated by unchanged pixels outside the corrupted region, and scale-invariant signal-to-distortion ratio improvement (SI-SDRi) within the interference window for audio experiments. Representative results are shown in Figure 2. 5Preprint 4.1 ADDITIVE LOCALIZED STRUCTURED IMAGE CORRUPTIONS 

We first consider additive localized corruptions on CelebA images (Liu et al., 2015), constructed by adding natural-image patches (van der Walt et al., 2014), MNIST digits (LeCun et al., 1998), and CIFAR-10 patches (Krizhevsky, 2009). These experiments test the target setting of this work: the corruption is additive, localized, and highly structured, while the clean diffusion prior is trained only on CelebA. Visual and quantitative results are reported in Figure 2 and Table 1, respectively. We compare against several DPS (Chung et al., 2023) baselines using the same CelebA clean prior. 

DPS-Inpaint treats the corrupted support as missing and inpaints it by conditioning the sampling process on the observations outside Ω. We additionally evaluate DPS under a Gaussian likelihood assumption within Ω, either using the oracle corruption mean μ or fixing μ = 0 , since in practice the corruption mean is inaccessible. The limited performance of these variants suggests that the evaluated Gaussian likelihood approximations are inadequate to remove highly structured localized corruptions. We also compare against SGM (Stevens et al., 2025), which uses separate diffusion models for the CelebA image distribution and each corruption distribution, as well as a variant of SGM which we endow with additional knowledge of the corruption support Ω. SORD outperforms both variants without requiring a learned corruption-specific prior. Further experimental details are provided in Section A.1. 4.2 ADDITIVE LOCALIZED AUDIO INTERFERENCE 

To evaluate whether the same support-guided inference principle extends beyond images to localized audio interference, we use the Slakh2100 (Manilow et al., 2019) dataset of instrumental music to construct additive mixtures in which an interfering source is present only within a known temporal window Ω. We compare SORD with Multi-Source Diffusion Models (MSDM) (Mariani et al., 2024). MSDM performs source separation using multiple source-specific diffusion priors, whereas SORD 

uses only a diffusion prior trained on the target source together with the temporal support Ω of the interference. The audio mixture is constructed as 

y = x + g · (MΩ ⊙ s) , (7) where x is the target source and s is the interfering source. We adjust g to control the interference-window Signal to Interference Ratio, SIR, levels between 0 and 5 dB, and consider the default Slakh mixing level g = 1 without SIR-based gain normalization. We consider both in-vocabulary (IV) and out-of-vocabulary (OOV) interfering sources, relative to the MSDM baseline. For a given target source among the MSDM-modeled sources {piano, guitar, bass, drums }, the IV set consists of the other three MSDM-modeled sources, while the OOV set consists of the remaining eight Slakh instrument classes for which MSDM has no corresponding diffusion model. We additionally consider human speech interference both as an OOV source for the original MSDM model and as an IV source for an augmented version of MSDM endowed with a speech diffusion model. Quantitative results are presented in Table 1, with per-source results in Table 9 and representative spectrogram visualizations in Figure 3 in the appendix. We find that MSDM can outperform SORD for certain target instruments and when the interferer is a modeled source (IV), but that SORD is generally more robust to unexpected (OOV) interferer types as it does not rely on prior knowledge of the corruption class. 4.3 WATERMARK REMOVAL 

We next consider visible watermarks, which provide a realistic localized structured corruption containing text or logos with varying color, shape, scale, and transparency. We use watermark examples from the Colored Large-scale Watermark Dataset (CLWD) (Liu et al., 2021). A watermarked observation follows the alpha-compositing model 

y = (1 − α) ⊙ x + α ⊙ w, (8) where x is the clean image, w is the watermark appearance, and α is its opacity map, which may vary at the pixel level. We define the corruption support as all pixels with nonzero watermark opacity.

This deliberately introduces model mismatch, since the true watermark opacity varies spatially across the logo. We evaluate watermark removal both on the CLWD dataset and on synthetically watermarked images from FFHQ (Karras et al., 2019) and Imagenette, a ten-class subset of ImageNet (Deng et al., 2009). For each dataset, we use the PatchWiper model pretrained on the large-scale Pixabay Real-world Watermark Dataset (PRWD) (Mo et al., 2025). SLBR is trained on paired watermarked and clean images from each dataset, and SORD uses a diffusion model trained only on the clean image datasets without any prior knowledge of watermarks. Quantitative results are reported in Table 2, with representative examples included in Figure 2. In this setting where the watermarking model (Equation (8)) deviates from the equal-weight additive corruption model assumed by SORD , SORD is competitive with, but does not always outperform, these domain-specific baselines trained on paired clean and corrupted data. 4.4 SHADOW REMOVAL 

Shadows constitute a qualitatively different corruption from the additive perturbations considered above, and thus a further model-mismatch stress test of SORD . We model a shadowed image as a multiplicative corruption: 

y = x ⊙ s, (9) where x is the clean image and s is a spatially varying attenuation field with s < 1 inside the shadow region Ω and s = 1 on Ωc. To apply its additive corruption-removal procedure, SORD applies its measurement-consistency updates in the log-intensity domain, log y = log x + log s, but still applies its pretrained clean-image diffusion model in the image domain (to estimates of x and s, not their pixel-wise logarithms). We evaluate shadow removal on the Image Shadow Triplets Dataset (ISTD) (Wang et al., 2018), which provides shadowed images, corresponding shadow-free targets, and binary shadow masks. ## 5 DISCUSSION 

We introduced SORD for removing localized structured corruptions using only a pretrained clean-signal diffusion prior and knowledge of the corruption support, without requiring paired clean and corrupted training data or a separately learned model of the corruption distribution. Across image and audio tasks, this support-guided strategy consistently improves over simply discarding the corrupted measurements, as is common in inpainting, and achieves competitive performance against several specialized methods that use corruption-specific training data or priors. Our experiments also reveal limitations arising from the absence of a corruption-specific prior and the reliance on known corruption support. When the corruption class is known and well represented by training data, methods that explicitly model that class can retain an advantage; conversely, SORD 

is particularly useful when the corruption is unexpected or otherwise difficult to model. SORD also depends on a sufficiently accurate corruption support: overestimating the support tends to degrade performance gracefully, whereas leaving corrupted pixels outside the support can be substantially more harmful because those observations are treated as trustworthy. Future work may focus on jointly estimating the corruption support alongside corruption removal, developing more principled uncertainty-guided refinement and stopping criteria, and extending the framework to broader non-additive or partially specified corruption models. 9Preprint

A.1 EXPERIMENT DETAILS 

Localized Structured Corruptions: All three benchmarks use the same 100 CelebA test images at 64 × 64 resolution. The natural-image corruptions are 32 × 32 pixel patches and CIFAR-10 corruptions are patches with randomly chosen heights and widths between 16 and 48 pixels.. The corruptions follow the additive model y = x + MΩ ⊙ n, where x is the clean image and n is the spatially localized corruption. For SORD , we use K = 32 diffusion samples and a maximum of 

M = 6 refinement iterations. We use a noise schedule σm that starts at 0.4 and linearly decreases to 

0.1.We compare against DPS (Chung et al., 2023) baselines using the same CelebA clean-image diffusion prior. DPS-Inpaint treats the corrupted support as missing and reconstructs it by conditioning on the observations outside Ω. We additionally evaluate DPS under a Gaussian likelihood corruption model assumption within Ω, either using the oracle corruption mean μ = μn or fixing μ = 0 . These variants test whether an explicit Gaussian noise model is sufficient to remove localized structured corruptions (it usually is not sufficient). We also compare with the two-prior SGM of Stevens et al. (2025), which jointly estimates the clean image and corruption using separate diffusion models; CelebA images with MNIST corruption is a main structured-noise setting in that work. For natural image-patch corruptions, we trained the SGM corruption model using synthetic training examples generated by placing randomly selected image patches of size 26 × 26 to 38 × 38 on a 64 × 64 black background, separately for each type of image patch corruption. For the masked SGM variant, we enforce the known support by projecting all clean-image estimates at intermediate timesteps to match y on Ωc.

Localized Audio Interference: Each example is a 10 second clip sampled at 22 .05 kHz, and contains a single target source (piano, in Table 1) together with one interfering source. The interferer is restricted to a single contiguous window covering half of the clip, and this window is provided to all methods. We also evaluate the same three DPS configurations used for localized image corruptions. DPS uses only the target-source prior and the exact temporal window, with 250 Karras–Euler reverse steps and y +σmax ϵ initialization. DPS-Inpaint discards the measurements in the interference window, while the remaining variants use an in-window Gaussian noise assumption with either oracle μ = μn

or default μ = 0 . For SORD , we use the exact interference window and K = 64 diffusion samples for the main experiments. 

Watermark Removal: CLWD is a paired synthetic benchmark containing 60 , 000 training images generated from 160 colored watermark designs and 10 , 000 test images generated from 40 addi-tional watermark designs. The dataset provides clean background images and their corresponding watermarked observations, allowing the watermark support to be determined directly. We consider three watermark-removal benchmarks. For FFHQ, we composite a subset of 12 CLWD train-split logos onto 25 held-out FFHQ faces at resolution 256 × 256 ; for Imagenette, we apply the same 12 logos to 25 Imagenette images at resolution 256 × 256 . In both cases, the watermark support occupies approximately 3% of the image. For CLWD, we use its test split, containing 40 test-split logos and 99 test images at resolution 64 × 64 , with an average corruption support of approximately 

14% of the image. Composites use the per-pixel watermark opacity specified by the CLWD dataset. The clean image priors are off-the-shelf ADM (Dhariwal & Nichol, 2021) checkpoints for FFHQ and ImageNet-256 (for Imagenette). For CLWD, we use an EDM2 (Karras et al., 2024) ImageNet-64 model fine-tuned only on the clean images from the CLWD training split. For SLBR, we train a domain-matched SLBR model from scratch on paired data from the corresponding training domain: paired FFHQ examples for FFHQ, and paired Imagenette examples for Imagenette. Training uses all of the CLWD train/test watermarks except for the 12 watermarks which we used for testing for FFHQ and Imagenette. We have followed the published training code for reproducing results. PatchWiper uses the published PRWD (Mo et al., 2025) checkpoint and predicts its own watermark support. All DPS variants use the same clean prior and exact corruption support as SORD . DPS-Inpaint discards the observations within Ω, while the two likelihood-based variants use a per-image Gaussian noise assumption with mean set either to the oracle mean of the true corruption within Ω, μ = μn, or to zero as a standard default.
