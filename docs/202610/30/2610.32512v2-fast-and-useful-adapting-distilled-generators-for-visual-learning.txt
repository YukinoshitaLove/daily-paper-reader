# Abstract 

Distilled text-to-image models generate images quickly, but classifiers trained on those images can perform substantially worse than classifiers trained on images from their slower base models. We adapt released distilled generators to pro-duce better training data while retaining fast generation. We fit a low-rank update to the student’s first prediction so that it reaches an intermediate state produced by its unguided base, then disable the update and let the unchanged student finish generation. The base supplies training targets and is absent at inference. Guided targets and shorter jumps give classifiers lower accuracy in our ablations, showing why the state used for supervision matters. Across three generator families, the adapted images train better classifiers than im-ages from the released students. With class-name templates at full ImageNet-1k scale, ResNet-50 top-1 rises from 8.4% to 34.2% for FLUX.2-klein and from 12.7% to 32.1% for SD-Turbo, using two and three denoiser evaluations per im-age, respectively. Fixed adapters also improve results on additional classes, fine-grained datasets, and other learner architectures and training recipes. Template-based adapta-tion remains useful against richer-prompt alternatives, while the benefit of combining adaptation with LLM generation prompts depends on the generator. Adapting a single gener-ation step makes fast generators substantially more useful for training visual models. 

# 1. Introduction 

Synthetic images can expand training sets for computer vi-sion without requiring another round of image collection and annotation. Text prompts provide control over the requested classes and allow datasets to be generated at different scales, supporting both learning from synthetic images alone and supplementing limited real data [2, 16, 32]. Their usefulness depends on what visual models learn from them: a generated dataset must help a classifier recognize real images. Diffusion-based text-to-image models make this approach flexible, but generating every image through many denois-ing steps becomes costly at dataset scale. Distillation com-presses generation into a few predictions, making large syn-thetic datasets faster to produce [33, 40]. That acceleration can come with a loss in the usefulness of the generated im-ages. Adamkiewicz et al . [1] show that classifiers trained on images from released distilled generators can perform worse than classifiers trained on images from their full-step coun-terparts. We aim to retain the speed advantage while making the released student’s images more useful for learning. We study this problem by comparing changes to the generator while holding the classifier training procedure fixed. Lower guidance and richer prompts already improve synthetic-data learning [10, 32], and replacing a distilled model’s first steps with its base can restore sample diver-sity [14]. We measure how these choices affect classification on real images and use the resulting comparisons to con-1arXiv:2610.32512v2 [cs.CV] 29 Oct 2026 struct supervision for a fast student. Additional classifier architectures and training recipes test whether the improve-ment extends beyond the primary learner. Our approach trains a low-rank update to the released student’s first prediction. From initial noise, this predic-tion learns to reach an intermediate state produced by the unguided base. We then disable the update and let the un-changed student finish generation. The base supplies targets during preparation and is absent at inference; the adapted samplers use two denoiser evaluations for FLUX.2-klein and three for SD-Turbo. First-step teacher regression and post-hoc diversity adaptation have precedents [12, 25, 39]. At full ImageNet-1k scale with template prompts, adapta-tion improves classifier accuracy over the previously fixed base-first samplers by +3.5 points on klein and +5.4 on SD-Turbo (Tab. 2). Matched ablations establish how tar-get guidance, jump endpoint, and update placement affect downstream classifier accuracy; comparisons with released models, base-first sampling, and available diversity methods test the resulting adaptation procedure. We evaluate three generator families across datasets, learners, and prompt regimes, and measure preparation and generation costs. Our contributions are: 1. Improving released generators for visual learning. We adapt three distilled generator families and compare them with the released students and base-first hybrids by train-ing classifiers under the same procedure. A public SDA comparison and further adaptation of DP-DMD’s released student test the approach alongside existing diversity methods (Sec. 3). 2. Controlled evidence for the adaptation targets. Chang-ing guidance and replacing early student steps motivate the correction; matched target-guidance, endpoint, and update-placement ablations establish which choices im-prove classifier accuracy (Sec. 4.4 and Tab. 3). 3. Scale and transfer with fixed adapters. Adapters se-lected during development improve classifier accuracy at full ImageNet scale and transfer unchanged to fine-grained datasets. Comparisons across prompts, learners, and real-data availability establish the tested regimes in which adaptation helps (Tabs. 2 and 4 to 6). We will release code to reproduce all experiments and to train and apply the adapter. 

# 2. Related Work 

Synthetic data for visual learning. Text-to-image models can supply images for training classifiers from scratch and supplementing limited real data [2, 16, 32]. The resulting accuracy depends on the generator, dataset size, prompts, and learning procedure. Lower guidance and richer descriptions improve supervised learning from synthetic images [10, 32], while StableRep uses multiple images generated from the same caption as positives for self-supervised representation learning [37]. These approaches establish useful ways to generate and learn from synthetic data. Adamkiewicz et al . [1] identify a complementary problem: images from newer and distilled generators can train weaker classifiers than images from older models. We address this problem by adapting released distilled generators and comparing their images under matched classifier training procedures, with additional learners testing the scope of the improvement. 

Diffusion acceleration and distillation. Latent diffusion and rectified-flow models support text-conditioned genera-tion through iterative sampling [9, 30]. Distillation reduces the number of model evaluations through objectives such as adversarial training, distribution matching, and consis-tency learning [26, 33, 40, 41]. Although these methods enable fast generation, maintaining visual quality does not guarantee that the images remain equally useful for training classifiers [1]. We build on their released students, adapting a single prediction and retaining the student’s unchanged completion steps. This lets us test improvements to synthetic training data without training another distilled generator from its base. 

Diversity preservation and adaptation. Guidance sched-ules and condition annealing adjust the trade-off between fidelity and diversity [21, 31]. Gandikota and Bau [14] re-store diversity by using base-model predictions for the initial steps of a distilled sampler. We evaluate this base-first pro-cedure as an alternative that requires no adapter training but uses the base at inference. Training-based methods also dis-tinguish early structural variation from later refinement. DP-DMD supervises the first student step with a teacher-derived target and trains the remaining steps through distribution matching [39]; CrossDistill assigns trajectory-preserving and distribution-matching objectives to different noise in-tervals in video generation [25]. CrossDistill’s direct-flow variant also regresses the first prediction toward an interme-diate teacher state; our adaptation applies this supervision to a released image student while leaving its completion steps unchanged. Other methods likewise modify distillation ob-jectives to preserve diversity [11, 19, 22, 23]. SDA directly adapts released few-step models with teacher-guided direc-tional alignment, and its Krea-2 release activates the adapter only during early sampling steps [12, 13]. We study how unguided teacher targets and first-prediction adaptation affect classifier accuracy when the released student’s completion steps remain fixed (Tab. S8). 23. Methodology 

## 3.1. Problem and approach 

We adapt a released distilled generator so that classifiers trained on its images perform better on real images. The adaptation uses the corresponding full-step base as a teacher and preserves the released student for the remaining genera-tion steps. We train a low-rank update to the first prediction to approximate an intermediate teacher state, then disable the update and let the student finish. The teacher is needed to prepare training targets but is absent at inference (Fig. 2). 

## 3.2. Constructing the teacher target 

We compare generated datasets through the accuracy of clas-sifiers trained on them, holding prompts, image seeds, and classifier training fixed within each comparison. Reducing base-model guidance improves this accuracy in the tested configurations, motivating targets that retain the base’s text-conditioned prediction without classifier-free guidance am-plification. We call this setting unguided , corresponding to a standard guidance weight of one. The target still depends on the text prompt. For each prompt and initial noise sample, we run the unguided base on a fine sampling grid and save its latent state at a selected noise level. This gives paired supervision: the released student receives the same noise and prompt and learns to predict a jump to that state. We compare guided and unguided teacher targets in the experiments to test this choice directly (Tab. 3). 

## 3.3. Correcting the early generation steps 

The target must also specify how much of the teacher trajec-tory the adapted prediction should replace. Our guidance-window comparisons show that strong guidance during early steps accounts for much of its observed cost to classifier accuracy. Replacing early student predictions with the un-guided base also improves accuracy more than replacing later predictions (Sec. 4.4). These results motivate learning the teacher’s early computation in one jump and reusing the student afterward. We choose the jump endpoint from the released student’s noise levels, allowing the unchanged student to continue on its existing schedule. Endpoint selection uses the ImageNet-100 and 200-class development protocols; a small ImageNet-1k pilot also contributed to method development. The se-lected targets and resulting evaluation counts appear in Tab. 1. The candidate endpoints and retention criteria are specified in Sec. A. Later evaluations use previously fixed adapters and samplers: klein and SD-Turbo at full ImageNet scale, and all four generator families on fine-grained datasets.

# 4. Experiments 

## 4.1. Experimental setup 

Our primary comparison trains a ResNet-50 [15] from scratch on 500 generated images per ImageNet-100 class (CMC subset [36]) and evaluates top-1 accuracy on the corre-sponding real validation images. Eight photo templates sup-ply class-name prompts, with matching prompts and image seeds across samplers. Classifiers use the same 100-epoch training procedure within each comparison. We evaluate FLUX.2-klein, SD-Turbo, and Z-Image-Turbo against their released students, unguided bases, and base-first samplers, alongside the public SDA adapter and DP-DMD’s released SD3.5-Medium student. Comparisons generate at 512 pixels; a separate DP-DMD check uses its documented 1024-pixel resolution with the unchanged adapter. Classifier hyperpa-rameters, baseline settings, and the broader generator survey appear in Sec. A. ImageNet-100 and the 200-class protocol provide devel-opment results, and the small ImageNet-1k pilot also in-formed method development. Full-scale ImageNet-1k [8] and Food-101, Cars, and Pets evaluations [7, 20, 27] use previously fixed adapters and samplers: klein and SD-Turbo at full ImageNet scale, and all four generator families on fine-grained datasets. The 200-class results include classes absent from adapter training but were also inspected during development (Tab. S1). Unless stated otherwise, tables and error bars report means and standard deviations over three classifier seeds. Primary ImageNet-100 adapter results pool three adapter-training runs with three classifier seeds each; other adapter results use one training run, with exceptions identified in their tables. Accuracy gaps compare mean per-class accura-cies, with paired 95% class-bootstrap intervals after averag-ing classifier runs, conditional on the generated datasets and fitted checkpoints. Four headline sets are regenerated inde-pendently; the ImageNet-100 LLM comparisons use three generated datasets with three classifier seeds each. Separate dataset-mean and classifier SDs appear in Tab. S15.

## 4.4. Evidence for the design choices 

Changing guidance only during early or late generation steps shows that the early part accounts for much of the observed accuracy loss. Likewise, replacing early student steps with the unguided base improves accuracy more than replacing later steps. Detailed guidance-window and base/student comparisons support the early-step design (Fig. S1). The adapter ablations then compare the teacher targets directly (Tab. 3). With the same finer teacher grid on klein, an unguided target gives 43.2%, while a guidance-four target gives 20.1%. SD-Turbo and Z-Image show the same direc-tion of change (Sec. B.2). Stopping the adapted jump at the original first endpoint also underperforms the selected target on klein and SD-Turbo, as do the tested deeper endpoints. Keeping the same first-step-trained adapter active during the remaining sampling steps lowers accuracy. This compari-son tests inference-time activation; it does not evaluate an adapter trained across timesteps. Increasing rank or training every weight gives comparatively small changes. Together, these comparisons support an unguided intermediate target and an update used only for the initial prediction.

These adapter comparisons retain their directions on the 900 classes outside fitting (Tab. S17). Klein’s unguided 20-step base also falls 2.4 points below its released student, whereas unguided SD 2.1 exceeds SD-Turbo by 1.5 points (Tab. S17). With limited real training data, adding adapted images improves real-only accuracy in the tested mixtures. With the full real ImageNet-1k training set, adding adapted images improves Sketch and R accuracy while slightly lowering in-distribution accuracy. The full results in the supplement delimit the settings in which synthetic augmentation helps (Tab. S18 and Sec. E.4). 

# 5. Discussion 

The teacher target shapes downstream learning. Learn-ing a jump to the selected unguided state gives higher classi-fier accuracy than matching a guided target or retaining the original first endpoint (Tab. 3). Further adapting DP-DMD’s released student also improves classifier accuracy, although a substantial gap to its unguided base remains (Sec. C.4). 7The evaluated comparisons do not isolate the source of that remaining gap. 

The useful choice depends on the generator and prompts. 

Template-based adaptation improves on released and base-first samplers on ImageNet-100 and on their LLM-prompt alternatives at ImageNet-1k with 100 images per class (Fig. 3 and Tab. S17). For klein at this dataset size, we therefore rec-ommend the original adapter with template generation; SD-Turbo benefits from adaptation with either prompt source. Released klein with LLM prompts avoids adapter prepara-tion, and base-first improves its accuracy when the base can be loaded. Captions derived from real images retain gains for both models but require access to those images (Tab. S14). Dataset also matters: base-first exceeds klein’s adapter on Cars (Tab. 4). 

Fast generation and low total cost are separate bene-fits. Once fitted, the adapter improves classifier accuracy at comparable or lower generation time than the released student, without the base at inference (Fig. 4). Preparation adds an initial cost: at 50,000 images, base-first is cheaper overall in the measured klein and SD-Turbo template set-tings, while adaptation gives higher accuracy (Tab. S2). At full ImageNet scale, estimated preparation plus generation costs 41.4 H100-hours for klein’s adapter versus 54.5 for base-first. SD-Turbo’s costs are approximately equal, while adaptation gives higher classifier accuracy in both families (Tabs. 2 and S2). Adaptation is useful when this gain justifies preparation, the adapter can be reused, or inference requires only the student. The unguided base remains an option when its higher accuracy justifies slower sampling. 

Set metrics help diagnose generators. Real-data recall and CLIP Fr´ echet distance track classifier accuracy across the studied configurations. Family-weighting and held-out-family analyses support these set-level diagnostics, with varying predictive performance across families (Tabs. S19 to S21). Shared prompts, seeds, and neighboring settings limit independence. The associations establish neither causality nor reliable classwise prediction; training classi-fiers remains the direct evaluation of usefulness. 

# 6. Limitations & Future Work 

Our method requires a compatible base model to construct targets and an intermediate state that the released student can complete. We select the endpoint empirically from the student’s schedule using downstream classifier results. We do not establish a rule that selects a useful endpoint for a new generator without this evaluation. Developing such a rule, or learning targets that better match the frozen student continuation, could reduce the cost of applying the method to additional generators. The evaluation also has a development boundary. ImageNet-100, the 200-class protocol, and the small ImageNet-1k pilot informed method choices; they are not independent final tests. Full-scale ImageNet-1k and fine-grained evaluations use previously fixed adapters and sam-plers, but classes absent from adapter fitting can still have appeared during development (Tab. S1). Most reported vari-ation concerns classifier training, while adapter-training vari-ation and independent dataset regeneration are measured for subsets of the comparisons. Repeating more compar-isons with independently generated datasets would better characterize their uncertainty. We evaluate classification and feature transfer; detection and segmentation remain untested. Prompt-specific fitting is evaluated with one training run per adapter. The rich-prompt ImageNet-1k evaluation uses 100 images per class and one generated dataset per sampler, so it does not establish the outcome at the full synthetic dataset size. Understanding how prompt distributions interact with the adapted predic-tion and frozen completion steps could improve transfer across prompt regimes. Additional tasks require accuracy and preparation-cost evaluation. Adaptation also inherits the biases and coverage limitations of the released genera-tors. Aggregate classification gains do not establish balanced performance across demographic groups or rare visual con-ditions, which require separate evaluation. 

# 7. Conclusion 

We adapt released distilled generators to produce images that train more accurate visual classifiers. A low-rank update teaches the initial prediction to reach an unguided interme-diate base-model state; the unchanged student then com-pletes generation without the base at inference. Controlled comparisons establish the importance of target guidance, endpoint, and update placement. The improvements extend across three generator families, with fixed klein and SD-Turbo adapters also benefiting full-scale ImageNet training and fine-grained recognition. These results provide a sim-ple way to improve training data from released generators while retaining fast generation. Prompt choice, preparation cost, and inference requirements determine the most useful configuration. 8Supplementary material

## A.1. Datasets, classifiers, and repetition 

Classifier training. The default learner is a ResNet-50 [15] trained from scratch for 100 epochs. We use SGD with Nesterov momentum 0.9, learning rate 0.2 at batch size 256, five warmup epochs followed by cosine decay, weight decay 10 −4, label smoothing 0.1, and bf16 autocast. Training uses random resized crops to 224 pixels and horizontal flips; evaluation resizes to 256 and center-crops to 224. The ViT-S/16 uses AdamW with learning rate 5 · 10 −4, weight decay 0.05, ten warmup epochs, and RandAugment (2, 9). 

Alternative classifier recipe. The recipe of [1], used in Tabs. 5 and S13, uses Adam at 10 −3, batch size 1,024, 80 epochs, CutMix, gradient clipping, and the published augmentations. It selects the checkpoint with lowest loss on a held-out tenth of the generated images. Our primary comparisons use the fixed 100-epoch schedule above. 

Repetitions and reporting. Unless stated otherwise, each generated set is evaluated with three classifier seeds, and tables report their mean and standard deviation. ImageNet-100 results for the three primary adapters and the first-level DP-DMD adapter pool three adapter-training seeds with three classifier seeds each. Transfer evaluations use the first trained adapter. Ablations use one adapter-training seed, except SD-Turbo rank 128, which uses three. Data-scaling evaluations use two classifier seeds, with the exception identified in the scaling caption. We assess generation variability by independently regenerating four headline sets. Top-1 averages test images. Gaps use mean per-class accuracy and paired class-bootstrap intervals after averaging classifier runs. Mean-class and image-averaged accuracy can differ on imbalanced test splits. The intervals condition on the observed datasets and fitted adapters; classifier and adapter runs do not count as independent dataset regenerations. 9Evaluation splits and real-data references. ImageNet classifiers are evaluated on the real validation images of their classes, 50 per class. The default real-data reference draws 500 images per class from the training split. Fine-grained synthetic sets also contain 500 images per class, but evaluation uses each dataset’s test split. Their real-data references use 500 real images per class for Food-101, all 8,144 training images for Cars, and all 3,680 for Pets. These references train for the same number of optimization steps as the generated sets. For representation transfer, we freeze the synthetic-trained ResNet-50, standardize its features, and select the linear probe’s L2 penalty on a held-out tenth of the target training split. Target images train only the probe. 

## A.2. Sampler development and adapter selection 

Development data. Sampler selection and reported ImageNet-100 accuracy use the same 5,000 real validation images. Table S1 distinguishes the development protocols from the later fixed-setting evaluations. In particular, the 200-class protocol and the ImageNet-1k scale pilot informed method development; classes absent from adapter fitting are not necessarily untouched during development. 

Endpoints and rank. For klein, we evaluate target noise levels 0.958, 0.884, 0.7175, and 0.511; for SD-Turbo, timesteps 749, 499, and 249. Subsequent endpoint ablations retain the klein and SD-Turbo choices: no alternative clears the specified replacement criterion of more than 1.5 ImageNet-100 points at no more denoiser evaluations. The conditional 200-class check for such a replacement was therefore not triggered. Rank 32 is retained when within one point of the best rank-8, rank-128, or full-update alternative. For Z-Image, the initial comparison selects 0.857 over 0.913; later targets 0.7895 and 0.7059 raise accuracy by less than one point and reduce its margin over the corresponding base-first sampler, so the reported endpoint is retained. Base guidance and hand-off settings use the tested candidate with highest ImageNet-100 top-1 within the stated role; their candidate sets are specified in the machine-readable manifest. Adapter optimization uses a disjoint 5% of noise/target pairs (at least 64) to measure state-regression error, with a fixed split seed of zero. This held-out error selects between the two full-update learning rates; standard low-rank adapters use the final 3,000-step checkpoint. This pair split is distinct from the downstream real-image validation set used to develop the sampler. The alternative classifier recipe selects its checkpoint on held-out generated images; our default classifier uses the final fixed-epoch model. 

## A.3. Feature metrics and cost measurement 

Feature metrics. DINOv3 features use the ViT-L/16 pretrained on LVD-1689M: the 1,024-dimensional final normalized class token, after bilinear resizing of the short side to 256 and center-cropping to 224 with ImageNet channel normalization. CLIP uses OpenAI ViT-L/14 projected image embeddings (768 dimensions), bicubic resize/crop to 224 and CLIP channel normalization. We L2-normalize both feature types. Precision, recall, density, and coverage use Euclidean distances within each class, with the fifth-nearest-neighbor radius excluding self-distances ( k = 5 ); metrics average over 100 classes with 500 real training and 500 generated images each. Recall is the fraction of real features inside the union of generated-feature balls. Fr´ echet distances use whole-set means and unbiased covariances. FID uses 2,048-dimensional Inception-v3 pool3 features with TensorFlow-FID weights, bicubic resize/crop to 299, and the same 50,000 real training images as reference. Preference metrics use 5,000 evenly spaced images per set: the LAION improved-aesthetic head on normalized CLIP ViT-L/14 embeddings and PickScore v1 scores for image and prompt pairs. 

Timing and amortization. Timings use one H100. The klein and Z-Image students, adapters, and base-first hybrids use batches of eight; SD-Turbo uses 32, while its unguided SD 2.1 base uses 16. Klein/Z-Image run in bfloat16 and SD in float16; the low-rank update is evaluated in float32. Each recorded batch interval starts immediately before the text-to-image pipeline call and ends after CUDA synchronization. It includes prompt encoding, denoising, and VAE decoding, and excludes model loading, image encoding, and writing packs. We average per-image times over the generated images, including the first batch; no separate warm-up batches are discarded. Legacy default-model timings use the first 256 images at the recorded settings. These are measured pipeline throughputs at the stated batch sizes, not batch-one latency or separately optimized hardware limits. FSG timings include its feature optimization. MoNO generates images sequentially and includes noise optimization, generation, and feature-history updates, with prompt encoding amortized over its input batch. Exact batch sizes for the other generators are in the manifest. The cost for M generated images is C(M ) = Cpairs + Cfit + M t image , counting preparation once per released adapter. Guided targets forfeit much of the gain. With the base’s default guidance applied at every target integration step, as in DP-DMD’s released teacher implementation, the adapter loses 23.1 points ([20.2, 26.2]) on klein and 13.2 ([10.5, 16.1]) on SD-Turbo. In all three families, the default-guidance targets also retain at most 32% of the Vendi gain and 25% of the recall gain (first training runs). The target must span the consequential early interval. Ending at the student’s original first level reaches only 32.1 on klein and 32.2 on SD-Turbo, retaining 57% and 55% of the full gain. Those results resemble completion from the base’s own states at the same levels (32.8 and 32.5); a later distilled step still traverses the sensitive interval. Vendi gains are similarly limited (22% and 39%). Deeper is not always better: 40.0 and 41.2 for deeper klein and SD-Turbo jumps fall below the selected targets. These comparisons support the selected intermediate target over either the original first endpoint or the deepest tested endpoint; they do not define a universal structural threshold. 

Seedwise target correspondence. The adapter preserves target content for individual seeds (Fig. 2). In DINOv3 space, an adapted image is more similar to the completed teacher state from its own initial noise than to states from other noise. The cosine-similarity advantage is 0.32 for klein, compared with 0.27 for base-first and 0.05 for the distilled model. Corresponding SD-Turbo values are 0.33, 0.27, and 0.08. This correspondence supports the interpretation that the adapter learns the target trajectory while the aggregate diversity comparisons alone do not identify how that correspondence arises.

## E.4. Augmenting real training data 

Benefits with limited real data. At 500 real images per class, adding adapted images improves accuracy over both real-only training and augmentation with released-student images. Released klein images provide no resolved improvement over the matched real-only reference, whereas base-first images improve it. With only 50 real images per class, the real-only reference reaches 34.0%. The adapter’s advantage over released-student images grows to +12.1 points for klein and +13.1 for SD-Turbo, and adapted images also outperform unguided-base images. Paired differences and uncertainty are collected in Tab. S23. 24 At full real-data scale. Adding adapted images to the full real ImageNet-1k training set improves ImageNet-Sketch and ImageNet-R accuracy while slightly lowering in-distribution accuracy. Adding released klein images instead lowers all three accuracies. Adapted images therefore provide the better synthetic augmentation in this comparison, with the benefit over real-only training concentrated on the shifted test sets (Tabs. S18 and S23).

Ranking protocol and limits. Within family f , we transform metric and accuracy ranks to (rank f −(nf + 1) /2) /n f and correlate the concatenated ranks. Intervals use 10,000 within-family bootstrap resamples of sets (seed zero). For held-out-family prediction, we fit isotonic regression on the other families, choosing its direction from their Spearman sign. Tied inputs are averaged with multiplicity weights; predictions interpolate linearly between knots and clip at endpoints. We refit in each bootstrap resample and correlate held-out predictions with accuracy within families and pooled across families (Tab. S20). This tests ranking, not calibration; SDXL and FLUX.1-dev remain uncertain. The bootstrap retains unequal family sizes and does not account for shared prompts, image seeds, neighboring sampling plans, or the choice of configuration collection.
