ABSTRACT
Super-telephoto imaging on mobile phones suffers from complex, device-
dependent degradations that are poorly captured by conventional handcrafted
pipelines. Collecting large-scale aligned training pairs across different phones
and diverse scenes, however, is both costly and difficult to scale. We therefore
study whether device-specific degradations can be learned from only a small num-
ber of physical captures and transferred for training a unified super-resolution
(SR) model. Our key idea is to factor degradation learning into two learnable
components: one captures the device-specific imaging characteristics under well-
resolved conditions, while the other captures the additional information loss and
artifacts that arise in super-telephoto imaging. We build a paired benchmark across
three phone brands (Huawei, Apple, and vivo) by photographing the same content
at two display sizes and zoom settings. The larger displayed image is captured
at the phone’s maximum optical zoom setting, while the smaller image is pho-
tographed directly at the phone’s highest native zoom setting through the phone’s
native imaging pipeline. The benchmark contains 519 training and 60 held-out
pairs. We use the benchmark to learn device-specific degradation, then apply it
to external images to synthesize thousands of additional pairs to train our UniTele
SR model. Relative to the pretrained Qwen and FLUX backbones before adap-
tation, the full UniTele training recipe substantially improves paired restoration
fidelity and perceptual similarity on the held-out benchmark and yields markedly
more stable and visually faithful restorations.

INTRODUCTION
Recovering fine detail beyond the optical zoom range is a central challenge in smartphone super-
telephoto imaging. Generative super-resolution (SR) offers a promising approach by using learned
image priors to reconstruct detail from severely degraded inputs (Wang et al., 2023b; Wu et al.,
2024b). However, visually plausible detail is not necessarily faithful to the photographed scene:
ambiguous observations can lead to incorrect textures or structures. Adapting these models to smart-
phone telephoto restoration therefore calls for supervision that connects real degraded observations
to their corresponding reference detail.
Obtaining such supervision is difficult because telephoto degradations are device dependent. The
same content can exhibit different color shifts, blur, and texture loss across phones (Figure 2). Paired
captures provide direct examples of these differences, but collecting and aligning them across de-
vices and diverse scenes is costly (Cai et al., 2019; Wei et al., 2020; Chen et al., 2019). Handcrafted
degradation pipelines readily generate pairs from large image collections, yet their predefined op-
erators may not adequately capture the target device’s imaging characteristics (Zhang et al., 2021;
Wang et al., 2021). Learned degradation models offer a way to synthesize more realistic training
data (Fritsche et al., 2019; Wolf et al., 2021). Building on this direction, we investigate how limited

We propose UniTele, a framework that learns device-specific degradations from a small capture set
and transfers them to external images for SR training. Our key idea is to learn the device’s imaging
characteristics and the additional degradation associated with super-telephoto imaging separately.
The first mapping learns the device’s imaging characteristics under well-resolved conditions; the
second learns the additional detail loss and artifacts in the corresponding super-telephoto input.
To learn these mappings, we construct a controlled paired benchmark using Huawei, Apple, and vivo
phones. We denote the original image displayed on the monitor as the high-resolution reference.
Each image is photographed at two display sizes and zoom settings. The larger image is captured
at the phone’s maximum optical zoom setting to obtain the high-resolution observation; the smaller
image is captured directly at the phone’s highest native zoom setting to obtain the low-resolution
observation. The benchmark contains 519 training pairs and 60 held-out pairs. The first mapping
is learned from high-resolution references to high-resolution observations, and the second from
high-resolution observations to low-resolution observations. We then apply the learned mappings
to external images to synthesize thousands of additional pairs. These synthetic pairs are combined
with real pairs to adapt pretrained generative models to telephoto SR.
Experiments on the held-out paired benchmark demonstrate improved restoration fidelity and per-
ceptual similarity across both Qwen and FLUX backbones. Qualitative comparisons further illus-
trate improvements in the preservation of scene structure.
Our contributions are threefold:
• We propose a device-conditioned degradation framework that separately learns device-
specific imaging characteristics and super-telephoto degradation from limited captures.
• We construct a controlled smartphone super-telephoto benchmark spanning Huawei, Ap-
ple, and vivo to characterize device-dependent degradations and evaluate restoration across
different phones.
• We demonstrate the effectiveness of UniTele on Qwen and FLUX backbones, with im-
provements in reconstruction fidelity, perceptual similarity, and visual quality.

RELATED WORK
Real-world super-resolution and optical zoom.
Classical single-image super-resolution (SISR)
creates low-resolution (LR) observations by applying spatially invariant blur and bicubic downsam-
pling to high-resolution (HR) images. This controlled formulation omits coupled effects of optics,
sensor noise, image signal processing, sharpening, compression, and spatially varying blur. RealSR
instead captures the same scene at different focal lengths and progressively registers the pairs for
supervision (Cai et al., 2019). DRealSR broadens real degradation diversity and shows that smooth
regions, edges, and textures pose different restoration challenges (Wei et al., 2020). Camera Lens
Super-Resolution (CameraSR) studies the lens resolution–field-of-view trade-off. It shows that re-
alistic lens pairs better support digital zoom than conventional synthetic downsampling (Chen et al.,
2019). Related mobile systems align and adaptively fuse concurrent wide and telephoto captures
to improve intermediate zoom (Wu et al., 2023). These studies establish the importance of real
optical observations and paired acquisition. Our task differs from fixed-scale SISR: aligned inputs
and targets may share a canvas but differ in effective optical detail and camera processing. We use
controlled, calibration-assisted captures to learn a forward model that transfers measured device
characteristics to digital source content.
Synthetic and learned degradation modeling.
Practical blind SR methods attempt to close the
synthetic-to-real gap by enriching the forward degradation process. BSRGAN combines blur, re-
sampling, sensor-inspired noise, and JPEG compression in randomly shuffled orders (Zhang et al.,
2021), while Real-ESRGAN introduces a high-order degradation pipeline and explicitly models
ringing and overshoot artifacts (Wang et al., 2021). Such pipelines are efficient and scalable, but their
realism is bounded by a predefined operator family and manually chosen parameter ranges. Learned
degradation models relax these assumptions. DSGAN uses frequency separation and adversarial
learning to transfer real-image characteristics to synthetically downsampled images while preserv-
ing low-frequency content (Fritsche et al., 2019). DeFlow instead models stochastic conditional
degradations in a shared latent space and learns from unpaired clean and degraded marginals using
conditional normalizing flows (Wolf et al., 2021). These approaches typically learn one aggregate
target degradation distribution. In contrast, mobile telephoto degradations are not interchangeable:
different phones, lenses, zoom levels, and ISP pipelines can produce distinct tone, blur, sharpening,
compression, and residual texture for the same scene. We therefore study a single conditional gener-
ator whose output distribution is indexed by device identity and anchored by a telephoto degradation
reference from that device. This formulation enables explicit device switching without maintaining
one degradation model per phone.
Generative priors and conditional image restoration.
Large pretrained generative models have
recently become strong priors for real-world image restoration. StableSR adapts a pretrained text-
to-image diffusion model to blind SR while introducing controllable feature wrapping to balance
perceptual quality and fidelity (Wang et al., 2023b). SeeSR extracts degradation-aware semantic
prompts to reduce semantic errors under severe input degradation (Wu et al., 2024b), and the One-
Step Effective Diffusion Network (OSEDiff) distills the restoration process into a single diffusion
step for efficient inference (Wu et al., 2024a). Adversarial Diffusion Compression for Real-World
Image Super-Resolution (AdcSR) further compresses one-step diffusion through structural prun-
ing and adversarial distillation (Chen et al., 2025). In parallel, all-in-one restoration methods use
learned prompts or language instructions to select among restoration behaviors. PromptIR learns
degradation-specific prompts for a unified restoration network (Potlapalli et al., 2023), whereas In-
structIR conditions restoration directly on human-written instructions (Conde et al., 2024). These
methods primarily use generative or conditional priors in the inverse direction, mapping an unknown
degraded image to a clean output. Our use of an image-editing foundation model, instantiated with
Qwen-Image-Edit-2511 (Wu et al., 2025; Qwen, 2025), is complementary: we adapt the prior in the
forward direction to map digital source content to a phone-specific synthetic LR image.

METHOD
We train a unified SR model using limited paired captures and external images without phone obser-
vations. UniTele learns device-specific degradations from the captures and applies them to external
3


content to expand paired supervision. Figure 1 shows controlled acquisition, two learned mappings
for pair synthesis, and joint training on real and synthetic pairs.
3.1
MULTI-DEVICE PAIRED ACQUISITION
Learning device-specific degradations requires paired observations with reliable content correspon-
dence, but obtaining a well-resolved counterpart to an in-the-wild super-telephoto image is difficult.
We therefore vary the size of an image displayed on a monitor to simulate the apparent size of the
same subject at different distances: a smaller image represents a more distant subject, while a larger
image provides a closer view of the same content.
Let x denote the original digital image, or high-resolution (HR) reference. Keeping the camera–
monitor distance fixed, we photograph the larger displayed image with device c at its maximum
optical zoom setting to obtain the HR observation hc. We then photograph the smaller displayed
image directly at the phone’s highest native zoom setting to obtain the low-resolution (LR) observa-
tion yc. The displayed image sizes are chosen so that the subject occupies roughly the same fraction
of the frame in hc and yc. In this pair, yc represents a distant subject photographed at the phone’s
highest native zoom, while hc provides a well-resolved observation of the same content. We pho-
tograph a calibration board at both zoom settings and estimate a homography from corresponding
corners to align hc and yc. The resulting (x, hc) pairs provide supervision for the device’s imaging
characteristics under well-resolved conditions, while (hc, yc) pairs supervise the additional detail
loss and artifacts associated with super-telephoto imaging.
The benchmark includes 519 training pairs and 60 test pairs from Huawei Pura 70 Pro+, iPhone 17
Pro Max, and vivo X300 Ultra, with 20 test pairs per device.

3.3
SUPER-RESOLUTION TRAINING WITH REAL AND SYNTHETIC DATA
To expand SR supervision without additional captures, we apply Equation 1 to external HR refer-
ences x, generating synthetic training pairs (ˆyc, ˆhc) with the corresponding synthetic HR observation
as the restoration target. These pairs supplement captured pairs (yc, hc), broadening content cover-
age while retaining real degradation examples. We use pretrained Qwen and FLUX models for SR
reconstruction, adapting each through LoRA to recover the HR observation from its LR observation.

EXPERIMENTS
We examine real–synthetic training across SR backbones, in-the-wild transfer, the forward-model
stages, and cross-device generalization.
4.1
EXPERIMENTAL SETUP
Experiments use 519 captured training pairs, 60 paired test images (Section 3.1), and a separate
in-the-wild set. We evaluate Qwen-Image-Edit-2511 (Qwen SR) (Qwen, 2025) and FLUX.2 [klein]
4B Base (FLUX.2 SR) (Black Forest Labs, 2026). We compare pretrained backbones with LoRA
adapters trained on real, synthetic, or mixed pairs; the mixed models are UniTele (Qwen) and
UniTele (FLUX). The Real-ESRGAN baseline trains Qwen SR on pairs synthesized with its degra-
dation pipeline (Wang et al., 2021). Official pretrained AdcSR (Chen et al., 2025) and OSEDiff (Wu
et al., 2024a) provide further comparisons.
Qwen SR training. We adapt Qwen using conditional flow-matching with timestep-weighted MSE,
applying LoRA to the attention Q/K/V and output projections, added Q/K/V and output projections,
image and text MLP output projections, and modulation layers with rank 32, scaling factor α 32, and
dropout 0. Training uses AdamW with learning rate 10−4 in the captured-data phase and 5 × 10−5
in the mixed-data phase, a constant schedule, an effective batch size of 8, and image resolution set
dynamically up to 786,432 pixels (typically 1024×768 in landscape or 768×1024 in portrait). The
captured-data and mixed-data phases run for 3,055 and 2,340 updates, respectively. Training uses 8
Ascend 910B2C NPUs and takes approximately 4.08 hours for the first phase and 3.13 hours for the
second.
5


FLUX SR training. We adapt FLUX using conditional flow-matching with timestep-weighted
MSE, applying LoRA to dual-stream attention projections, added Q/K/V projections, input and out-
put projections, joint attention–MLP projections, and the attention output projections in 20 single-
transformer blocks with rank 32, scaling factor α 32, and dropout 0. Training uses AdamW with
learning rate 10−4, a constant schedule, an effective batch size of 8, and image resolution set dynam-
ically up to 262,144 pixels (typically about 576 × 432). The captured-data and mixed-data phases
run for 2,466 and 2,531 updates, respectively. Training uses 8 Ascend 910B2C NPUs and takes
approximately 0.57 hours for the first phase and 0.55 hours for the second.
For full-reference evaluation, we report RGB PSNR, SSIM, MAE, LPIPS (Zhang et al., 2018), and
the predicted-to-target high-frequency (HF) energy ratio. We report per-device scores and their arith-
metic mean across devices; an HF ratio closer to one indicates better agreement in high-frequency
energy. For Human Preference (HP), data-evaluation experts compare each output with its aligned
GT and assign a 1–10 score based on visual similarity, content consistency, and color shift; we report
the mean score. For in-the-wild images without paired targets, we report MUSIQ (Ke et al., 2021),
CLIP-IQA (Wang et al., 2023a), and NIQE (Mittal et al., 2013), alongside qualitative comparisons.

GENERALIZATION TO IN-THE-WILD IMAGES
We evaluate transfer from screen-based training to in-the-wild scenes on 16 iPhone LR images
without paired HR targets, using fixed checkpoints without further adaptation. Both UniTele variants
use a real-to-synthetic sampling ratio of 1:2; GPT Image 2 (OpenAI, 2026) provides an additional
comparison. We report mean no-reference scores at 1024×768, resizing FLUX.2 outputs from their
native working resolution of 576 × 432 before evaluation.
Table 2 shows that UniTele (Qwen) improves MUSIQ and CLIP-IQA over its pretrained backbone,
with a slight increase in NIQE. UniTele (FLUX) scores worse than its backbone on all three metrics,
while GPT Image 2 obtains the best scores. In Figure 5, UniTele (Qwen) produces clearer window
grids and ship railings than the Real-ESRGAN-trained baseline, with fewer conspicuous structural
distortions than the Qwen base model. These examples illustrate transfer to real scenes, but no-
reference scores and visual comparisons cannot verify the fidelity of recovered details without HR
targets.

CONCLUSION
We presented UniTele, a device-conditioned approach that learns camera-domain rendering and
super-telephoto degradation from a limited set of aligned captures, then transfers both transforma-
tions to external images to expand SR supervision. On the 60-pair three-phone test set, combining
9


real and synthetic pairs improved PSNR and LPIPS over real-world-only training for both Qwen
and FLUX backbones. Forward-model ablations support the factorized synthesis design, while the
single-device ablation shows the value of training across phones for this benchmark. Results on in-
the-wild images suggest qualitative transfer, although the no-reference scores are mixed and cannot
establish whether recovered details match the scene. Together, these findings indicate that learned,
device-conditioned pair synthesis can extend the utility of a small capture set for smartphone tele-
photo restoration.
Limitations.
The capture protocol is restricted to a planar display, and residual alignment error
and display-specific artifacts may affect both supervision and evaluation. Device coverage is finite,
and a generative degradation model may alter content.
