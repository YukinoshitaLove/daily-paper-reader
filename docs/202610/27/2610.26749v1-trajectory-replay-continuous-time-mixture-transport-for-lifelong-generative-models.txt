Transport for Lifelong Generative Models

Adapting pre-trained continuous-time generative models to sequentially incoming data without
forgetting earlier tasks is a central challenge in lifelong learning. A leading strategy, generative
replay, uses a generative model trained on historical tasks to augment its own continual update
with synthetic samples from experience. However, it requires full trajectory simulations at every
training step, which accumulate discretization errors and can degrade the replayed historical
distribution. Here, we propose trajectory replay, which bypasses sample generation by training
an updated velocity field to match both a frozen historical velocity along its trajectories and
the incoming task’s velocity field, weighted by a stability-plasticity coefficient. Through the lens
of continuous-time transport, we show the resulting optimum averages the two task velocities
by their respective densities and generates the mixture of their probability paths. Combined
with mean velocity distillation, trajectory replay accesses historical trajectories using only a
few network evaluations per continual update. Empirically, it outperforms generative replay
and distillation baselines on class-incremental image generation. In two biological case studies,
trajectory replay extends generative perturbation models to new contexts and experimental batches
across two data modalities while preserving treatment-effect prediction. Code is available at
https://github.com/valence-labs/trajectory_replay.

Introduction
Continuous-time generative models, such as diffusion and flow matching (Song et al., 2021; Lipman
et al., 2023), have gained traction across image generation (Dhariwal & Nichol, 2021; Esser et al.,
2024), language modeling (Li et al., 2022), and scientific applications (Morehead et al., 2026; Sriram
et al., 2024). Trained on large corpora of data to capture complex, multi-modal distributions, they
yield reusable generative priors for a range of downstream tasks (Rombach et al., 2022). As novel
data accumulates, however, effective strategies are needed to update pre-trained models with incoming
knowledge while preserving generative capabilities over the historical distribution. Joint retraining on
the union of historical and incoming data grows more expensive with every update, and is infeasible
when the original data is no longer accessible, for instance due to privacy or licensing constraints.
Incorporating new data without full retraining motivates continual learning (CL) strategies (Wang
et al., 2024), whereby a seed model is updated on incoming data streams while mitigating forgetting of
previous tasks. In generative modeling, each task corresponds to a new data distribution, absent from
the historical dataset, that the model must learn to sample from (Guo et al., 2025). CL approaches
applied to generative models include architectural evolution (Smith et al., 2024), regularization (Song
et al., 2024), and replay (Rolnick et al., 2019). Among them, generative replay (Shin et al., 2017) is
one of the most established (Van de Ven et al., 2022): the objective over incoming tasks is augmented
with samples generated by the model itself over the historical distribution, alleviating catastrophic
forgetting without storing or revisiting historical data.
Applying standard generative replay to continuous-time frameworks is expensive, as each replay sample
requires iterative simulation of the underlying dynamics. Approximation errors compound along these
trajectories, degrading replay fidelity and, in turn, subsequent CL updates (Gao & Liu, 2023). One line
of work stabilizes replay by renoising generated samples and regressing the frozen historical model’s

To address these shortcomings, we introduce trajectory replay (Fig. 1), a flow-based generative replay
framework for continuous-time models. Given a batch of incoming data and a vector field trained to
generate the historical task distribution, our method splits the CL-update objective for a joint-task
velocity field into two regression terms. The first is a standard conditional flow matching loss (Lipman
et al., 2023) over the incoming data. The second regresses the joint-task velocity toward the frozen
historical field along its own generative trajectory at intermediate times, so replay never runs through
to fully generated samples. This halves the simulation cost in expectation and makes replay more
robust to discretization error. A stability-plasticity coefficient weights the two terms and controls how
much mass the optimum assigns to the historical and the incoming density.
We validate trajectory replay in controlled simulations and class-incremental image-generation bench-
marks, where it improves sample fidelity and retention of earlier tasks across sequential updates at a
fraction of the network evaluations of existing CL strategies. Since continual adaptation is particularly
important in biological discovery, where measurements arrive sequentially across cell lines, donors, and
experimental batches, and where historical screens may be inaccessible, we evaluate two drug-discovery
applications of generative perturbation prediction in single-cell gene expression and microscopy. Across
these settings, trajectory replay offers the best stability-plasticity balance among methods that store
no historical data. In summary, our contributions are as follows:
• Trajectory-level replay objective. We formulate generative replay as velocity-field regression
along the historical model’s generative trajectories, and derive a matching continual update rule for
its mean velocity (Geng et al., 2026a), reducing each update to fewer network evaluations.
• Optimality and stability-plasticity control. We show the population optimum of our trajectory
objective is a density-weighted mixture of the historical and incoming probability paths, with the
mixture weight controlling the stability-plasticity trade-off, and that the objective recovers a weighted
forward Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence up to time reweighting.


• Gains across scales and domains. Trajectory replay improves the fit-retention trade-off over
baselines on simulations, image benchmarks, and two biological case studies.

2.2
Continual learning and replay for generative models
Problem statement. We consider a CL paradigm where a generative model trained on an initial
data distribution is extended to sequentially arriving data streams. We illustrate our setting across
a single update step, though our framework extends to multiple iterations. Denote q ∈P(X) as the
historical density, the ground-truth distribution of the initial task dataset before sequential updates,
and q′ ∈P(X) as the incoming data’s ground-truth density for the subsequent CL step. The goal is
to approximate a target density ˜q ∈P(X) that preserves the ability to sample from both, mitigating
catastrophic forgetting on the old task while ensuring accurate adaptation to incoming information.
Generative replay. Consider a flow model {pt}t∈[0,1] approximating q at t = 1 (Sec. 2.1). Naive fine-
tuning on samples from q′ leads to forgetting, as the velocity field is retrained to transport noise onto q′


alone, overwriting the transport onto q in the regions the two paths share. Generative replay (Shin et al.,
2017) mitigates this by augmenting training batches with samples generated from p1 at rate λ ∈(0, 1),
modulating the stability-plasticity trade-off that governs how far the model may deviate from the
historical task. However, generating each replay sample requires simulating the full generative trajectory
defined in Eq. (3), making this augmentation expensive and susceptible to discretization errors.

Trajectory replay for continual learning
First, we characterize the terminal distribution a model generates after a replay update. Training on
replay-augmented batches with the objective in Eq. (2) optimizes a vector field whose updated terminal
marginal approximates ˜q, the mixture of historical and incoming densities (details in Sec. A.4):
˜q(x) := λq(x) + (1 −λ)q′(x).
(5)
This mixture behavior admits a variational justification: modeling CL updates as a λ-weighted mixture
is equivalent to minimizing a forward KL divergence objective. Specifically, one can show that the ˜q in
Eq. (5) is the unique global minimizer of the following optimization problem:
arg min
ρ∈P(X)
λDKL(q ∥ρ) + (1 −λ)DKL(q′ ∥ρ) .

The formalization and proof are in Sec. A.5. Since the forward DKL(q ∥ρ) penalizes low mass where q
has support, its minimizer is mode-covering, discouraging collapse onto either task and thus retaining
history while adapting to incoming data, as desired for continual updates. In this work, we develop a
more efficient continuous-time solution for such mixture-based updates at the trajectory level.

Baselines. Joint training on all tasks gives an upper bound, and naive sequential fine-tuning measures
forgetting. We run fine-tuning separately for diffusion and flow matching, ruling out the generative
objective as the source of any difference. We compare against diffusion-based generative replay and gen-
erative distillation (Masip et al., 2025), which regresses the frozen teacher’s noise prediction on renoised
replay samples. We reproduce both under our protocol for ImageNet-100, since they were not evaluated
there originally. For flow-based CL, generative and trajectory replay are each compared using numerical
integration of the teacher flow or a MeanFlow approximation (Sec. 3.2) with one or four flow-map steps,
under both equal-task and adaptive weighting (selecting λ by minimizing validation FID across seen
tasks). In Tab. 3, we add LoRA and gradual fine-tuning as adapter and regularization baselines.
Results. Trajectory replay is the strongest method family, achieving the best non-joint FID on all
three datasets and the best KLD on both unconditional benchmarks, outperforming generative replay
and diffusion-based baselines (Tab. 1). For trajectory replay, adaptive weighting generally yields the
lowest FID, whereas equal-task weighting remains competitive and consistently improves class-mass
recovery. On FMNIST and CIFAR-10, the weights selected for trajectory replay track the equal-task
rule, while those for generative replay settle below it, consistent with the criterion discounting its
lower-fidelity replayed samples (Fig. 15). Trajectory replay also remains robust when ODE integration
is replaced by one or four flow-map steps, while generative replay often degrades under the same
approximation (Fig. 16). The runtime advantage of MeanFlow distillation emerges only as teacher
steps grow (Fig. 17). Trajectory replay is cheaper end-to-end too, by 1.22× to 1.65×, with the margin
growing with generation cost (Fig. 12).
4.4
Application: perturbation modeling in single-cell transcriptomics
Task. We apply trajectory replay to perturbation modeling, where a generative model of cellular
responses to treatment is updated as screens arrive in new cellular contexts. In each screen, data arrive
as tuples (x, y, c) of a high-dimensional gene expression vector x, a perturbation label y (a molecule
or a gene suppression), and a context c (a cell line or a donor), and a generative model approximates
the treatment response via p(x | c, y) (Palla et al., 2026). The initial task trains on a subset of
contexts, and updates bring the same perturbations to novel ones. Success requires (I) preserving
perturbation modeling on earlier contexts, (II) fitting the incoming ones, and (III) generalizing to
unseen context-perturbation combinations from the incoming data.

At the final
checkpoint, we report retention on previously learned strata, fit on newly introduced ones, and

two-stage adaptation, which first fits the new context embedding alone and then unfreezes the full
model, and generative and trajectory replay. Real replay stores past tasks as a privileged reference.
Results. We jointly examine fit, retention, and held-out performance (Fig. 4; full tables in Tabs. 6
and 7). Replay is the only family reaching both axes: freezing retains without fitting, while LoRA,
GFT, two-stage, and full fine-tuning either only fit or neither. Freezing can still top the retention
column because the historical task lives in the velocity field rather than in the conditioning embeddings,
so a frozen field preserves it by refusing to adapt (Tabs. 6 and 7). Trajectory replay edges ahead of
generative replay on the single-update Replogle-Nadig stream and clearly surpasses it across the five
sequential tasks of Parse-PBMC, approaching real replay without storing cells. Point color confirms
that held-out performance follows the upper-right frontier rather than either axis alone, since an unseen
combination needs both the perturbation response carried from old contexts and the basal structure
of the new one. As in the image streams, replacing ODE integration with one or four flow-map steps
leaves trajectory replay at parity while generative replay degrades (Fig. 24).


4.5
Application: Continual phenotypic modeling in microscopy
Task. We extend Sec. 4.4 to microscopy, where x is an image and the model captures the morphological
phenotype a perturbation induces. Image-based profiling is a standard readout in drug discovery, but
experiments acquired at different times differ in staining and imaging conditions, producing batch effects
that complicate comparisons across experiments. The RxRx1 dataset (Sypetkowski et al., 2023) images
U2OS cells under gene knockdowns across experimental batches acquired weeks apart, and we treat
each batch as one task. Since the perturbation vocabulary is identical across tasks, the shift is purely
technical, so any loss of performance on earlier batches reflects forgetting of their acquisition conditions.
Setup (details in Sec. D.6.1).
We stream three U2OS experiments (01 →02 →03) over 1,071
perturbations, conditioning an ablated diffusion model (ADM) U-Net (Dhariwal & Nichol, 2021) on
perturbation and experiment identity, with six-channel 96 × 96 image crops modeled natively. Baselines
follow Sec. 4.3, adding real replay as a privileged reference that stores past images. The MeanFlow
backends are run for trajectory replay under equal-task weighting only, since the Jacobian-vector
product in the MeanFlow residual becomes costly at this resolution. With only a few dozen test images
per perturbation, Fréchet distances cannot be estimated per condition, so we generate one image per
test condition, matching the perturbation composition of generated and reference sets, and report FID
against pooled test images of all seen experiments after each update (Zhang et al., 2025). We also
report a Fréchet distance in DINOv2 (Oquab et al., 2024) features (FDD), with full results in Sec. D.6.2
and Tab. 9.

5
Related works
Lifelong learning for generative models.
CL methods for generative models fall into three
broad families: architectural updates, which allocate task-specific parameters (Smith et al., 2024),
regularization, which constrains how far parameters may drift from those learned on earlier tasks (Dong
et al., 2024; Kirkpatrick et al., 2017), and replay, which rehearses past tasks during each update (Sun
et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024; Guo et al., 2025). Replay has been used to curb forgetting both in
sequential classification (Shin et al., 2017; Wu et al., 2018; Ye & Bors, 2020) and in continuous-time
generative frameworks (Gao & Liu, 2023; Skierś & Deja, 2025; Liao et al., 2025). It has also been
moved from the input space to intermediate representations, where generating features is easier than
generating pixels (Liu et al., 2020), and we discuss the relation of this line of work to our setting in
Sec. A.14. As in our setting, several works treat generation not only as a source of replay samples but


as the task to be preserved itself (Simone et al., 2025; Zając et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2026). Closest to
our work is Masip et al. (2025), who use a diffusion model trained on historical data as a teacher for a
sequential noise predictor, supervised on noise-augmented replay samples. Their replay still generates
complete samples before renoising them, whereas trajectory replay stops the historical trajectory at an
intermediate time and never forms a finished sample.
Flow matching and density mixtures. Trajectory replay builds on flow matching (Lipman et al.,
2023; Tong et al., 2024a) and on its distillation into few-step samplers (Geng et al., 2026a; Boffi et al.,
2025). It also relates to work on controlling likelihoods in continuous time (Albergo et al., 2025; Su et al.,
2025; Römer et al., 2026) and on sampling from mixtures of generative models (Liu, 2022), both of which
have mostly been studied as inference-time control over frozen models (Skreta et al., 2025), whereas we
use these ideas to train a model across sequential updates. To our knowledge, mixtures of probability
paths have not previously been used to formulate continual updates of continuous-time generative
models. Most related within this line is Thorkelsdottir & Banerjee (2026), who anneal the historical
model toward the incoming task, while trajectory replay preserves old tasks while integrating new ones.
6
Discussion
Conclusion. We introduced trajectory replay, which replaces sample-level generative replay with
velocity-field regression along the historical model’s own trajectories. Its optimum is the density-weighted
average of the two task velocities, generating the mixture of their probability paths, so the stability-
plasticity coefficient sets what the update retains rather than only trading it off, and the objective
recovers a weighted forward KL divergence up to time reweighting. A matching rule for the mean velocity
carries this optimum to the flow-map level. Because replay stops at intermediate times, it halves the
expected simulation cost and makes replay more robust to discretization error. Empirically, trajectory
replay outperforms replay and distillation baselines on class-incremental image generation, stays robust
under few-step replay, and extends perturbation models to incremental experimental measurements.
Limitations. Trajectory replay still involves partial simulation under a historical model, and its
adaptive version, though favorable on image generation, requires a training run per candidate weight at
every update. The mean velocity aids sampling but requires a Jacobian-vector product whose memory
cost grows with the state, which restricted its use in our microscopy experiments. Partial replay limits
how solver error enters each update, but not the approximation error of the historical model itself,
which every update inherits and which may compound over long task sequences, echoing forgetting
bounds for continually fine-tuned Large Language Models (LLMs), while our streams span at most
five tasks. Our reliance on flow matching also leaves extension to autoregressive models open. Finally,
future work will explore CL updates of more efficient distillation models.

previous tasks.

A.12
Integration on the selection of λ
The coefficient λk governs not only a single update but the weight each task retains across the whole
stream. Unrolling Eq. (5) over the stream expresses the terminal density as a mixture over all tasks
seen, with weights determined by the schedule {λk}K−1
k=1 . Holding λk = λ constant gives the incoming
task weight 1 −λ, the previous one λ(1 −λ), and any task introduced m updates earlier λm(1 −λ),
with the initial task retaining λK−1 since it never entered as an incoming task: the weight decays
geometrically in task age, so a constant rate discounts older tasks rather than balancing them.
Uniform weight instead requires λk = k/(k + 1), since at step k the historical model carries k tasks
at weight 1/k each, and weighting it by k/(k + 1) against 1/(k + 1) for the incoming task leaves all
k + 1 tasks at 1/(k + 1). Selecting λk is therefore a choice of how the stream is weighted overall, not
only of the current stability-plasticity trade-off, and the two schedules above correspond to the settings
compared in Sec. 4. The adaptive policy admits no such closed form, since the weights depend on
validation outcomes at each update, so the terminal mixture it induces is known only after the fact.

are visited.
Caveats with trajectory replay.
The argument only covers the states the solver actually reaches.
A solver that systematically avoids a region leaves the student unconstrained there, and with finite
capacity the shift in which states are visited is not innocuous either, since supervising the wrong points
trades approximation error between regions. Fig. 7 and the matched-backend comparisons of Sec. D.3
measure the combined effect of both mechanisms.
A.14
Relation to feature replay
Trajectory replay is loosely connected with feature replay approaches, sharing a similar motivating
diagnosis: replaying generated images is expensive, and errors in those images enter training as if they
were data. Liu et al. (2020) address this by splitting the network at a layer, freezing and distilling
the feature extractor, and replaying intermediate features rather than images. A generator produces
features for past classes, which are paired with their old labels and fed to the classifier. The gain is
that feature distributions are lower-dimensional and far easier to model than pixels, so the generator
can be accurate where an image generator would not be.
A continuous-depth view. The same structure appears in our setting. Under the neural ODE
correspondence (Chen et al., 2018), integration time is depth and a flow is a weight-tied residual network
(He et al., 2016) whose block at depth t is
xt+∆t = xt + ∆t vt(xt) ,
(67)
so vt is the residual branch and xt an intermediate representation. Generative replay runs every block
to obtain the output x1 and treats it as a sample. Trajectory replay runs a prefix and supervises the
residual branch at the depth it reached. Two consequences follow. The expected prefix is half the
depth, which is the accounting of Sec. A.13. And because the blocks share parameters, a single sampled
t trains the whole network, so no layer-wise schedule is needed.
Where the two differ. The resemblance stops at what is replayed. Feature replay generates a
substitute for a real activation and pairs it with an old label, so the generator’s fidelity decides whether
the training pair is correct, exactly as in standard generative replay. The requirement is moved to
an easier space rather than removed. Trajectory replay generates no substitute for anything. The
state xt is a probe location, and the target is the frozen model’s own velocity there, which is a valid
input-output pair wherever the solver landed (Sec. A.13). The split also differs: feature replay’s is
architectural and fixed. Ours is temporal and resampled every step, so no layer is privileged.

Model. The velocity field is an unconditional MLP of width 256 and depth 4, with a 64-dimensional

(Tong et al., 2024a). We sample using Euler with step size 0.05 (20 evaluations). Given the task’s
simplicity, we did not ablate model capacity.
Pretraining. The task-0 model trains for 400 epochs with batch size 256 and Adam (Kingma & Ba,
2014) at learning rate 10−3, seed 0. A single checkpoint is shared by every update run, so all differences
across methods and seeds come from the update alone.
Update. Each update trains for 400 epochs with the same optimizer settings, for seeds {0, 1, 2} at
every λ in the grid. We compare naive fine-tuning, which continues training the historical checkpoint on
incoming data alone with no replay term, against generative and trajectory replay, plus a joint-training
reference fit directly on samples from ˜q. Generative replay integrates the frozen teacher to t = 1 and
treats the samples as data, drawing a fraction λ of every batch from the teacher and the remainder
from the incoming task, then taking a single flow-matching loss over the combined batch. Trajectory
replay splits each batch evenly, draws one shared t ∼U(10−4, 1] per step, integrates the teacher only to
t, and matches the student field against the teacher field there, with λ weighting the historical and
incoming terms of the loss.
Evaluation. We generate 4,000 samples per run with the same sampler and report two quantities.
Generated historical mass ˆπ0 (Fig. 2a) is the fraction of samples whose nearest mode mean is mode 0.
Energy distance is computed between the generated samples and the held-out reference set, restricted
to the active coordinate on which the mode means differ (Fig. 7). All figures show three seeds.
Sampling-budget comparison (Fig. 7). The teacher-cost curves use d ∈{128, 256} at λ = 0.5
(equal-task), training for 513 epochs (200,070 optimizer steps) with evaluation every 10 epochs on
2,000 samples, for seeds {0, 1, 2}. Cumulative teacher evaluations follow analytically from the fixed
Euler protocol: at 100,000 points and batch size 256, there are 390 steps per epoch, generative replay
spends ⌈1/0.05⌉= 20 evaluations per step, and trajectory replay spends E[⌈t/0.05⌉] + 1 = 11.5, since t
is uniform on (0, 1], integrating only to an intermediate time plus one frozen-field evaluation at the
anchor. Over the full run, this is 4.0 × 106 teacher evaluations for generative replay against 2.3 × 106
for trajectory replay.

whole stream is repeated for seeds {0, 1, 2}, which vary initialization, minibatch order, and sampling
noise.
Replay policies. We compare naive fine-tuning against generative and trajectory replay under three
ways of setting the replay weight.
• Constant. λ ∈{0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 0.9} held fixed across the stream.
• Equal-task. λk = k/(k + 1) at update k, the parameter-free choice that keeps all seen tasks equally
weighted.
• Adaptive. The full update is run once for each candidate in {0.25, 0.5, 2/3, 0.75, 0.8, 0.9}, and we keep
the model whose 4,000 generated samples minimize energy distance to a balanced validation mixture
over all tasks seen so far, so the selection uses only validation data available at that point in the
stream and no test data. The chosen model becomes the historical model for the next update, and
the sweep is repeated there, which costs one full training run per candidate per update.
Running a policy to the end of the stream therefore fixes a target composition over tasks: equal-task
yields the uniform target, whereas a constant λ discounts older tasks geometrically (Sec. A.12). The
generated task mass is scored against that target.
Evaluation. After every update, we generate 10,000 samples and report two quantities against the
test splits of the seen tasks. Energy distance is computed against the balanced mixture formed by
concatenating equal-size test sets across seen modes. Generated task mass assigns each sample to its
nearest task mean, adds a pseudocount of 0.5 to keep the divergence finite when a task receives no
samples, and is compared with the uniform target by forward categorical KL. All figures show three seeds.

Stream layout and final samples. Fig. 8a shows the five-task ring layout and the order in which
tasks arrive, and Fig. 8b shows the samples drawn after the final update: naive fine-tuning retains only

closest to the balanced target.
Weighting policies. Fig. 9a places every final model on the trade-off between retaining earlier tasks
and fitting the newest one. The constant-λ runs trace a clear frontier, from λ = 0.9 retaining history at
the cost of T4 to λ = 0.25 doing the reverse, with λ = 0.5 and 0.75 in between. Both scheduled policies
sit near the uniform target, well inside the frontier that any single constant weight can reach, which is the
practical case for scheduling λk rather than tuning it once. Fig. 9b shows that the adaptive sweep selects
weights closely tracking the equal-task rule λk = k/(k + 1) at every update, for both replay methods.
This is expected, since the five modes carry equal mass and the balanced criterion is optimized by exactly
the composition equal-task produces in closed form: the agreement validates the adaptive procedure
rather than separating the two schedules, and explains why they perform comparably in Fig. 3b.

D.3
Class-incremental image generation
D.3.1
Experimental details
Streams. Each dataset is split into disjoint class groups arriving sequentially, with class orders drawn
per seed via the Avalanche package (Carta et al., 2023) so that the stream is identical across methods.
We run seeds {42, 69, 1714} (same as Masip et al. (2025)), each fixing the class order and training
randomness. Split Fashion-MNIST and Split CIFAR-10 give five tasks of two classes each and share
the same partition at a given seed. Split ImageNet-100 gives three tasks of 34/33/33 classes. Within
every task, a deterministic 10% of the training split is held out for validation, and the official test split
is reserved for reporting.
Inputs. Fashion-MNIST is resized to 32×32 single-channel and CIFAR-10 to 32×32 RGB, both nor-
malized to [−1, 1], with train-time horizontal flips on CIFAR-10 only. ImageNet-100 is modeled in latent
space: images are resized to 256×256 and encoded once with a frozen stabilityai/sd-vae-ft-mse
autoencoder1 into 32×32×4 scaled latents, cached to disk, with train-time flips. Latents are decoded
to pixels only for evaluation and sample grids.
Model. The velocity field is a Diffusers UNet2D (von Platen et al., 2022) on the 32×32 state, trained
on the linear noise-to-data interpolant with target x1 −x0. Fashion-MNIST and CIFAR-10 reuse the
medium configuration of Masip et al. (2025), with block widths (128, 256, 256, 256) and attention at
one resolution (35.8M parameters), so our numbers are directly comparable to their diffusion baselines.
ImageNet-100 was not considered by Masip et al. (2025), so we selected its backbone and budget with
a task-0 pilot crossing the medium configuration against a large latent-diffusion-style one, of widths
(128, 256, 512, 512) and attention at the two lowest resolutions (106.4M parameters), over optimizer
budgets of {10, 20, 50, 100, 200}k steps, picking the lowest FID on the task-0 classes (Fig. 11). This
selects the large backbone at 100k steps. The same pilot crossed conditional against unconditional
variants, and we model ImageNet-100 class-conditionally since the unconditional runs were clearly worse
at this scale, with label dropout 0.1 and classifier-free guidance (Zheng et al., 2023) 1.5 at sampling time.
Training. Every task is trained with Adam under the per-dataset budget in Tab. 2. Joint training
reuses real data from all tasks seen so far: Fashion-MNIST and CIFAR-10 hold the epoch count fixed
as the union grows, whereas ImageNet-100 holds the optimizer-step count fixed, so per-task compute
does not grow with the stream.

Replay. Both families share the stream and budget and differ only in what the historical term regresses
onto.
• Generative replay draws a fraction λ of each batch from the frozen previous-task model and trains a
single flow matching loss on the combined batch.
• Trajectory replay splits each batch evenly, samples one shared time per step, integrates the teacher
only to that time, and matches the student field against the teacher field there, with λ weighting the
historical and incoming terms.
On ImageNet-100, both condition the historical branch on labels cycled uniformly over all classes seen
so far, and generate without guidance, unlike evaluation.
The equal-task policy sets λk = k/(k + 1), and the adaptive policy selects λ from the per-dataset grid
by validation FID on 2,048 samples. The ODE backend integrates the teacher with Euler at the replay
step size in Tab. 2. The MeanFlow backend replaces it with a distilled mean-velocity model using
N ∈{1, 4} jumps, distilling task 0 post hoc at learning rate 10−4 with gradient clipping 1.0 for the
per-dataset epochs in the table, then co-training the field and the mean velocity on later tasks with
logit-normal time pairs, as in Geng et al. (2026a) (see algorithm 2).
Adaptive loss weighting. Task 0 distillation also uses adaptive loss weighting with the exponent
in Tab. 2, but sequential updates do not: rescaling each branch by its own error would change their
relative weight away from λ and 1 −λ, and the fixed point of Prop. 3.3 would no longer be the mixture
mean velocity.
Baselines. We report a series of reference points, all on the same stream, per-task budget, backbone,
and evaluation protocol as our methods, following the scheme of Masip et al. (2025).
• Joint training retrains on the union of all real data seen so far, storing every past sample, and is
therefore an upper target rather than a competing method.
• Naive fine-tuning continues training on the incoming task alone with no retention mechanism, giving
the lower target. Since the baselines below use a different generative objective, we report it for both,
as diffusion naive fine-tuning and flow matching naive fine-tuning, separating the gain attributable
to replay from that attributable to the objective.
• Generative replay (diffusion) is the diffusion counterpart of generative replay above.


• Generative distillation (diffusion) (Masip et al., 2025) also replays from the frozen previous-task
model, but supervises with the teacher’s own noise prediction at a randomly drawn timestep rather
than with the injected noise, so the student matches the teacher’s denoising behavior along the whole
trajectory instead of only reproducing its final samples. This is the strongest published no-storage
continuous-time baseline on these streams.
• LoRA (Hu et al., 2022) and gradual fine-tuning (GFT) (Thorkelsdottir & Banerjee, 2026) are adapter
and regularization baselines, reported separately in Tab. 3. LoRA adapts the velocity field with rank
r ∈{8, 16, 32} and α = 2r, and GFT self-distills from the frozen previous model with a distillation
weight β annealed from 1 to 0, under sigmoid and linear schedules.
Evaluation. After the final task we generate 10,000 samples and report stream FID in Inception feature
space (Szegedy et al., 2016) against pooled held-out images from all seen classes. Fashion-MNIST
and CIFAR-10 are modeled unconditionally, so class composition is emergent and forgetting shows up
as mass collapsing onto recent classes. Class-mass recovery (KLD) compares the classifier-predicted
histogram against the target composition, using the pretrained convolutional neural network (CNN)
classifiers released with the diffusion baselines (Masip et al., 2025), and isolates retention from the
sample quality captured by FID. For ImageNet-100, labels are drawn from the target composition at
sampling time, so the histogram matches by construction, and we report FID alone. All numbers are
means with standard errors over seeds.

D.4
Synthetic perturbation shift generalization
D.4.1
Experimental details
Task.
Data arrive as pairs (c, y) of a context c and a perturbation y, and a generative model
approximates p(x | c, y), mirroring Sec. 4.4 in a controlled setting where the ground truth is known.
Contexts shift the Gaussian mean, and perturbations add a fixed offset shared across contexts, so
perturbation effects are transferable by construction.
The update introduces a new context but
withholds one perturbation within it, leaving a single combination (c⋆, y⋆) never observed in either
phase. Recovering it requires (I) retaining the perturbation direction learned on historical contexts and
(II) fitting the basal position of the new one, so held-out error reads out the balance between the two
rather than either alone.
Data. We build a 6 × 4 grid of conditions in R2: six contexts c placed on a hexagon of radius 4, and
four perturbations y applied as an additive offset of scale 1.5 shared across contexts, with y = 0 the
unperturbed control. Each cell (c, y) is an isotropic Gaussian with σ = 0.25, providing 800 training and
200 validation points, and the single held-out cell provides 1,000 test points, with data seed 0.
Stream. Pretraining uses contexts 0–4 with all four perturbations. The update introduces context 5
with perturbations 0–2 only, so the held-out combination is (c⋆, y⋆) = (5, 3).
Model. The velocity field is a conditional MLP of width 256 and depth 4, with a 64-dimensional
sinusoidal time embedding and 32-dimensional learned embeddings for context and perturbation, trained
on the linear noise-to-data interpolant. Sampling uses Euler at step size 0.05 (20 evaluations).
Pretraining. The context-0–4 model trains for 1,000 epochs with batch size 1024 and Adam at
3 × 10−4, with one checkpoint per seed.
Update. Each update trains for 2,000 epochs with the same optimizer settings, for seeds {10, 20, 30, 40, 50}
at every λ in the grid, each loading the pretraining checkpoint of its own seed. We compare naive
fine-tuning against generative and trajectory replay, sweeping λ ∈{0.05, 0.1, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 0.9, 0.95}.
Replay conditions are drawn from the pretraining support, so no data from the held-out combination
enters training at any point.
Evaluation. After the update, we generate 500 samples conditioned on (c⋆, y⋆) and report energy
distance to that cell’s held-out test set, with the naive fine-tuning value indicated for reference. Lower
is better.
D.4.2
Results
Fig. 21a shows the layout: contexts separate cleanly, and the shared perturbation offsets give each
context the same internal geometry, so the held-out combination has a well-defined target location.
Fig. 21b traces held-out energy distance as a function of λ. Both replay methods trace a U, with a
minimum near λ = 0.5 that is more than an order of magnitude below naive fine-tuning, which has no
mechanism for retaining the perturbation direction. The shape is the point: held-out error rises at both
extremes, so the unseen combination is recovered neither by staying close to the historical model nor by
fitting the incoming context alone, but only where the update balances the two. The two replay methods
are indistinguishable at the optimum and separate only at high λ, where trajectory replay degrades faster.
Fig. 21c shows the balance directly: the generated population coincides with the held-out target at
λ = 0.5 and is displaced from it at either extreme. Recovering an unseen combination therefore requires
fitting the incoming context without discarding the transferable structure carried from the historical ones.

D.5
Application: perturbation modeling in single-cell transcriptomics
D.5.1
Experimental details
Datasets. We use two perturbation datasets whose context variable is a biological or technical batch
rather than a semantic class. Throughout, a stratum is one context-by-perturbation combination, the
unit at which conditional distributions are modeled and at which every metric below is computed.
Exact values for every setting are in Tab. 4.
• Replogle-Nadig (Replogle et al., 2022; Nadig et al., 2025). Genome-scale CRISPRi screens in which
the context is the cell line (Jurkat, K562, RPE1, HepG2) and the perturbation is the targeted gene,
2,023 of them plus a non-targeting control.
• PBMC cytokine screen (Oesinghaus et al., 2025). Cytokine stimulations in which the context is
the donor, twelve in total, and the perturbation is the applied cytokine, 90 of them plus a PBS
(phosphate-buffered saline) control. Cells additionally carry one of 18 annotated cell types, used as a
covariate rather than as a context.
• Preprocessing. Following Adduri et al. (2026), both are restricted to the 2,000 highly variable genes,
library-size normalized to 104 counts and log(1+x) transformed, with no further scaling.
• Splits. Split labels follow the few-shot definitions of Adduri et al. (2026): cells in non-few-shot
contexts are training data, while in few-shot contexts the perturbations on that context’s test list
become held-out test cells and the remainder fine-tuning data. Controls stay trainable everywhere.
• Latent space. To avoid leakage, flow matching operates on a zero-centered PCA basis fit only on
task-0 training cells, so later contexts never influence the coordinate system. We use 50 components
on Replogle and 100 on PBMC, retaining 34.2% and 58.4% of the variance (see Tab. 5).
• Decoding. Latents return to gene space through a per-task decoder Dk composing the tied task-0
PCA inverse with a per-gene affine calibration (ak,g, bk,g) fit by scalar least squares on that task’s
training cells, so that Dk(z) = Ak(W⊤z + µ) + bk with Ak diagonal. A single tied inverse cannot
absorb the per-task shift in gene scale, which the calibration corrects, but being diagonal, it cannot
recover variance discarded by the truncation to d components: it fixes gene scale, not the subspace.
The bank is fit once per dataset, frozen, and shared by every method and seed, so no part of the
reported gap can come from a better decoder. A qualitative reconstruction display on the pooled
later-task datasets is in Fig. 26.
Streams. Replogle is a two-task stream: task 0 trains jointly on Jurkat, K562, and RPE1, and task 1
introduces HepG2. PBMC is a five-task stream over eight pretraining donors followed by Donor1,
Donor4, Donor9, and Donor12. The perturbation vocabulary is shared across contexts, so each task
adds a context row rather than a new label space. The model conditions on context and perturbation
embeddings, plus a cell-type covariate on PBMC. Task 0 is trained once per seed and shared by every
method.
Model and training. The velocity field is an MLP over PCA latents with 128-dimensional sinusoidal
time and condition embeddings concatenated to the input, trained on the linear noise-to-data interpolant
with target x1 −x0. Replogle uses a flat SiLU MLP of width 2048 and depth 4, and PBMC uses a
pre-normalized residual MLP of width 2048 and depth 6. Both were fixed by the two-stage selection
study in Tab. 5.
• PCA width (panel a), from single-seed pilots at a fixed 1024 × 4 backbone. The fit falls monotonically
with width on both datasets, giving 50 components on Replogle, while on PBMC 50 and 100 are
within a thousandth of each other, and we take 100 following Klein et al. (2025).
• Backbone (panel b), sweeping width, depth, and residual connections under the final sequential
protocol and selecting on task-0 held-out ∆. The flat MLP wins on Replogle and the smaller residual
model on PBMC.
Both selection studies use seed 42 alone, and no method is tuned against them afterwards. Every
method uses Adam at 10−4 with batch size 1024, under the budgets in Tab. 4, for seeds {42, 69, 1714}.


Replay. Replay follows the same formulation as in the image experiments, with the teacher integrated
by Euler at step size 0.05 and equal-task weighting setting λk = k/(k + 1). Two things differ here.
Replay conditions are sampled proportionally to the stratum frequencies of the old training pool, since
the conditioning space is a context-by-perturbation grid rather than a class label. The adaptive policy
selects from λ ∈{0.25, 0.5, 0.75} by a balanced Pearson ∆, the unweighted mean of the old-task and
new-task stratum macros, scored on the training strata of every task seen so far with K = 200 generated
cells per stratum. The MeanFlow backends distill the task-0 teacher with logit-normal time sampling
(µ = 0.4, σ = 1.0) and diagonal fraction 0.25, under the same step budget as task-0 training itself
(Tab. 4), then replay with N ∈{1, 4} jumps.

Baselines. All baselines share the stream, backbone, budget, decoder bank, and evaluation protocol,
differing only in what is updated or replayed.
• Naive fine-tuning trains on the incoming context alone, sweeping three independent freeze switches:
whether the velocity field trains, whether perturbation rows seen earlier train, and whether earlier
context rows train. Rows first appearing on the new task always train.
• LoRA adapts the MLP with rank r ∈{4, 8, 16} and α = 2r, leaving the output layer frozen and
merging the adapter once the task finishes.
• Gradual fine-tuning self-distills from the frozen previous model with a distillation weight annealed
from 1 to 0, under sigmoid and linear schedules.
• Two-stage fine-tuning spends the first 10% or 50% of the task training only on the new context
embedding row, then unfreezes the full field.
• Real replay mixes stored real cells from earlier tasks into each batch, a privileged reference rather
than a competing method because it retains raw data.
Generative and trajectory replay are run with all three teacher backends and both λ policies.
Evaluation. All numbers come from a single evaluation of the final checkpoint, reported on four tracks.
• Retention, the macro over the task-0 training strata: Jurkat, K562 and RPE1 on Replogle, the eight
pretraining donors on PBMC.
• Fit, the unweighted mean of the per-context macros for every context introduced sequentially
thereafter: HepG2 on Replogle, and Donor1, Donor4, Donor9 and Donor12 on PBMC.
• Stream, the union of the two above (reported, for example, in Fig. 25).
• Held-out, the test perturbations of every context seen so far.
We generate K = 500 cells per non-control stratum and take the basal from decoded observed control
latents rather than from generated controls, so only the treated side is synthesized. Following the
CellEval metric suite in Adduri et al. (2026), the headline metric is Pearson ∆, the correlation between
predicted and observed perturbation-minus-control profiles, macro-averaged over strata. We additionally
report DE Overlap@N, the fraction of the N observed significant differentially expressed genes recovered
among the top-N predicted, with N set per perturbation. Results are means with standard errors over
seeds.
D.5.2
Additional results
Velocity model selection and full results. Tab. 5 reports the PCA dimension and backbone sweeps,
both run on task 0 alone to avoid leakage. Fit falls monotonically with PCA width on both datasets
(panel a), since wider bases spend capacity on directions that carry little perturbation signal. On
Replogle, the 50-component basis is clearly best, whereas on PBMC 50 and 100 are within a thousandth
of each other, and we take 100 following Klein et al. (2025). The backbone sweep (panel b) separates
the two datasets: on Replogle, the flat MLP wins on fit and task-0 held-out with retention tied within a
thousandth, while on PBMC the residual backbone is ahead on both retention and held-out, and among
residual configurations the smaller 2048×6 model gives up little on retention while scoring higher on
held-out, so we keep it.
Tabs. 6 and 7 give every configuration behind Fig. 4. On the single Replogle update, the replay families
are close, with trajectory replay retaining slightly more than generative replay (0.604–0.620 against
0.573–0.594 Pearson ∆) and held-out tied at 0.39–0.41. Over the four sequential updates of PBMC, the
gap widens on every axis, to 0.430–0.451 retention against 0.306–0.403 and 0.332–0.350 held-out against
0.289–0.330, placing trajectory replay level with real replay on fit and held-out despite storing no cells.
The MeanFlow backends leave it unchanged on both streams but cost generative replay substantially
on PBMC, where MF-1 retention drops to 0.306.


The freeze grid explains why a memory-free non-replay baseline can still top the retention column.
Retention depends almost entirely on the velocity field, and adaptation almost entirely on it too, so the
three switches are not three comparable knobs.
• Field frozen, old embeddings frozen (vf=0, pert=0, ctx=0). Only the new context row is learned, so
task 0 is preserved almost exactly, and PBMC retention is the highest of any memory-free method at
0.491. A single embedding vector cannot express the new context’s perturbation responses, so fit
stalls at 0.233 against 0.515 for trajectory replay.
• Field frozen, old embeddings trainable (vf=0, pert=1, ctx=1). The old rows drift to compensate for
a field that cannot move, costing retention (0.400) for no fit at all (0.232) on PBMC. On Replogle,
the same relaxation does buy fit, 0.359 against 0.193, at a smaller retention cost.
• Field trainable (vf=1, any embedding setting). Fit recovers to 0.289 and retention collapses to
0.056–0.064, while the embedding switches change neither, confirming that the field rather than the
conditioning rows carries the historical task.
Freezing therefore buys retention only by refusing to adapt, which is why the two axes have to be read
jointly rather than ranked one column at a time.

Alternative metrics. Fig. 22 repeats the fit-retention view under two metrics from Adduri et al.
(2026) that stress different failure modes. L1 discrimination (top) ranks each predicted profile against
the observed profiles of all strata by L1 distance and reports how far its own target sits in that ranking,
so it rewards predictions that are specific to their condition rather than merely close to the average
response. Residual Pearson ∆(bottom) subtracts the mean perturbation effect across strata before
correlating, leaving only the context-specific deviation, so a method predicting the shared response
alone scores near zero. The layout is unchanged under both: freezing sits top-left, the adapters and
fine-tuning variants low on retention, and the replay family in the upper-right corner with trajectory
replay adjacent to real replay. Residual ∆matters most, since it rules out the reading that all methods
recover the same global shift and differ only in how much of it they keep.

D.6
Application: Continual phenotypic modeling in microscopy
D.6.1
Experimental details
Data. We use RxRx1 (Sypetkowski et al., 2023), a fluorescence-microscopy screen of gene knockdowns
acquired in separate experimental batches, following the dataset choice, preprocessing pipeline, and
modeling approach of Zhang et al. (2025) and Jones et al. (2026). Exact values for every setting below
are in Tab. 8.
• Content.
We restrict to the U2OS cell line as Zhang et al. (2025), spanning three acquisition
experiments and 1,071 perturbation identities, of which 100 form the official evaluation subset. Since
every crop is U2OS, cell-type conditioning carries no information and is disabled.
• Crops. Each sample is a 96×96 crop with all six fluorescence channels retained, modeled natively
rather than as an RGB projection or a latent code.
• Normalization. Pixels are mapped to [−1, 1]. At training time, we add uniform dequantization noise
to avoid fitting the quantization lattice, and apply random flips. Neither is applied at evaluation.
• Conditioning. The model receives perturbation and experiment identity as independent learned
embeddings. Experiment identity is an observed acquisition covariate, not a phenotype.
• Channel projection. Generated and real tensors are mapped to RGB through the same fixed linear
projection and re-quantized before any metric, so the comparison never mixes projections.
Stream. Tasks arrive in the fixed order U2OS-01 →U2OS-02 →U2OS-03, so the shift is technical
rather than semantic: the perturbation vocabulary is identical in every task and only the acquisition
batch changes. A deterministic 10% of each experiment’s training rows is held out for validation and λ
selection, and the official test rows are never trained on, replayed from, or used for selection. Task 0 is
trained once per seed and shared by every method within each objective family, the flow matching and
the diffusion one, so all differences arise from the updates.
Model and training. The velocity field is the ADM U-Net (Dhariwal & Nichol, 2021) adapted
to 96×96×6 inputs, trained on the linear noise-to-data interpolant with target x1 −x0. Every task
receives the same budget of 25,000 optimizer steps with Adam and a power-function EMA (Karras
et al., 2024), and all reported samples come from the EMA weights.
Replay. Generative and trajectory replay follow the same formulation as in the image experiments
(Sec. D.3), with the teacher integrated by Euler at step size 0.05. Equal-task weighting gives λ = 1/2
at task 1 and 2/3 at task 2, and the adaptive policy selects from {0.25, 0.5, 0.75} by macro validation
FID across the experiments seen so far. As in the image streams, we also ask whether a distilled mean
velocity can replace the ODE teacher: we distill a MeanFlow model at task 0 for 25,000 steps, matching
the per-task budget but at batch size 16 rather than 64, and replay with N ∈{1, 4} jumps. Here this
is restricted to trajectory replay under equal-task weighting, since the MeanFlow residual requires a
Jacobian-vector product whose activation footprint scales with the 96×96×6 state, making co-training
substantially more expensive than at 32×32. The reduced batch is a direct consequence of the same
cost.
Baselines. We report four reference points, all on the same stream, per-task budget, backbone, and
evaluation protocol as our methods (Tab. 8).
• Joint training retrains on the union of all real data seen so far, storing every past sample, and is
therefore an upper target rather than a competing method.
• Real replay mixes stored historical images into each batch. It is likewise privileged but respects the
sequential budget, isolating the cost of replaying generated rather than stored samples.
• Naive fine-tuning continues training on the incoming experiment alone with no retention mechanism,
giving the lower target.
• Generative distillation (diffusion) (Masip et al., 2025) replays samples from the frozen previous-task
model but supervises them with the teacher’s own noise prediction at a randomly drawn timestep
rather than with the injected noise, so the student matches the teacher’s denoising behavior along the

Stream evolution. Fig. 27 tracks the same quantities after each update, and panel a shows the final
checkpoint hiding a non-monotonic effect. Naive fine-tuning loses U2OS-01 at the first update, rising
to roughly 97 FID, then falls back to 36.0, nominally the best memory-free value in that column. This
is not retention but a similarity artifact: U2OS-01 and U2OS-03 are visually close in illumination
and staining intensity, whereas U2OS-02 is the outlier (Fig. 29), so training on the third experiment
partially restores the first. The excursion in between is the real measurement, and its size, a threefold
FID increase in a single update, is what makes the point about forgetting under a purely technical
shift. Both replay families avoid it, with trajectory replay flat across the stream and generative replay
drifting upward from the second update. Panel b shows the pooled metric over a growing reference set,
where trajectory replay separates at the first update and holds the margin.
