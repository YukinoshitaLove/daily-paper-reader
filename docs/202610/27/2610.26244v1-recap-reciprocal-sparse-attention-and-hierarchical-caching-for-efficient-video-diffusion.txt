ABSTRACT
Diffusion transformers generate high-quality videos but remain computationally
expensive. Sparse attention and temporal caching reduce computation within and
across denoising steps, respectively, offering a natural composition. However,
predictions produced with sparse attention become sources for temporal reuse,
coupling their errors along the sampling trajectory. We observe that improving the
accuracy of these sources recovers fidelity without changing the reuse schedule,
and that sparsification errors remain directionally aligned across nearby steps,
suggesting that correction information can be shared rather than recomputed. These
observations motivate RECAP, a training-free method that couples sparse attention
with hierarchical caching through reciprocal correction, adaptively coordinated
by online error feedback. Cached dense refinement corrects omitted interactions
in current sparse estimates, while fresh sparse statistics track input changes and
regulate the aging correction. The resulting predictions then renew the sources
for whole-step prediction reuse, allowing prediction history to advance between
dense refreshes. We evaluate RECAP on multiple video diffusion models against
sparse-attention methods, caching methods, and their direct compositions. On
HunyuanVideo at 720p 5-second generation, RECAP achieves a 3.05× speedup
and outperforms all tested direct compositions in both fidelity and inference speed.
Code is available at https://github.com/lyraxtg/RECAP.

INTRODUCTION
Diffusion transformers have emerged as a powerful architecture for visual generation (Peebles &
Xie, 2023), but the quadratic complexity of attention and repeated transformer evaluations make
inference expensive. Video generation is especially costly due to the length of its spatiotemporal
token sequences (Yang et al., 2025b; Kong et al., 2024). Sparse attention reduces the interactions
computed within each denoising step (Xi et al., 2025; Hu et al., 2026), while temporal caching reuses
features or predictions across steps (Liu et al., 2025a). Since the two approaches save computation

along different axes, a natural way to accelerate further is to compose them directly. In such a
composition, however, sparse-attention predictions become the sources that caching reuses in later
steps. Sparsification error therefore affects both the current sampler update and subsequent reused
predictions, making the accuracy of these sources central to the speed–fidelity trade-off.
We study this coupling in direct compositions and make two observations. First, source accuracy
matters for fidelity. When we replace the cached tensors with dense counterparts while keeping
the reuse schedule and the sparse predictions used by the sampler, the deviation from the dense
trajectory decreases. Computing every source densely, however, would forfeit the savings of sparse
attention, so we ask whether correction can be shared across steps. Second, we find that sparsification
errors remain strongly aligned across nearby denoising steps. This persistence suggests that a single
dense correction can serve several sparse evaluations, whose corrected predictions then provide more
accurate sources for temporal reuse. Sparse attention and caching should therefore correct each other
rather than act independently, as Fig. 2 illustrates. Section 3.1 details both analyses.
Motivated by these observations, we propose RECAP, which connects sparse attention and a two-level
cache hierarchy through reciprocal correction, as illustrated in Fig. 3. At the attention level, a cached
dense refinement supplies the detail that the current sparse estimate leaves unresolved. As attention
inputs evolve across steps, fresh sparse statistics in turn regulate how strongly this aging refinement
is applied, so that cached and current information correct each other. At the prediction level, the
corrected outputs renew a prediction cache, from which later steps are extrapolated without another
transformer evaluation. Sparse computation thus links the two levels, and one dense refresh can
support several sparse evaluations together with the prediction reuse that follows them.
This design also naturally supplies the feedback for adaptive online scheduling. Each computed
prediction reveals the error of the extrapolation it replaces, and each dense refresh reveals the error of
the refinement it renews. Since corrected sparse evaluations renew prediction history while reusing
existing refinement, the two caches can be renewed at different rates. The prediction error thus
decides when to compute a new prediction, and the refinement error decides whether this computation
needs a dense refresh. Under a fixed budget, these decisions choose among prediction reuse, corrected
sparse computation, and dense refresh, adapting the schedule to each sample without offline search.
Our contributions are summarized as follows:
• We identify source accuracy and temporal persistence of sparsification error as key opportunities
to improve sparse–cache composition. More accurate cache sources recover fidelity, while
aligned errors across steps motivate sharing dense correction across sparse evaluations.
• We propose RECAP, which couples sparse attention with hierarchical caching through reciprocal
correction. Cached dense refinement corrects sparse attention, corrected predictions renew the
sources for whole-step reuse, and online error feedback decides when each cache is renewed.
• We evaluate RECAP on three video diffusion models, where it improves speed–fidelity trade-
offs over individual accelerators and direct sparse–cache compositions. On HunyuanVideo, it
achieves a 3.05× speedup with higher fidelity than all tested compositions, as Fig. 1 shows.

3.1
MOTIVATION
Background. At denoising step t, a diffusion transformer predicts pt = fθ(xt, t, c) from latent
xt and conditioning c, and the sampler advances as xt+1 = Ψt(xt, pt). Sparse attention reduces
computation within each evaluation by omitting or approximating query-key interactions, while
caching reuses earlier features or predictions across steps. These complementary savings make their
combination a natural way to further accelerate sampling. However, sparse outputs also become
sources for subsequent reuse, so sparsification error can affect both the current sampler update and
later steps. This coupling motivates a closer examination of how the two mechanisms interact.
Composition error and source accuracy. We sweep the settings of DFSAttn, TeaCache, and their
direct composition in Fig. 4a, finding that combining the two approximations need not cause a
disproportionate loss of fidelity. If the two errors simply accumulated, the composed error would
match the vector sum of the standalone errors. Instead, it falls below this sum in 103 of 104
configurations in Fig. 4b, revealing a favorable interaction between sparse attention and caching.

To reduce the remaining error, we focus on source accuracy, since sparse evaluations supply the
sources of subsequent reuse. In DFSAttn with TeaCache, we replace cached tensors with dense
counterparts computed on the same inputs, while retaining the sparse predictions for sampler updates
and the reuse schedule. As Fig. 4c shows, this reduces latent deviation from the matched dense
trajectory, demonstrating that more accurate cache sources can improve the fidelity of the composition.
Persistence of sparsification error. Computing every source densely, however, would forfeit the
savings of sparse attention. We therefore ask whether correction can instead be shared across steps.
Along dense trajectories, we compare sparse and dense predictions on identical inputs to isolate
sparsification error from trajectory drift. Figure 4d shows that these errors remain strongly aligned
across nearby steps for all thresholds, so a correction measured at the source step still points in the
right direction at later steps. This suggests that a single dense correction can support multiple sparse
evaluations by combining a fresh sparse estimate with cached detail that is costly to recompute.
Method overview. RECAP connects these insights through a two-level cache hierarchy, as illustrated
in Fig. 3. The persistence of sparsification error motivates sharing dense correction across sparse
evaluations, which we develop as attention-level refinement in Sec. 3.2. The resulting improved
predictions then serve as more accurate sources for whole-step temporal reuse in Sec. 3.3. Finally,
Sec. 3.4 coordinates renewal at both levels through online error feedback. At each step, RECAP either
reuses an extrapolated prediction, performs corrected sparse computation, or refreshes densely.
3.2
ATTENTION-LEVEL CACHING FOR SPARSE RECONSTRUCTION
The source-accuracy findings in Sec. 3.1 motivate improving sparse predictions before reuse, and the
temporal alignment of sparsification error suggests sharing correction across steps. Since this error
originates in the omitted interactions, we realize both ideas at the attention level, where a fresh coarse
estimate tracks changes in omitted attention and cached dense refinement supplies the discrepancy
that this estimate leaves unresolved. Both are maintained separately for each layer and attention head.

EXPERIMENTS
4.1
EXPERIMENTAL SETUP
Models and baselines.
We evaluate RECAP on CogVideoX-1.5-5B (Yang et al., 2025b),
HunyuanVideo-13B (Kong et al., 2024), and Wan2.1-1.3B (Team Wan et al., 2025), each with
50 steps. Wan2.1 results are in Appendix Sec. E.1. Baselines include SVG (Xi et al., 2025), Re-
ttention (Chen et al., 2025), SVOO (Luo et al., 2026), and DFSAttn (Hu et al., 2026) for sparse
attention and TeaCache (Liu et al., 2025a), LeMiCa (Gao et al., 2025), and NaviCache (Lv et al., 2026)
for caching. Pairing SVOO and DFSAttn with each caching baseline yields six direct compositions.
Appendix Sec. D extends RECAP to FLUX.1-dev (Black Forest Labs, 2024) image generation. For
image generation, we additionally compare with the caching method Spectrum (Han et al., 2026).
Evaluation metrics. We use optimized VBench prompts (Huang et al., 2024), with initial noise,
guidance, and sampler settings matched across methods within each comparison. Fidelity to dense gen-
eration is measured using PSNR (Horé & Ziou, 2010), SSIM (Wang et al., 2004), and LPIPS (Zhang
et al., 2018) on paired lossless frames. CLIP-IQA+ (Wang et al., 2023) and VBench (Huang et al.,
2024) assess generation quality. For VBench, we average six quality dimensions after its official
per-dimension normalization, as detailed in Appendix Sec. E.2. The main tables also report the
individual imaging quality (IQ), aesthetic quality (AQ), and temporal flickering (TF) scores.
Implementation details. The main experiments on CogVideoX-1.5 and Wan2.1-1.3B are conducted
on NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPUs, while those on HunyuanVideo use NVIDIA A800 GPUs. We use
FlashAttention-2 (Dao, 2024) for dense refreshes, the Block Sparse Attention library (Guo et al., 2024)
for selected interactions, and custom Triton kernels (Tillet et al., 2019) for coarse-tail estimation and
refinement. RECAP requires no training or model fine-tuning. We select its computation budget and
error tolerances using the lightweight parameter selection procedure in Appendix Sec. C.4. Appendix
Sec. C provides the full parameter settings for each model and describes the memory optimizations.

4.3
ABLATION STUDIES
We ablate each component of RECAP on CogVideoX-1.5, namely attention reconstruction, prediction
reuse, and online scheduling. Each variant changes one component with all other settings fixed.
Appendix Sec. E.4 repeats every ablation at the fast setting of Tab. 2, with the same conclusions.
Attention reconstruction. The reconstruction in Sec. 3.2 pairs fresh coarse estimates with cached
dense refinement. Removing the refinement at the same computation locations lowers PSNR from
36.15 to 34.57 dB in Tab. 3a. Freezing the initial refinement and partition lowers it further to 30.12 dB.
The refinement thus supplies detail missing from the coarse estimate and must be renewed during
sampling. Adapted PISA (Li et al., 2026) and Re-ttention (Chen et al., 2025) change the cost of
each sparse evaluation. Table 3b therefore compares them with our reconstruction both at the same
locations and at matched runtime. RECAP leads in both, by 4.31 and 7.47 dB at matched runtime.
Prediction reuse. Section 3.3 extrapolates reused steps from a prediction history that corrected
sparse evaluations also renew. Restricting this renewal to dense refreshes lowers PSNR from 36.15 to
31.80 dB in Tab. 3c, showing that corrected sparse evaluations are valuable reuse sources. Disabling
reuse and computing all 50 steps raises PSNR to 38.79 dB, but latency grows from 619.69 to 845.51 s,
so reuse saves 27% of runtime. At matched latency, RECAP instead reaches 41.45 dB in Tab. 3d.
Online scheduling. The scheduler in Sec. 3.4 places computed steps by prediction error, concentrating
them where predictions change quickly. Spacing the same number of steps evenly lowers PSNR from
36.15 to 35.70 dB in Tab. 3c. Error-guided placement thus improves fidelity at no extra cost.
5
CONCLUSION
We presented RECAP, a training-free method that couples sparse attention and hierarchical caching
through reciprocal correction instead of composing them directly. We showed that the accuracy of
cache sources affects the fidelity of the composition and that sparsification errors persist across nearby
steps. Building on these findings, RECAP maintains a two-level cache hierarchy. At the attention level,
cached dense refinement corrects sparse attention, while fresh sparse statistics regulate how strongly
this aging refinement is applied. At the prediction level, corrected predictions renew the sources for
whole-step reuse. Online error feedback decides when to compute and when to refresh densely, so the
schedule adapts to each sample without costly offline search. Across three video diffusion models,
RECAP improves the speed–fidelity trade-off over individual accelerators and direct compositions,
reaching a 3.05× speedup on HunyuanVideo with higher fidelity than all tested compositions.
