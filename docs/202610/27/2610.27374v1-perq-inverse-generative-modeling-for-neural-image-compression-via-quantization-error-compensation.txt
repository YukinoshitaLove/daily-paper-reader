Abstract
Neural image codecs face a fundamental trade-off between pixel fidelity and percep-
tual quality. Distortion-optimized codecs preserve pixel-wise fidelity but produce
blurred reconstructions at low bitrates, whereas generative codecs leverage learned
priors to improve perceptual quality but tend to exhibit saturating PSNR at higher
bitrates and require separate models for different bitrate targets. We propose PerQ,
which reframes perceptual reconstruction as inverse generative modeling with
bounded support. Specifically, we define the quantization cell as the set of latents
that yield the same transmitted symbols and derive a transport bound showing that
reconstructions from this cell remain within a rate-determined radius of the codec
output. This motivates performing generative reconstruction within the codec-
induced quantization cell, rather than sampling from an unconstrained image prior.
PerQ implements this principle by attaching a rate-conditioned flow-matching com-
pensator to a frozen, multi-rate distortion-optimized backbone, enabling perceptual
enhancement while preserving the backbone’s coding performance. Experiments
on Kodak and CLIC2020 show that PerQ achieves perceptual performance compa-
rable to generative codecs such as MS-ILLM in LPIPS and FID, while maintaining
competitive PSNR relative to distortion-optimized codecs such as ELIC. A sin-
gle model checkpoint supports a wide bitrate range with fast decoding, and the
compensator provides consistent gains across three different backbones. The
implementation is available at https://jasminepp.github.io/PerQ/.

Introduction
Image compression has long been a fundamental research topic in signal processing and a core
component of modern visual data infrastructure [11]. Conventional image codecs such as JPEG [55],
BPG [5], and the intra modes of AV1 [19] and VVC [10], have been widely adopted over the past
decades. More recently, end-to-end learned image compression [3, 4, 44] have achieved competitive
or superior rate-distortion performance compared with conventional codecs based on pixel-wise
metrics, such as PSNR [57]. However, optimizing pixel-wise distortion does not necessarily yield
perceptually pleasing reconstructions, especially at low bitrates, where severe bitrate constraints limit
the preservation of fine details and high-frequency image content, often resulting in visually blurred
reconstructions. This limitation has motivated growing interest in perceptual image compression,
which prioritizes perceptual fidelity over pixel-wise similarity and has led to the development of
perceptual and generative image codecs.
Recent generative codecs have demonstrated promising perceptual quality at extremely low bitrates
[27, 62]. However, extending this perceptual advantage to practical deployment remains challenging.

source pixels, but most generative codecs, whose reconstructions are dominated by a generative prior,

We ask, Can these competing objectives be reconciled within a single codec without sacrificing the
advantages of distortion-optimized compression? We propose PerQ, an image codec that reframes
perceptual reconstruction as inverse generative modeling with bounded support, leveraging the
quantization structure of an existing codec to constrain perceptual refinement. We characterize the set
of latent representations that yield the same transmitted symbols as a quantization cell and derive a
transport bound relating the quantization step to a rate-determined radius in pixel space. Unlike prior
approaches that augment distortion-optimized backbones with perceptual losses [21, 31, 43] and
use learned generative priors to explore the image manifold, PerQ employs a flow-matching-based
compensator as a decoder-side plug-in to improve perceptual quality while keeping the refined
latent within this bounded region. Integrated into the decoder, the compensator leaves the bitstream
unchanged and supports the backbone’s existing multi-rate operating range with a single model,
while retaining fast decoding. Importantly, the derived transport bound guarantees that the refined
reconstruction remains within a rate-determined pixel-space radius of the backbone’s output, even
with perceptual optimization. This design brings together the perceptual advantages of generative
codecs, the pixel fidelity of distortion-optimized codecs, and broad bitrate coverage within a single
model (Figure 1). The main contributions of this work are:
• (C1) Inverse generative modeling with bounded support. We formulate perceptual reconstruction
as inverse generative modeling over a rate-determined bounded support, characterized by a transport
bound (§3.1) that relates the quantization step to a pixel-space radius around the output of a multi-
rate distortion-optimized codec.
• (C2) A rate-conditioned flow-matching compensator design is designed (§3.3) for perceptual
reconstruction within the bounded support. A single checkpoint supports multiple backbone rates,
while the decoder-side design preserves the bitstream and generalizes across different backbones.
• (C3) PerQ: A perceptual-fidelity-balanced image codec integrates a bounded-support compen-
sator with a frozen distortion-optimized backbone, bridging the gap between generative perceptual
codecs and distortion-oriented codecs by combining strong perceptual quality with competitive
pixel fidelity in a single, multi-rate model.
Extensive experiments on Kodak and CLIC2020-test (Table 1) show that PerQ retains most of the
perceptual gains of strong generative codecs such as MS-ILLM while achieving competitive pixel
fidelity against VVC (VTM intra), while enabling efficient four-step decoding at 86 ms/MP. Moreover,
a single checkpoint covers nearly two orders of magnitude in bitrate, and the same compensator
delivers consistent gains across two additional backbones. These results demonstrate that PerQ
balances perceptual quality, pixel fidelity, and bitrate flexibility within a single, efficient codec.

Related Work
Learned image compression originates from the joint optimization of an analysis/synthesis transform
and a learned entropy model [51, 3], with subsequent progress driven primarily by more effective
entropy models and stronger transforms. Hyperpriors capture spatially-varying scale [4]; discretized
Gaussian mixture likelihoods refine the conditional entropy model [13]; channel-wise autoregressive
factorizations sharpen it further [44]; checkerboard contexts parallelize spatial autoregression [22];
and recent designs combine grouped channel attention [20] with multi-reference context [29, 28] or
transformer-CNN hybrid transforms [38]. The best performing neural image codecs match or exceed
VVC all intra mode on both PSNR and MS-SSIM, with real-time variants closing the latency gap
[25], and they remain visually faithful across moderate to high bitrates. The picture changes at low
rates, where the bit budget is limited to preserve high-frequency content, and reconstructions become
visibly blurred. The results in [8, 9] show that the divergence between source and reconstruction
marginals widens at low rates and cannot be closed with further distortion-oriented training.
Generative image codecs are designed to primarily target lower bitrates by conditioning recon-
structions on a generative prior trained to match natural-image statistics, trading pixel-wise fidelity
for perceptual realism. GAN-based codecs integrate adversarial training into the rate-distortion
objective [52, 2, 43, 46, 21], with subsequent work exposing the distortion-perception trade-off
during decoding [1, 31] and obtaining variable-rate operation from a single codec [34]. Alternatively,
diffusion-based codecs employ learned denoising at the decoder, with early work casting compression
as reverse channel coding of noise samples through a Gaussian diffusion process [50]. Follow-up
studies generalize this to pretrained foundation priors [54] and perform multi-rate operations on top
of a frozen prior [27]. A separate line trains a conditional diffusion model as the codec decoder. CDC
[59] replaces the VAE decoder with a conditional diffusion model, PerCo [12] couples a spatial latent
with a global textual code for ultra-low-rate decoding, and other designs guide a frozen text-to-image
or semantic prior [33, 36, 48, 24] with compressed content. These designs typically incur multi-step
sampling costs during decoding. More recent work distills the iterative process into a single step
[26, 63, 18, 58, 62]. Since reconstruction is conditioned on the generative prior rather than driven by
the bitstream alone, pixel-space fidelity saturates as bpp increases, and PSNR improves sub-linearly
with rate. Moreover, most designs commit to a single rate point at training time, requiring a separate
model per operating point on the rate-distortion curve.

Method
As mentioned in Section 1, unconstrained generative compression typically conditions reconstructions
on a learned image prior, often incurring a penalty in pixel-wise fidelity as a result. This design is
acceptable in applications that can tolerate hallucinated details and deviations from source fidelity, as
3


opposed to distortion-oriented codecs. In this paper, we aim to close the perceptual gap of distortion-
optimized image codecs while preserving reconstructions that remain faithful to the source. To
this end, we constrain perceptual reconstruction within the quantization-error space induced by the
compression backbone. In this section, we first derive the structural properties of this constraint in
§3.1 and §3.2. We then propose a flow-matching based compensator in §3.3, which is integrated with
a compression codec backbone (§3.3.2). We describe the training methodology in §3.4.

Experimental Configurations
Implementation Details.
We use the intra-frame model of DCVC-RT [25] as our default compression backbone, supporting
64 rate points, with the architectural modifications described in Section 3.3.2. We trained it from
scratch on the MLIC training dataset [29, 28], with a batch size of 64 for 1M steps in the first stage
(backbone training) and a batch size of 16 for 200K steps in the second stage (compensator training).
More training details and configurations are provided in the Appendix B.
Evaluation. We evaluate PerQ on Kodak [16] (24 images at 768 × 512), CLIC2020-test [14] (428
images at ∼2K resolution). We measure pixel fidelity using PSNR in RGB space, and perceptual
quality using LPIPS [61], DISTS [15], and FID [23] computed on 256×256 patches following [46].
Benchmark Methods. We compare against four baseline categories. The conventional reference is
VVC (VTM-22.0 intra) [10]. For distortion-optimal learned codecs we include ELIC [20] (PSNR
anchor), EVC [56] and DCVC-RT intra [25]. For GAN-based generative codecs we include MS-
ILLM [46] (perceptual anchor), HiFiC [43], and Control-GIC [34]. For diffusion-based generative
codecs, we include PerCo [12], CDC [59], DiffEIC [36], StableCodec [63], and OSCAR [18]. All
neural baselines are re-run from publicly available checkpoints. Reconstructions are generated on a
single NVIDIA-4090 GPU, except for DiffEIC [36], whose 50-step decoding is prohibitively slow on
the 4090 and is therefore generated on NVIDIA-H200. Decoding latency and complexity reported in
Table 1 are measured on a single NVIDIA-4090 GPU for fair comparison.

Results and Discussion
Quantitative comparison. Table 1 and Figure 4 position PerQ across the deployment Pareto frontier
on the Kodak and CLIC2020-test datasets. With a single checkpoint, PerQ performs close to ELIC on
PSNR (−1.6% vs. −2.0% BD-rate against VVC on Kodak, +2.7% vs. −1.9% on CLIC2020-test)
while achieving large perceptual gains (−57.4% in LPIPS, −63.2% in DISTS and −67.9% in FID
on Kodak, and −70.6%, −77.4% and −91.9% on CLIC2020-test). As shown in Figure 1 (right),
PerQ is the only codec that combines such perceptual gains with VVC-level PSNR, whereas existing
perceptual codecs offer significantly inferior PSNR performance (+89.9% BD-rate against VVC for
MS-ILLM and +116.3% for HiFiC on CLIC2020-test). It can also be observed that per-rate trained
generative codecs achieve higher perceptual BD-rate gains (e.g., MS-ILLM, StableCodec), but they
require separate checkpoints per rate point or operate over narrower rate ranges.
Qualitative comparison. Figure 1 (left) and Figure 5 compare reconstructions at comparable bitrates.
At low rates, ELIC produces visibly blurred textures, while generative codecs recover sharpness but
with a substantial PSNR drop (more than 5 dB below ELIC). PerQ recovers much of this sharpness
with PSNR results close to ELIC. In Figure 1, for example, it matches the LPIPS of OSCAR with a
6.5 dB higher PSNR. At high rates, generative codecs introduce unfaithful texture details, while PerQ
delivers sharpness with similarly high fidelity to ELIC (28.22 vs. 28.40 dB) using the fewest bits.
This pattern confirms the quantitative results in Table 1 and verifies the effectiveness of the bounded
compensator. More visual examples are available in Appendix D.
Ablation Study. We ablate the design choices of PerQ on the CLIC2020-test dataset [14]. We
study the compensator design, the different backbone, and the bound size that sets the width of
the sub-cell the compensator can reach. Specifically, Table 2 ablates four components of the default
PerQ design. All variants share the frozen backbone and therefore decode identical bitstreams. It
can be observed that replacing E-LPIPS with LPIPS (v1.1) slightly decreases performance, and
reducing the E-LPIPS weight to 0.25 (v1.3) increases the FID BD-rate by 7.20%, while a weight of
0.5 (v1.2) performs similarly to the default. Reducing flow-matching from four integration steps to
one (v2.1) degrades perception (+7.96% LPIPS, +5.63% DISTS, +14.75% FID), and lowering λFM
(v3.1, v3.2 and v3.3) degrades all metrics, by up to +28.31% in FID. Finally, the 0.5× compensator
(18.1M parameters, v4.1) performs close to the default (at most +3.38%, in FID), while the 0.25×
one (8.8M, v4.2) loses perceptual quality (+8.85% LPIPS, +15.64% FID).

6
Conclusion
We presented PerQ, a learning-based image codec that addresses the trade-off between pixel-wise
fidelity and perceptual quality by formulating perceptual reconstruction as inverse generative mod-
eling with bounded support. Specifically, we characterized the quantization cell of a multi-rate
distortion-optimized codec as the set of latents that yield the same transmitted symbols, and derived a
transport bound that constrains the deviation of reconstructions within this cell from the backbone’s
output. Building on this formulation, we developed a rate-conditioned flow-matching compensator
that performs perceptual reconstruction within the codec-induced bounded support, preserving the
backbone’s multi-rate capability. Experiments on Kodak and CLIC2020-test demonstrate that PerQ
achieves strong perceptual quality with competitive pixel fidelity, while supporting a wide bitrate
range through a single model and efficient decoding. To the best of our knowledge, PerQ is the first
generative image codec to combine these properties in a single framework.

B
Implementation details
B.1
Training configurations
Stage 1: backbone (distortion only).
The compression backbone (DCVC-RT intra-frame model
[25]) is trained from scratch on the MLIC training set [29, 28] with the rate-distortion objective
L = R + λ · LMSE for 1M steps at batch size 64. The Lagrange multiplier λ is sampled from [4,
4096] for 64 rate points, following [35].
We use the AdamW optimizer [41] with (β1, β2) = (0.9, 0.999) and weight decay 10−2. The peak
learning rate is 2 × 10−4, reached by a linear warmup from 10−6 over the first 100K steps, with
a multi-step schedule that halves the learning rate at 50%, 75%, 87.5%, and 93.75% of training.
Gradients are clipped to a global ℓ2 norm of 1.0. Over the first 10% of training, the weight of the
rate term and λ are ramped up linearly, from 0 to 1 and from 4 to the sampled value, respectively.
Training images are randomly cropped to 256×256.
Stage 2: compensator (perceptual optimization).
In stage 2 we freeze the encoder, decoder,
hyper-prior, and entropy models, and add the compensator and discriminator for joint training. We
train for 200K steps at batch size 16 with λ ∈[4, 4096] and the Stage 2 objective of Section 3.4.
The weight of the latent flow-matching term is λFM = 104, the weight of the adversarial term is
λGAN = 0.1, and the E-LPIPS term has the same weight as the MSE term.
LE-LPIPS uses the VGG backbone [30, 61, 49] during training; we report the LPIPS variant with
AlexNet [32] features for evaluation. Optimizer and schedule follow stage 1, except the peak learning
rate is 1 × 10−4, the warmup is 10K steps without the rate and λ warmup, and we keep the weight
decay to 10−2 on the compensator’s and discriminator’s parameters. The GAN term is disabled for
the first 20K iterations and then linearly warmed up over the next 20K iterations to its full weight.
Compensator architecture.
The velocity field vψ is a stack of 10 ConvNeXt blocks [40] with
hidden width of 512, an MLP expansion ratio of 4 and a kernel size of 5. It first computes the
sum of a 1×1 convolution of the scaled flow state q rt and a 1×1 convolution of the channel-wise
concatenation of ˆy, the upsampled ˆz, and q. Timestep conditioning is injected through a sinusoidal
positional embedding followed by a linear layer with SiLU, and each block maps it through a zero-
initialized linear layer to the shift, scale, and gate of an adaLN-zero modulation [47]. The output head
is a LayerNorm followed by a zero-initialized 1×1 convolution, which makes the compensator output
only the bounded prior noise at the start of training, i.e., it acts as the identity up to this noise. We use
4 Euler steps for flow matching at both training and inference, with bound scale m=0.1 (Eq. 10) and
prior noise standard deviation σ = 0.029 ≈m/
√
12 (Section 3.3.1).
The default compensator has 31.0M parameters. The compensator-size ablation (v4.1 and v4.2 in
Table 2) uses two smaller compensators that change only the hidden width and keep the depth, kernel
size, bound and training recipe: PerQ-0.5× with a hidden width of 384 (18.1M parameters) and
PerQ-0.25× with a hidden width of 256 (8.8M parameters).
Discriminator.
The adversarial term uses a multi-scale rate-point-conditioned patch discriminator
[43] with 3 spatial scales (input downsampled by 1×, 2×, 4× via average pooling) and a separate
per-rate-point parameterization (per-rate kernels at each conv layer). Each scale uses 3 strided
convolutional layers with base width 48, doubled at each layer (capped at 512), followed by a 4×4
stride-1 conv and a final 4×4 patch logit head. All convolutions except the final logit head use
spectral normalization [45] and LeakyReLU(0.2) activations. The discriminator is trained jointly
with the compensator, where both are updated at every step by the same AdamW optimizer, with
hinge losses for the discriminator and the generator.
Backbones of the generality study.
The three backbones of Table 4 are all trained from scratch
on the MLIC training set with the Stage 1 objective, 64 rate points and the consolidated bottleneck
16


quantization of Section 3.3.2. DCVC-RT is the default backbone described above. ELIC [20] keeps
its original architecture (320 latent channels in five uneven groups) and follows the DCVC-RT recipe,
except that λ is sampled from [32, 4096]. MLIC++ [28] keeps its original architecture (320 latent
channels in 10 slices) with λ ∈[32, 4096]. It is first trained for 1M steps on 256×256 crops at batch
size 64 with a peak learning rate of 10−4, and then fine-tuned for 400K steps on 512×512 crops at
batch size 32, with a peak learning rate of 10−4, 5K warmup steps, no rate or λ warmup, and the
learning rate halved at 37.5%, 75%, 87.5%, and 93.75% of the fine-tuning. Since λ ≥32, ELIC
and MLIC++ do not reach the lowest rates of DCVC-RT (see the bpp ranges in Table 4). On each
backbone, the compensator and all Stage 2 settings are those of the default model, except that λ
follows the range of the backbone and MLIC++ is trained on 512×512 crops.

E
Limitations and Societal Impacts
Limitations. Alongside the positive characteristics, limitations still remain with PerQ. First, it does
not reach the highest perceptual quality attained by fully generative codecs, although it remains
very competitive on distortion-based metrics and has achieved a better trade-off between fidelity
and realism. Moreover, our design relies on a frozen distortion-optimized backbone and jointly
fine-tuning the backbone may further improve performance. A unified theoretical framework that
jointly optimizes the rate-distortion-perception trade-off should be investigated as future work.
Societal impacts. This paper proposes an advanced general-purpose learned image codec that can
potentially reduce image storage requirements while maintaining both high fidelity and perceptual
quality. This capability may also help reduce the carbon footprint of a wide range of related
applications.
