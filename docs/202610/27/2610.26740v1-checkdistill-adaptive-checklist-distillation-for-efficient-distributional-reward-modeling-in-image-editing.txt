Abstract
Reinforcement learning for instruction-guided image editing requires reward models that
provide reliable, accurate, and efficient feedback. Direct scalar prediction compresses
multidimensional editing preferences, whereas generative evaluators can expose their
judgment through explicit reasoning. However, broad scoring criteria do not specify all
instance-dependent editing and preservation constraints, and generating long reasoning
trajectories is costly for online optimization. We introduce CheckDistill, a pointwise reward
modeling framework that internalizes instruction-adaptive checklists. We decompose each
editing condition into atomic, independently verifiable requirements and construct four-
dimensional scoring labels together with structured verification evidence. After supervised
score initialization, a privileged teacher conditions on checklists, item-level judgments,
and dimension-level rationales to supervise the student’s own scoring trajectories. A
reliability-weighted on-policy self-distillation objective adjusts supervision at score tokens
according to agreement between the teacher’s expected scores and reference labels, while
retaining candidate probability weights in a truncated token support. The deployed student
directly predicts four score distributions without checklist inputs or explicit reasoning
and aggregates their expectations into a scalar reward. CheckDistill achieves aggregate
accuracies of 72.30%, 56.67% and 82.90% on MMRB2, MER-Bench and EditReward-Bench,
respectively, exceeding the compared open-source baselines on all three metrics.

Introduction
Instruction-guided image editing is a key application of multi-modal generation, enabling users
to perform diverse image modifications through natural language instructions. Reinforcement
learning can substantially improve image editing performance, with reward models playing
a critical role in guiding optimization. An effective reward model must evaluate candidate
results across complementary dimensions, distinguish subtle yet consequential differences, and
provide accurate preference rankings and score singals. Frequent reward queries during online
reinforcement learning further require reward models to combine comprehensive, fine-grained
evaluation with efficient scoring.
Existing reward modeling approaches do not fully meet these requirements. As shown in
Fig. 1(a), regression-based reward models (Xu et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2023a; 2026) enable efficient
scoring. However, these models rely on coarse-grained subjective preferences, typically learned
through pairwise objectives such as the Bradley–Terry loss (Bradley and Terry, 1952). These ob-
jectives alone provide neither explicit verification evidence nor absolute score calibration. These
limitations reduce interpretability and may make score distributions more prone to drift and
the models more vulnerable to reward hacking. Fig. 1(b) shows the reasoning-based generative
reward models (Luo et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026a; Kan et al., 2026). This paradigm uses fixed

These limitations motivate two complementary principles. First, Dynamic Checklist Scal-
ing: reward judgments should be grounded in instance-adaptive identification of editing and
preservation requirements, decomposed into fine-grained, atomic, and independently verifiable
checks. Expanding the coverage and specificity of these checks can support more accurate
and robust reward judgments. Second, Context-Compressed Reward Scoring: efficient online
deployment requires reducing the decoding overhead of lengthy chain-of-thought reasoning
context while preserving reward quality. Therefore, the core challenge is to align score distri-
butions under standard editing inputs with their evidence-conditioned counterparts, while
reducing inference-time dependence on extensive verification contexts and explicit reasoning.
Guided by these principles, we propose CheckDistill, an image editing reward modeling
framework that combines dynamic checklist-based data construction with reliability-aware on-
policy self-distillation, as illustrated in Fig. 1(c). The framework builds structured verification
evidence during training and transfers evidence-enhanced judgments into efficient direct
scoring.

Policy Self-Distillation (CR-OPSD). The teacher additionally receives checklists, item-level
judgments, and dimension-level rationales, providing distributional supervision on response

Our main contributions are as follows:
• Dynamic checklist-based reward supervision. We construct CheckDistill-40K, pairing
four-dimensional scores with instance-specific verification evidence through dynamic
checklists and human preference-consistency filtering.
• Reliability-aware distillation for efficient reward scoring. We propose CR-OPSD to
transfer evidence-conditioned scoring behavior into a direct reward model through
reliability-aware on-policy distribution alignment.
• Leading reward accuracy and downstream editing gains. CheckDistill outperforms
the compared state-of-the-art open-source reward models on image editing reward
benchmarks and improves Qwen-Image-Edit through reward-guided post-training.
Key insight. Scaling instance-specific dynamic checklists defines the frontier of reward quality;
compressing reasoning enables deployment at scale.

Related Work
Reinforcement learning (RL) (Black et al., 2024; Fan et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2025a; Xue et al., 2025;
Zheng et al., 2026) has emerged as an important post-training paradigm for visual generation,
3


enabling diffusion and flow-based models (Rombach et al., 2022; Podell et al., 2024; Esser et al.,
2024; Black Forest Labs et al., 2025) to achieve stronger alignment. Its effectiveness, however,
relies heavily on accurate and efficient reward models.
Regression-based Reward Modeling.
Regression-based visual reward models learn human
preferences from vision-language representations using regression or Bradley–Terry-style pref-
erence objectives (Wu et al., 2023b; Xu et al., 2023; Kirstain et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2023a; Zhang
et al., 2024a; Wu et al., 2024a; Zhang et al., 2024b; Chen et al., 2025; Ma et al., 2025; Wang
et al., 2025a; Wu et al., 2026). These methods are efficient at inference, but typically output
scalar scores without explicit reasoning, limiting interpretability and diagnostic feedback for
fine-grained evaluation.
Generative Reward Modeling.
Generative reward models such as EditScore (Luo et al., 2026),
RationalRewards (Wang et al., 2026a), SpatialReward (Long et al., 2026), and UnifiedReward-
Think (Wang et al., 2025b) produce explicit rationales or chain-of-thought reasoning for fine-
grained reward prediction under fixed evaluation criteria. However, these criteria are not
instantiated into concrete, input-specific verification requirements, and inference-time rea-
soning introduces substantial overhead in large-scale online RL. JRM (Yang et al., 2026) and
Z-Reward (Jin et al., 2026) improve efficiency by internalizing reasoning into direct reward
prediction, but still lack instance-adaptive verification criteria.
Instance-adaptive methods tailor evaluation criteria to each input. RubricRM (Kan et al.,
2026) generates input-specific dimensions and scoring criteria, but remains focused on high-
level evaluation dimensions. Edit-R1 (Guo et al., 2026) decomposes editing instructions into
sample-specific principles for finer-grained verification, but still requires explicit reasoning at
inference time. Checklist-based methods translate evaluation requirements into directly verifi-
able items (Cook et al., 2024); rDPO (Yu et al., 2026), for example, constructs instance-specific
checklist-style rubrics primarily for preference data construction. In contrast, CheckDistill uses
source-image-grounded atomic checklists only as privileged information during reward-model
training, distilling fine-grained instance-specific verification requirements into direct reward
prediction without checklist generation or explicit reasoning at inference time.
3
Method
As shown in Figure 3, we propose CheckDistill, a generative reward model that leverages
instance-specific checklists for fine-grained evaluation and internalizes checklist-derived evalu-
ation knowledge through CR-OPSD. Given an input x = (u, Isrc, Iedt), CheckDistill maps it to a
multi-dimensional scoring response y, where u denotes the editing instruction, Isrc the source
image, and Iedt the edited image.
3.1
Evaluation Formulation
Evaluation Dimensions.
We evaluate image editing from two complementary aspects: Se-
mantic Consistency (SC) and Perceptual Quality (PQ). SC comprises three dimensions: Instruction
Following (IF), which measures whether the requested edits are correctly executed; Attribute
Preservation (AP), which measures whether unspecified attributes of edited objects are preserved;
and Context Preservation (CP), which measures whether non-edited content remains unchanged.
PQ measures the overall visual quality of the edited image. All four scores, SIF, SAP, SCP, and
SPQ, are defined on a discrete scale from 0 to 9.
Multidimensional Reward Aggregation.
To obtain a scalar feedback signal for downstream
post-training of the editing model, we account for the varying importance of different di-
mensions by first aggregating the three SC scores using dimension-specific weights, and then

Experiments
4.1
Performance on Reward Modeling Benchmarks
Implementation Details.
We implement CheckDistill on Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct (Bai et al.,
2025), Qwen3.5-4B, and Qwen3.5-9B. Unless otherwise specified, we set K = 10 and use
reliability weights in [0.8, 1.2]. For reward aggregation, the IF/AP/CP weights are set to
(0.4, 0.35, 0.25), with SC/PQ exponents of 0.8/0.2. Implementation details are provided in
Appendix A.
Baselines and Evaluation Protocol.
We evaluate the reward modeling performance on
MMRB2 (Hu et al., 2026a), MER-Bench (Long et al., 2026), and EditReward-Bench (Luo et al.,
2026). We compare CheckDistill with closed-source visual judges (GPT-4.1, Gemini-3.1-Pro,

Privileged-Information Design.
We ablate the privileged information provided to the teacher
by progressively introducing checklist C, checklist judgments (C, J), dimension-wise rationales
(C, J, A), and dimension scores (C, J, A, g), while keeping the SFT initialization, training data,
student inputs, and optimization settings unchanged. As shown in Table 3, (C, J, A) achieves
the best overall performance across all three benchmarks, reaching 72.30% on MMRB2, 56.67%
on MER-Bench, and 82.90% on EditReward-Bench.
However, further exposing dimension scores to the teacher, i.e., directly providing the ground-
truth targets as in OPSD, does not yield additional gains: (C, J, A) outperforms (C, J, A, g)
by 2.20, 0.84, and 1.60 points on MMRB2, MER-Bench, and EditReward-Bench, respectively.
These results demonstrate the effectiveness of checklist-based privileged information, where
checklist judgments and dimension-wise rationales further enrich the supervision without
directly exposing score labels.
CR-OPSD Component Ablation.
We ablate the key design choices of CR-OPSD while keeping
the privileged information (C, J, A), initialization, training data, and optimization settings fixed.
Specifically, w/o Reliability Weighting removes reliability modulation by setting ct = 1 for all
positions; Linear Reliability Mapping replaces the cosine mapping in Eq. 5 with a linear mapping;
and Argmax-based Reliability replaces the expected teacher score with the teacher’s maximum-
probability score token for reliability estimation.
As shown in Table 3, CR-OPSD consistently achieves the best overall performance across
all three benchmarks. Compared with removing reliability weighting, it improves MMRB2,
MER-Bench, and EditReward-Bench by 2.30, 1.67, and 0.80 percentage points, respectively,
demonstrating the benefit of modulating privileged supervision according to teacher reliability.
The cosine mapping further improves over its linear counterpart by 1.00, 0.50, and 0.60 points,
while using the expected teacher score instead of the argmax score yields gains of 1.70, 1.17, and
0.30 points. These results validate both reliability-aware weighting and the use of distributional
score information for more robust privileged on-policy self-distillation.
5
Conclusion
We present CheckDistill, an efficient reward modeling framework for instruction-guided image
editing. It combines instance-specific dynamic checklists for fine-grained verification with CR-
OPSD to distill evidence-supported scoring behavior into a direct-scoring model that requires
neither checklist inputs nor explicit reasoning at inference time. Experiments demonstrate
strong reward modeling performance and improved image editing through reward-guided
reinforcement learning using DiffusionNFT. Together, these findings show that scaling instance-
specific verification improves reward quality, while reliability-aware distillation enables efficient
reward scoring for image editing at scale.

A.4
Reward Benchmark Evaluation Details
We evaluate reward prediction on MMRB2, MER-Bench, and EditReward-Bench, none of which
overlaps with the data used for CheckDistill. MMRB2 reports single-reference, multi-reference,
and overall accuracy, where the overall accuracy is computed as a sample-count-weighted
average of the single- and multi-reference subsets rather than their arithmetic mean. MER-Bench
reports ranking accuracy under the 2-pair, 3-pair, and 4-pair settings, with their arithmetic mean
used as the overall metric. EditReward-Bench evaluates prompt following (PF), consistency
(CS), and overall preference accuracy. For CheckDistill PF is given by the instruction-following
score, while CS is computed from the attribute-preservation (AP) and context-preservation (CP)
scores using weights of 0.35 and 0.25, respectively, consistent with their weights in the overall
reward. The final scalar reward follows Eq. 8 and 1. Except for specialized reward models
with their own inference formats, all evaluated models use the same system prompt. Each
candidate is scored independently in a pointwise manner, after which pairwise comparisons
and multi-candidate rankings are obtained by comparing the resulting scalar scores.

D
Background
Multimodal Instruction-Guided Image Editing.
Instruction-guided image editing requires
models to execute requested transformations while preserving unrelated visual content. Recent
20


open-source and open-weight models, exemplified by Qwen-Image and FLUX.1 Kontext, have
substantially expanded the capabilities of language-driven image editing [Wu et al., 2025a;
Black Forest Labs et al., 2025; Hu et al., 2026b; Meituan LongCat Team et al., 2025; Wu et al.,
2025b; Liu et al., 2025b; Super Intelligence Team, 2026]. Capability-centric data design further
supports the joint development of generation and editing through coordinated supervision and
training curricula [Wang et al., 2026b]. Meanwhile, commercial systems such as GPT Image
and Gemini bring image generation and editing into interactive creative workflows [OpenAI;
Google DeepMind], while virtual try-on research extends these capabilities toward fashion
applications that require faithful preservation of garment appearance and person identity [Chen
et al., 2026; Choi et al., 2024; Chong et al., 2024]. To assess these increasingly diverse capabilities,
benchmarks including GEdit-Bench, ImgEdit-Bench, REDEdit-Bench, and CPI-Bench collec-
tively broaden evaluation across editing tasks and practical usage scenarios [Liu et al., 2025b;
Ye et al., 2025; Super Intelligence Team, 2026; Zhou et al., 2026]. These developments motivate
alignment methods that jointly account for instruction fulfillment, content preservation, and
perceptual quality.
Reinforcement Learning for Image Editing.
RL provides a post-training mechanism for
optimizing visual generators against feedback beyond supervised reconstruction objectives.
DDPO and DPOK establish policy-gradient approaches for diffusion models (Black et al., 2024;
Fan et al., 2023); Flow-GRPO and DanceGRPO extend group-relative optimization to flow-based
and broader visual generation settings (Liu et al., 2025a; Xue et al., 2025), while DiffusionNFT
formulates online reward-driven updates through the forward process (Zheng et al., 2026).
Building on these foundations, editing-specific methods increasingly emphasize the design
of reward feedback. HIVE learns human preferences to guide instructional editing (Zhang
et al., 2024b). UniWorld-V2 combines DiffusionNFT with MLLM logit-based feedback and
group filtering to stabilize editing optimization (Li et al., 2025). Edit-R1 trains a principle-based
reasoning verifier and uses its rewards to optimize editing models with GRPO (Guo et al.,
2026). At the system level, Swift-Image combines parallel expert RL with multi-teacher on-
policy distillation to mitigate interference among heterogeneous objectives (Hu et al., 2026b).
Together, these approaches motivate rewards that reliably distinguish successful edits from
subtle preservation failures while remaining efficient enough for repeated online evaluation.
Regression-based Reward Modeling.
Regression-based reward models learn holistic or
dimension-specific preferences from vision-language representations, commonly through re-
gression or pairwise preference objectives. ImageReward, PickScore, and HPS/HPSv2 model
human preferences for text-to-image generation (Xu et al., 2023; Kirstain et al., 2023; Wu et al.,
2023b;a); MPS extends evaluation to multiple dimensions (Zhang et al., 2024a), while HPSv3
explores broader visual preference modeling (Ma et al., 2025). For image editing, HIVE and
EditReward learn editing-specific preference signals (Zhang et al., 2024b; Wu et al., 2026), and
ADIEE develops automated editing evaluation (Chen et al., 2025). Avoiding lengthy rationale
generation makes direct scoring attractive for online RL. However, scores alone do not explicitly
expose which concrete editing or preservation requirements are satisfied, motivating richer
supervision during reward learning.
Generative Reward Modeling.
Generative reward models make evaluation more explicit
by producing rationales or structured critiques. EditScore, RationalRewards, SpatialReward,
and UnifiedReward-Think investigate reasoning-based visual assessment from complementary
perspectives (Luo et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026a; Long et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2025b). Such
reasoning can provide detailed diagnostic evidence, but autoregressive evaluation introduces
additional computation when repeated across online RL samples. JRM and Z-Reward address
this efficiency challenge by internalizing reasoning into more direct reward prediction path-
ways (Yang et al., 2026; Jin et al., 2026). Our focus is on the supervision transferred into such
efficient predictors: instance-specific verification requirements grounded in both the source
21


image and the editing instruction.
Instance-Adaptive Verification.
Adaptive evaluation tailors its criteria to individual inputs.
RubricRM generates input-specific dimensions, weights, and scoring criteria (Kan et al., 2026),
while Edit-R1 decomposes editing instructions into principles for explicit verification (Guo
et al., 2026). Generated checklists have also been studied for language-model evaluation (Cook
et al., 2024); in multimodal preference optimization, rDPO uses instance-specific checklist-style
rubrics to construct and filter preference data (Yu et al., 2026). CheckDistill combines adaptive
verification with efficient reward inference: source-image-grounded atomic checklists and their
verification evidence serve as privileged information during reward-model training. Distilling
this supervision into direct multidimensional score distributions enables reward prediction
without checklist inputs or explicit reasoning at deployment.
E
Checklist Construction and Judge Templates
E.1
Checklist Construction Template
Given one or more reference images and an editing instruction, the checklist constructor
generates atomic verification items for Instruction Following (IF), Attribute Preservation (AP),
and Context Preservation (CP). Each item contains five key fields: type, indicating the evaluation
dimension; object, specifying the edited or preserved entity; location, describing its spatial
position; bbox_2d, giving the normalized bounding box in the reference image; and content,
specifying the atomic requirement to be verified. Bounding-box coordinates are normalized
to integer values in [0, 1000], and bbox_2d is set to null for newly added objects when no
expected generation region can be determined. For multiple reference images, ref_image
further identifies the source image containing the object. The checklist is returned as a single
JSON list, as specified in the template below.
Checklist Construction Template
You are a strict and meticulous expert in designing image-editing checklists. Given one or more reference images and an
editing instruction, generate checklists that can guide subsequent evaluation.
**## Input**
Prompt: {request}
Image input: one or more reference images <image>
**## Generation Procedure**
1. Inspect the reference image(s): describe key elements that are actually visible in the reference image(s), including
their location, count, color, shape, pose, and other observable attributes. Do not infer or imagine any invisible
content.
2. Parse the instruction: decompose the editing request into concrete add/remove/modify operations.
3. Generate checklists: decompose each operation into independently verifiable checklist items.
**## Checklist Types**
- Instruction Following (IF): the specific editing action that must be performed on an object involved in the edit.
- Attribute Preservation (AP): the key attributes of an edited object that should remain unchanged during editing.
- Context Preservation (CP): for each object not involved in the edit, set the content to "Keep this object unchanged."
**## Generation Rules (Must Be Followed)**
1. **Groundedness**: The object, location, and content in every checklist item must be directly grounded in either the
visible content of the reference image(s) or the explicit editing instruction.
Do not introduce objects, attributes, or locations that are unsupported by either the reference image(s) or the editing
instruction.
2. **Atomicity**: Each checklist item must describe only one independently verifiable target. Do not combine multiple
operations, objects, or attributes into a single item.
3. **Removal Operations**: Specify only the object to be removed and its location. Do not describe how the background should
be filled after removal.
4. **BBox Localization**: Each checklist item must include `bbox_2d`, indicating the bounding box of the object in the
reference image in the format [x1, y1, x2, y2], where the coordinates correspond to the top-left and bottom-right
corners and are normalized to integer values in [0, 1000]. The `bbox_2d` must tightly cover the actually visible extent
of the object. When multiple reference images are provided, additionally use `ref_image` to indicate the 1-based index
of the reference image containing the object. For an IF item involving a newly added object that does not exist in the
reference image, set `bbox_2d` to the expected generation region; if the expected region cannot be determined, set it
22


to null.
5. **Self-Check**: Before output, verify that every checklist item is directly supported by either the reference image(s) or
the editing instruction, and that each `bbox_2d` is consistent with the actual object location.
**## Output**
Write all checklist items into a single JSON list. The output language must be consistent with the editing instruction:

Discussion
Z-Reward (Jin et al., 2026) and our CheckDistill both distill reasoning-supported assessment
into direct scoring, but address different challenges. Z-Reward focuses on representing pref-
erence uncertainty and fine-grained score differences through reasoning-conditioned score
distributions. Our starting point is that image-editing evaluation requires instance-specific
verification of both requested changes and preservation constraints. We therefore intro-
duce Dynamic Checklist Scaling to translate each editing instruction and source image into
atomic verification criteria, enriching the teacher context with checklists and their supporting
judgements.
This focus on verification evidence also shapes our distillation design. While Z-Reward transfers
scoring knowledge from a large reasoning teacher to a compact student, we use on-policy self-
distillation with an evidence-enriched teacher of the same architecture. Distribution-Aligned
Context Compression transfers the teacher’s scoring behavior at student-generated prefixes,
and reliability weighting modulates supervision at score positions according to teacher–label
agreement, accounting for potentially incorrect teacher judgements. Our contribution centers
on constructing and reliably internalizing instance-specific verification evidence, enabling
direct scoring without checklist inputs or explicit reasoning at inference time.
