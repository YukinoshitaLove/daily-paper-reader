Abstract
Image editing models produce impressive results for indi-
vidual edits, but their quality often deteriorates over multi-
ple editing turns. Each editing turn introduces small distri-
bution shifts that accumulate over successive edits, creating
a gap between the input distributions encountered during
the editor’s original training and multi-turn inference. We
introduce a training scheme that makes multi-turn image
editing stable under this accumulating distribution shift. A
key is to force the editor to operate on its own outputs from
previous editing turns during training, bringing the princi-
ple of Self-Forcing to image editing. The main challenge is
that self-generated editing trajectories deviate from ground-
truth trajectories, making direct supervision unreliable. We
address this challenge through a distillation objective that
leverages a teacher image editor to provide a reliable train-
ing signal when the model operates on its own previous
outputs. We introduce an asymmetric conditioning scheme:
while the student performs edits sequentially on its own out-

puts, the teacher remains anchored to the original clean
image and receives a compound editing instruction. Exper-
iments show substantially reduced degradation over long
multi-turn sequences while maintaining editing quality and
instruction adherence.
1. Introduction
Image editing with modern generative models has reached
an impressive level of quality, enabling complex modifica-
tions while faithfully preserving the appearance and content
of the input image [6, 24, 26, 44, 49]. Yet, practical editing
is rarely a single operation. Users often refine an image
through a sequence of edits, where the output of one edit
becomes the input to the next. This setting is commonly
referred to as multi-turn image editing. With current image
editing models, each editing turn introduces small distribu-
tion shifts that accumulate as the model repeatedly operates
on its own outputs. These shifts create a growing gap be-

tween the input distributions encountered during the editor’s
original training and multi-turn inference. This gap leads to
accumulating artifacts, loss of fine details, and unintended
changes in appearance (see Figure 1). Effective image edit-
ing therefore requires not only high-quality individual edits,
but also stability across successive editing turns.
Several approaches have been proposed to mitigate
degradation in multi-turn image editing.
Inference-time
methods target specific sources of degradation [27, 38, 47,
62], but may incur computational overhead and are lim-
ited by the complexity of distribution shifts in multi-turn
editing. Other approaches train models on multi-turn edit-
ing sequences [16, 39, 52, 61]. Training on ground-truth
sequences leaves a gap between training and inference,
while training on self-generated sequences poses supervi-
sion challenges, leading to inconsistent targets, restrictions
on training sequences, or objectives that do not explicitly
address distribution shifts.
We present a training scheme for multi-turn image edit-
ing that maintains stability under accumulating distribution
shifts. Our scheme is inspired by autoregressive video gen-
eration, where models similarly operate on their own out-
puts and face accumulating distribution shifts. Following
the principle of Self-Forcing [17], we train the editor on its
own outputs from previous editing turns, allowing it to learn
from the distribution shifts encountered during multi-turn
inference. However, self-generated editing trajectories in-
evitably deviate from ground-truth trajectories, making di-
rect supervision with ground-truth images unreliable.
To address this challenge, we employ score distillation in
a teacher-student framework to train the editor on its self-
generated trajectories without requiring ground-truth tar-
gets for its intermediate outputs [37, 48, 57]. We intro-
duce an asymmetric conditioning scheme for the student
and teacher. The student’s input may already deviate sig-
nificantly from the teacher’s training distribution, making it
unsuitable for reliable teacher guidance. We therefore con-
dition the teacher on the original image and a compound
instruction combining all edits up to the current turn, while
the student performs edits sequentially on its own outputs.
This provides a training signal that reflects the intended ed-
its without inheriting the errors accumulated along the stu-
dent’s trajectory.
We evaluate our method against existing multi-turn im-
age editing approaches and demonstrate significant quali-
tative and quantitative improvements in preserving editing
capabilities while mitigating accumulated distribution drift.
Our training is efficient and introduces no inference-time
overhead, while making no assumptions about the nature
of distribution shifts or restrictions on editing sequences.
Through ablation studies, we further demonstrate the im-
portance of our asymmetric conditioning scheme for stable
multi-turn editing.
2. Related Work
Text-guided image editing.
Early text-driven manipu-
lation largely steered pre-trained GAN latent spaces via
vision-language models [11, 36].
The advent of diffu-
sion models broadened semantic coverage and synthesized
complex scenes more flexibly. Subsequent approaches im-
proved source-image preservation and fine-grained con-
trol through noise perturbation and trajectory inversion [5,
10, 19, 33, 34, 46], alongside spatial blending and cross-
attention steering [1, 3, 7, 14, 45].
Recent efforts ex-
tend inference-time control to continuous-time flow mod-
els through structural layer control [4], flow inversion [20,
40], inversion-free sampling trajectories [23], and text-
embedding control for continuous editing [18, 21]. Comple-
menting inference-time methods, instruction-tuned archi-
tectures learn a direct mapping using synthetic pairs [6], hu-
man annotations [59], and multi-task supervision [13, 42].
Emerging state-of-the-art transformer-based diffusion and
flow backbones further push visual quality and composi-
tional reasoning, demonstrating unprecedented instruction
fidelity and fine-grained spatial control [25, 26, 49, 50]. De-
spite these single-step capabilities, isolated edit accuracy
does not prevent error propagation, identity degradation, or
semantic drift when applied iteratively across extended edit-
ing sequences.
Multi-turn image editing.
Maintaining image quality
and content consistency across successive edits is a cen-
tral challenge in multi-turn image editing, as errors can
accumulate over the editing sequence.
Agentic frame-
works tackle this challenge through planning, memory, and
reflection [41, 54], while training-free methods improve
consistency through inversion and attention control [62],
high-frequency feature preservation [27], inversion shar-
ing [38], and low-frequency latent alignment [47]. Other
approaches learn sequential editing directly: VINCIE learns
in-context editing from video-derived sequences [39], while
AnchorEdit combines supervised causal multi-turn train-
ing with self-generated histories and identity-anchoring
memory [52]. Reinforcement learning methods optimize
trajectory-level editing rewards [16] or jointly optimize
history-dependent reasoning and image generation [55].
More recently, MT-OPSD distills clean-conditioned editing
behavior onto states generated through identity-edit roll-
outs [61], which accumulate errors while keeping the in-
tended content fixed. Our training instead follows the edit-
ing process as it unfolds during inference: successive se-
mantic edits with evolving content and preservation require-
ments. Through distribution matching, we align the stu-
dent’s evolving output distribution, modeled by a learned
auxiliary editor, with the target distribution defined by a
teacher conditioned on the original image and cumulative
instructions, without paired intermediate targets.

3. Preliminaries: Score Distillation
Score Distillation Sampling (SDS) [37] uses a pretrained
text-to-image diffusion model ϵϕ to optimize a differen-
tiable renderer G parameterized by θ. It relies on the ob-
servation that the model’s noise prediction at each noise
level is proportional to the score of the noisy image distri-
bution [43]. SDS uses this score to guide images produced
by G without requiring paired target images. For a sampled
timestep t and Gaussian noise ϵ, the SDS gradient is:

SDS is known to produce blurry or oversaturated results,
which has motivated numerous follow-up works [9, 32, 58,
63]. Some of these works have identified the high variance
associated with subtracting the sampled noise ϵ from the
model’s prediction as a contributing factor. In particular,
Variational Score Distillation (VSD) [48] addresses this is-
sue by replacing the sampled noise ϵ subtracted from the
model’s prediction with the prediction of an auxiliary model
ϵLoRA fine-tuned on images produced by G.
Noise-Free Score Distillation (NFSD) [22] provides a
new interpretation of score distillation.
Specifically, it
decomposes the conditional noise prediction into three
components: condition alignment, correction toward the
model’s image distribution, and denoising: ϵϕ(zt; c, t) =
δC+δD+δN. Under this interpretation, subtracting the auxil-
iary prediction in VSD approximately cancels the denoising
component, leaving the directions associated with condition
alignment and domain correction to guide optimization.
While SDS and VSD optimize individual instances
for a given prompt, Distribution Matching Distillation
(DMD) [56, 57] extends this approach to training a gen-
erative model. DMD uses the VSD loss to train a student
model, guided by a pretrained diffusion model referred to
as the teacher. The student learns to generate images for
arbitrary prompts. Our method adopts this teacher–student
formulation for training a stable editor.
Although these methods were originally formulated for
noise-predicting diffusion models, score distillation can
also be applied to flow-matching models [17]. This exten-
sion relies on converting predicted flow velocities into score
estimates. We adopt this formulation in our method.
4. Method
Image editing models are typically trained on pairs of input
and target images [26]. However, in multi-turn editing, the
model repeatedly receives inputs generated by its own pre-
vious predictions, which it was not trained to handle. This
creates a train–test gap that can cause errors and artifacts to
accumulate over successive edits.
To address this gap, we train our multi-turn image edit-
ing model Gθ to edit images produced by repeatedly apply-
3


ing the model itself, as illustrated in Figure 2. We refer to
the resulting sequences of edited images as rollouts. Su-
pervising the model on these rollouts is challenging even
when a ground-truth editing sequence is available. Since an
edit can have many valid outcomes, the model’s output at a
given turn may differ from the corresponding ground-truth
image, even if the edit was applied correctly. As a result,
subsequent edits in the rollout are applied to images that
differ from those in the ground-truth sequence. Therefore,
the ground-truth images cannot serve as paired targets for
supervising these edits.
Inspired by Self-Forcing [17], which addresses a sim-
ilar challenge in autoregressive video generation, we use
score distillation to supervise our student model Gθ, which
becomes our stable editor after training. We compute the
score-distillation signal using a pretrained flow-matching
editor vϕ that serves as the teacher.
A key challenge is that the student’s intermediate out-
puts may contain accumulated errors, making them unreli-
able inputs for the teacher. To address this, we introduce
asymmetric conditioning of the student and teacher through
compound-edit supervision. While the student applies edits
sequentially to its own outputs, the teacher is always con-
ditioned on the original image and a compound instruction
combining all edits up to the current turn. This allows the
teacher to provide a training signal that does not inherit the
errors accumulated along the student’s trajectory.
4.1. Self-Generated Editing Rollouts
We generate editing rollouts by repeatedly applying the stu-
dent editor Gθ to its own outputs (left part of Figure 2).
Each rollout starts from a source image I0 and a sequence of
E instructions c1, . . . , cE, with E = 10 during training. We
describe the dataset of instruction sequences in Section 5.
At turn e, the editor applies the next instruction ce to the
completed output from the previous turn:
Ie = Gθ(Ie−1, ce),
e = 1, . . . , E.
Thus, after the first turn, the editor operates on its own gen-
erated images, as it does at inference.
We implement the student with FLUX.2-klein-9B, an
image editor that operates in four denoising steps. We de-
note the complete four-step editing process by Gθ and a
single model prediction within this process by vθ. We use
the convention where σ = 1 corresponds to pure noise and
σ = 0 to a clean image, with the predicted velocity point-
ing toward noise. At a denoising step with noise level σ, let
xσ be the current state. The model’s prediction provides an
estimate of the clean output: ˆx = xσ −σvθ(xσ), where, for
brevity, we omit the dependence of vθ on the noise level,
the previous image, and the current instruction.
For a given training rollout, we consider an edit turn
e ∈{1, . . . , E} and a denoising exit step s ∈{1, . . . , 4}.
We first obtain Ie−1 by applying the preceding e −1 ed-
its, each using the full four-step denoising process without
tracking gradients. At turn e, we execute the denoising pro-
cess up to step s, where we compute the clean output esti-
mate ˆxs
e. Gradients are propagated only through the model’s
prediction at this step, without backpropagating through
earlier denoising steps or edit turns. Throughout the roll-
out, we pass the edited images directly between turns in the
model’s latent space, avoiding repeated decoding and en-
coding.

Asymmetric Conditioning.

ing instructions, preservation of relevant image details, and
generation of realistic, artifact-free images. We obtain this
signal from a pretrained flow-matching image editor vϕ,
which serves as our teacher. Specifically, we evaluate its
score on a noisy version of ˆxs

zτ(ˆxs

tion, our editing teacher is conditioned on both an image
and an instruction. It can therefore guide the output toward
results that satisfy the requested edit while preserving rele-
vant details of the conditioning image.
The remaining question is how to condition the pre-
trained editing teacher vϕ. Its conditioning image should
serve as the source of the edit, while its text input should
describe the intended change from that image to ˆxs
e. A nat-
ural choice is the previous output Ie−1 and the current in-
struction ce, which are also the inputs to the student Gθ at
turn e. However, beyond the first turn, Ie−1 is generated by
the student and may already contain artifacts. This creates a
distribution shift for the teacher, which was trained on clean
images. Moreover, since the teacher is expected to preserve
details of its conditioning image that are not affected by the
instruction, it may also preserve these artifacts rather than
correct them. We examine this conditioning choice in the
ablation study in Section 5.4.
Instead,
we condition the teacher on the original
clean image I0 and the accumulated instructions he =
(c1, . . . , ce). This allows the teacher to score the result of
multiple edits as a single compound edit, without relying

5. Experiments
5.1. Experimental Setup
Implementation details.
Our student is FLUX.2-klein-
9B [25], a 4-step distilled model, which we fine-tune with
a LoRA [15] of rank 16 (27.8M trainable parameters, 0.3%
of the model). The teacher and the auxiliary editor are both
FLUX.2-klein-base-9B, the undistilled model of the same
family. The teacher is frozen and uses classifier-free guid-
ance with scale 2. The auxiliary editor trains its own LoRA
of the same size as the student and is not guided. The stu-
dent, being distilled, runs without classifier-free guidance,
with 4 sampling steps in training rollouts as at inference.
Each training rollout is a chain of 10 edits, and the student’s
output latent is passed directly to the next edit, without go-
ing through the VAE. The noise level τ is drawn uniformly
over [0, 1), independently for every turn, both for the stu-
dent’s loss and for training the auxiliary editor.
The student and the auxiliary editor are optimized with
AdamW [30] (β = (0, 0.999), weight decay 0.01, gradient
clipping at 10, bf16), with learning rates of 4 × 10−5 for
the student and 8 × 10−6 for the auxiliary editor. We train
for 505 iterations: 5 warm-up iterations that update only
the auxiliary editor, followed by 500 iterations in which the
auxiliary editor is updated at every iteration and the student
at every fifth, i.e., 100 student updates. Training takes about
4.4 GPU-hours on a single NVIDIA B200. At inference we
use the same 4-step unguided sampler.
Training data.
The inputs for the training rollouts are
96 synthetic 1024 × 1024 images generated with FLUX.2-
klein-9B from short scene descriptions. Each image comes
with one cumulative chain of 10 edit instructions (960 in
total). All edits are local: each adds, removes, replaces or
resizes an object, or changes one of its attributes, such as
its color. Our evaluation benchmark, EdiVal-10 (Sec. 5.3),
consists of real photographs, so no image is shared between
training and evaluation.
Baselines.
We compare our stable editor with its pre-
trained backbone model, training-free strategies for reduc-
ing multi-turn drift, and a trained multi-turn editor. Our
backbone model baseline is FLUX.2-klein-9B without our
fine-tuning, using the same 4-step sampler as our student.
We evaluate Emu Edit’s pixel-thresholding strategy [42],
VAE-LFA [47], and FreqEdit [27] using the same backbone
model. For Emu Edit, we follow MT-OPSD’s implementa-

5.2. Qualitative Evaluation
Figures 1 and 3 compare our model with its backbone model
on editing sequences interspersed with no-op instructions.
We further compare against the training-free methods and
MT-OPSD on sequences of consecutive semantic edits in
Figure 4. The source scenes for these comparisons were
generated independently of the training data using FLUX.2-
klein-9B. Figure 4 demonstrates that degradation can ac-
cumulate even under simple editing instructions: after ten
successive object removals, all competing methods visibly
damage the surrounding scene, while our model success-
fully preserves it.
Each training-free method ties every output to part of the
previous image, and this is also its main weakness: when
an edit has to change exactly that part, content from ear-
lier turns leaks into the result. Emu Edit reverts pixels that
change only slightly, so remnants of earlier images survive;
VAE-LFA aligns low-frequency latent statistics with earlier
outputs, so their colours persist; and FreqEdit steers high-
frequency detail toward the previous image, so their tex-
tures persist. Figure 8 in Appendix F illustrates these effects
over three successive background replacements. MT-OPSD
leaves no such traces but blurs the image and washes out
its colours, consistent with the degradation observed in Fig-
ure 4.
Further comparisons with the backbone model, including
a full sequence of fifteen edits, are shown in Figs. 9 and 10
in the appendix.

text, count, and position. Each chain also reserves one or
two anchor objects that no instruction may edit or mention.
The language model then writes the instructions from a per-
chain plan of objects and edit types. They are checked by
rule-based validators and by the language model acting as a
critic, and rejected turns are sent back for rewriting. Every
language-model step of its construction uses Claude Sonnet
5.5 [2].
Metrics.
We score the final image of each chain (turn 10)
on three aspects and average over chains. Quality: a VLM
judge (Gemma-4-31B [12]) rates the image with the image-
quality prompt of KRIS-Bench [51], and ∆HPSv3 [31]
measures how much a learned quality estimate changes
from turn 1 to turn 10. Preservation: the same judge rates,
with the KRIS-Bench consistency prompt, how well every-
thing the instructions do not ask to change is kept; locally,
we measure the DINOv2 [35] distance to the source on the
unedited anchor objects and the LPIPS [60] distance on the
background outside all edited objects. Editing: the judge’s
KRIS-Bench instruction-following score checks that the re-
quested edits are carried out, so that preservation cannot
come from skipping them. Prompts, masks and the judge’s
controls are detailed in Appendix B.

5.4. Ablation Study
We evaluate the role of asymmetric conditioning on EdiVal-
10 by varying the inputs provided to the teacher, auxiliary
editor, and student, and the length of the training rollouts.
The teacher and auxiliary editor receive identical condition-
ing in all variants. All variants are trained for 100 student
updates. Table 2 reports the quantitative results, and Fig-
ure 5 illustrates the conditioning variants qualitatively.
Teacher and auxiliary conditioning.
Our teacher and
auxiliary editor are conditioned on the original image and
the compound instruction, anchoring score distillation to the
clean source. We compare this design with two alternatives:
additionally providing the previous student output to both
models, and replacing the original image and compound in-
struction with the previous output and current instruction.
Both alternatives substantially reduce preservation: consis-
tency drops from 3.85 to 2.81 and 2.28, respectively, com-
pared with 3.03 for the pretrained base model. Figure 5
illustrates the former variant: as with the base model, the
image degrades over the turns, and blotchy noise spreads
over the walls and the rug. These results suggest that con-
ditioning the score models on intermediate student outputs
can reinforce accumulated artifacts rather than correct them.
Student conditioning.
Our student receives only the pre-
vious output and current instruction. Providing the origi-
nal image as an additional reference improves consistency
from 3.85 to 4.08, but reduces instruction following from
3.38 to 2.80. As shown in Figure 5, earlier edits are undone
as later ones are applied: the recoloured stool and ball re-
turn to their original colours. Thus, stronger preservation
of the source does not necessarily imply successful multi-
turn editing. Our asymmetric design maintains both scene
consistency and the changes introduced across successive
turns.
Rollout length.
We train on rollouts of 10 edits, the same
length as the evaluated sequences. A student trained on 5-
edit rollouts still improves over the base model after ten
turns (consistency 3.29 vs. 3.03), but recovers only about
a third of our gain (3.85) and is significantly worse than our
model on every preservation measure. Training on its own
outputs over the full length of the sequences it edits at in-
ference is therefore important.
6. Conclusions, Limitations and Future Work
We introduced a training approach for stable multi-turn im-
age editing.
Our central idea is that stability should be
learned over the entire recursive process, rather than at in-
dividual editing steps. We achieve this through asymmet-
ric conditioning: the student learns from its own generated
editing trajectories, while the teacher remains anchored to
the original clean image and the accumulated editing in-
structions. This allows us to supervise a long, imperfect
sequence of edits through a shorter, clean alternative path,
without exposing the teacher to the errors accumulated by
the student.
Interestingly, multi-turn editing does not always lead to
degradation. When edits repeatedly replace the subject and
background, existing models can maintain visual quality

even over long sequences (Fig. 7). We attribute this to the
fact that such edits generate new content rather than pre-
serving existing details. In contrast, edits that require con-
tent to persist across many turns are more susceptible to ac-
cumulated errors. Our work therefore focuses on sequences
in which unaffected content must remain consistent.
Our approach substantially reduces accumulated drift,
but some challenges remain.
Particularly difficult edit-
ing instructions may still affect previously edited content
(Fig. 6), suggesting that stability and instruction follow-
ing are related but distinct objectives.
Furthermore, al-
though our method improves stability beyond the sequence
lengths seen during training, extending this robustness to
much longer editing sequences remains an interesting di-
rection for future work.
Beyond image editing, our approach suggests a more
general training principle for recursive generative pro-
cesses. Self-Forcing exposes the model to the distribution it
will encounter during inference, while asymmetric teacher
supervision provides reliable guidance from a clean refer-
ence. Score distillation connects these two paths without
requiring paired target outputs, which is particularly use-
ful when many valid results are possible. We believe this
combination may be useful in other generative tasks where
models repeatedly operate on their own outputs.

Quality.
The judge rates the final image with the image-
quality prompt, checking structural coherence, natural ap-
pearance, absence of artifacts and texture fidelity, and yield-
ing a score from 1 to 5. The prompt describes every im-
age as AI-generated, and on our real photographs the judge
also reports defects in untouched sources (their mean score
is 3.57); since all methods are scored on the same pho-
tographs and compared chain by chain, this affects the abso-
lute scores but not the comparisons. ∆HPSv3 is the change
in HPSv3 [31], a learned estimate of perceived image qual-
ity, from the first edited image (turn 1) to the final image.
Measuring from turn 1 rather than from the source separates
the quality lost over the chain from the effect of a single edit.
Preservation.
On the whole image, the judge rates
consistency with the source using the visual-consistency
prompt. It sees the source, the final image and the chain’s
ten instructions as one numbered list, and scores from 1 to
5 how well everything the instructions do not ask to change
is preserved (5: all such elements unchanged; 1: most of
the scene altered). As controls, on the first 20 EdiVal-10
chains it scores each source compared with itself 5, and each
source compared with the final image of a different chain 1.
Locally, we measure each chain’s anchor objects, which no
15


instruction edits or mentions (Sec. 5.3). We crop the box of
each anchor from the source and from the final image, grey
out in both crops the parts that fall inside the box of any
edited object, and compute the DINOv2 [35] distance (one
minus the cosine similarity of the CLS features) between
the two crops. Because the boxes are coarse, an anchor can
lie largely inside the box of an edited object (e.g., a shirt
worn by a person who is edited), leaving too little of it to
compare. We therefore use an anchor only if at least 30%
of its box remains after the masking, and take a chain’s an-
chor distance as the mean over its usable anchors; 148 of
the 252 chains have at least one, and the anchor distance
is averaged over these chains. We also compute LPIPS [60]
between the source and the final image over the background,
i.e., the whole image outside the boxes of all edited objects
(a median of 57% of the image). Added objects are placed
in this region, so the background distance should be read
together with the instruction-following score.
Editing.
A model can preserve content trivially by not
editing, so we check that the requested edits are carried out
and survive the chain. The judge applies the instruction-
following prompt: it sees the source, the final image and
the chain’s ten instructions as one numbered list, and scores
from 1 to 5 whether the requested changes are present and
correct (5: all present and accurate; 3: partially; 1: not at
all), leaving unrequested changes to the consistency score.
As a negative control, showing the source image instead of
the final image yields a score of 1 for all 20 chains.
C. Long Sequences with Repeated Content Re-
placement
To distinguish sequence length from long-term preserva-
tion demands, we consider a 40-edit sequence produced by
the unmodified distilled FLUX.2 Klein 9B model (Fig. 7).
Starting from a dog in a living room, we repeat four ed-
its ten times: replace the dog with a cat, move the scene
to a beach, replace the cat with a dog, and move the scene
back to a living room. The subject and background are each
replaced every two turns, so neither must retain its origi-
nal details throughout the sequence. The outputs remain
visually coherent through turn 40, without an obvious pro-
gressive loss of visual quality. However, the animal’s ap-
pearance and the room layout vary across cycles; returning
to the same semantic categories does not imply recovery of
the original image. This qualitative example shows that a
long editing sequence need not exhibit progressive degra-
dation when content is repeatedly replaced, and motivates
evaluating stability in settings that require content to persist
across many edits.
