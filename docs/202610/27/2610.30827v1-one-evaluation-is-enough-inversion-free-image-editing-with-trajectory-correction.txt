ABSTRACT
Real-image editing commonly relies on inversion and iterative sampling, while efficient one-step editors often stabilize
a large update by estimating and averaging editing fields at multiple noise levels. We ask a stricter question: is a single
temporal field evaluation sufficient for reliable inversion-free editing? Our answer rests on a previously underused
duality in a variance-preserving (VP) one-step model: one source-target query at a shared noisy state simultaneously
determines a local angular semantic tangent and a clean endpoint displacement. We integrate the tangent over the
analytically known remaining VP angle to obtain a nominal one-step transport, and use the endpoint displacement
to correct its long-horizon extrapolation through a constrained projection. This transport-first, correction-second
construction yields Endpoint-Calibrated Transport (ECT), a closed-form, training-free editor requiring only one noise
level, two text conditions packed into one denoiser batch, and one latent update. ECT requires no image inversion,
trajectory integration, second temporal observation, or iterative optimization, and can be directly applied to pretrained
epsilon-prediction VP one-step generators. Across the reported benchmark settings, ECT delivers stable gains in
editing quality within an extremely short editing time.
Keywords: Text-guided image editing; Inversion-free editing; One-step diffusion models; Endpoint-calibrated transport

Introduction
Text-guided real-image editing must accurately realize a
target instruction while preserving unedited regions, spatial
layout, and subject identity. Existing approaches use diffu-
sion inversion, feature or attention injection, and iterative
denoising to retain source structure. Although effective,
inversion optimization and long sampling trajectories incur
substantial latency and memory cost, limiting interactive
use (Hertz et al., 2022; Mokady et al., 2023; Tumanyan
et al., 2023; Cao et al., 2023).
Recent work improves efficiency along two complemen-
tary directions. Inversion-free editors construct a direct or
consistency-based process between source and target con-
ditions, while distilled generators compress synthesis into
one or a few steps (Xu et al., 2024; Kulikov et al., 2025;
Liu et al., 2023; Sauer et al., 2024; Dao et al., 2024). Yet
high-fidelity direct editors generally still integrate or correct
model fields over multiple times. Even when the final edit
uses one latent update, representative one-step methods sta-
bilize that update with information collected at more than
one noise level (Lu et al., 2026). Reliable editing from one
temporal support thus remains challenging.
This paper studies single-evaluation image editing: can
a stable inversion-free edit be constructed by querying the
source and target conditions at the same noisy state and
at only one noise level? Multiple condition-time branches
can be packed into a single batch, but every additional tem-
poral observation still enlarges the batch, activation traffic,
CPU scheduling, and memory pressure. We therefore char-
acterize an editor by its temporal supports, condition-time
branches, batched denoiser calls, and latent updates. We
seek stable edits without additional temporal observations.
Our key observation is that a single conditional query of
a VP one-step model determines one edit axis: the angu-
lar tangent and clean-endpoint displacement derived from
the shared state are collinear. We calibrate its magnitude
by integrating the frozen local tangent over the known re-
maining VP angle, obtaining a nominal single-evaluation
angular transport. This schedule-based rescaling is a first-
order approximation, not a guarantee that the original dis-
placement underestimates the true edit. A closed-form
endpoint-exposure shrinkage then moderates the extrapo-
lation along the same axis, yielding Endpoint-Calibrated

Related Work
Diffusion-based real-image editing.
Diffusion proba-
bilistic models and their deterministic samplers (Ho et al.,
2020; Song et al., 2020), together with classifier-free guid-
ance (Ho & Salimans, 2022), underlie most text-guided
editors. Noise-and-denoise editing such as SDEdit (Meng
et al., 2022) and instruction-tuned editors such as Instruct-
Pix2Pix (Brooks et al., 2023) modify images directly in
pixel or latent space, while text-guided diffusion editors
manipulate cross-attention, inject intermediate features, or
share self-attention to transfer target semantics while retain-
ing source layout (Hertz et al., 2022; Tumanyan et al., 2023;
Cao et al., 2023). Their preservation ability commonly
depends on DDIM inversion, null-text optimization, or
source-reference branches that reconstruct an input-specific
diffusion trajectory (Mokady et al., 2023; Miyake et al.,
2025). Although these mechanisms provide fine-grained
control, the reconstruction process introduces many sequen-
tial denoising steps, per-image optimization, and storage of
intermediate activations or features. This creates a persistent
trade-off between faithful reconstruction, editability, and
interactive efficiency.
Inversion-free direct editing. Inversion-free methods
avoid reconstructing an exact noise code by coupling source
and target predictions through consistency sampling or con-
ditional transport (Xu et al., 2024; Kulikov et al., 2025).
This direct view removes a major computational bottleneck
and exposes the editing displacement itself as the central
object of study. Trajectory-aware regularization can fur-
ther preserve source structure by constraining conditional
dynamics (Kim et al., 2026), and recent rectified-flow edi-

One-step generation and editing. Distillation, adversar-
ial training, and rectified flow have compressed high-quality
generation into one or a few steps (Liu et al., 2022; Lipman
et al., 2022; Song et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2023; Sauer et al.,
2024; Nguyen & Tran, 2024; Dao et al., 2024), building
on large-scale latent and rectified-flow backbones (Rom-
bach et al., 2022; Podell et al., 2023; Esser et al., 2024;
Yan et al., 2024; Greenberg, 2025). Editing is harder than
unconditional generation because the single large update
must simultaneously introduce target semantics and pre-
serve input-specific content. Existing efficient editors there-
fore retain several generation steps or stabilize a final update
through residuals evaluated at multiple times (Deutch et al.,
2024; Gong et al., 2025; Nguyen et al., 2025; Lu et al.,
2026; Li et al., 2026). We use the VP angular horizon to ob-
tain nominal global transport from one local residual, then
calibrate it with the same query’s clean endpoint signal.
3
Method
ECT denotes the complete editor, including both analytic
stages. We first convert a source-target conditional residual
at one noise level into a nominal endpoint transport using
VP angular geometry. We then apply a proximal endpoint
correction to that transport under an endpoint-exposure con-
straint. Figure 2 contrasts this computation with multi-step
FlowEdit and two-time ChordEdit, while Figure 4 illustrates
the motivation for calibrating a single-step displacement.
3.1
From Two-Time Averaging to One Evaluation
Given a source image Isrc, source prompt csrc, and target
prompt ctar, a pretrained VAE encoder produces xsrc =
E(Isrc). We seek a direct edit with one latent update,
xedit = xsrc +ut.
(1)
Here ut is the single latent edit vector, applied once at noise
level t, that carries the source latent to its edited version;
the whole method is a recipe for constructing it from one
query. A residual observed at one time is local, and naively
enlarging it into a global update can over-edit structure. A
representative remedy averages residuals from two noise
levels (Lu et al., 2026), but even batched into one call it still
uses two temporal supports and four branches.
More generally, let the conditional editing residual at time
t be
Rt(zt) = εφ(zt,t,ctar)−εφ(zt,t,csrc).
(2)
The residual Rt is the only condition-specific signal a single
query provides, so every editor below—including ours—
is ultimately a rule for turning Rt into the update ut of
Equation 1. A two-time estimator constructs a stabilized
direction in the generic form
¯Rt,δ = wtRt(zt)+wt−δRt−δ(zt−δ),
wt +wt−δ = 1,
(3)
where the second observation reduces the uncertainty of ex-
trapolating a local residual into one large update. Effective
as it is, this couples editing to two schedule locations and
doubles the condition-time width, even though the update is
applied once.

4.1
Experimental Setup
Benchmarks and backbones. We evaluate on PIE-Bench
and SNR-Bench with SD-Turbo (SD-T) (Sauer et al., 2024)
and SwiftBrush-v2 (SB-v2) (Dao et al., 2024). The main
setting is strictly single-evaluation—one temporal support,
two text-conditioned branches in one denoiser batch, one
latent update—with t = 0.75, ζ = 0.7, and λ = 1 unless
stated otherwise. Appendix J reports seed and parameter
studies. Extra-NFE variants appear only in the ablations of
Table 3(c) and Appendix J.2, not in the main results.
Metrics. We report MSE, PSNR, SSIM (Wang et al.,
2004), and LPIPS (Zhang et al., 2018), all computed after
masking the edit region so they measure preservation of the
unedited content, and CLIP cosine similarity (Radford et al.,
2021) for target-prompt alignment on the full edited image.
ImgEdit-Judge (Ye et al., 2026) additionally scores Content
and Identity Preservation (CP), Visual Quality and Artifact
Control (VQ), and Physical and Perceptual Coherence (PC)
on independent 1–5 scales. Appendices D and F give the
preprocessing, formulae, aggregation, and full judge proto-
col. System efficiency uses synchronized per-image latency,
time-weighted CPU-process utilization and resident set size
(RSS), and peak PyTorch-reserved VRAM, under identical
backbone, resolution, process scope, and instrumentation
per comparison. These are resource demands, not quality
scores: lower is lighter. Appendix E defines every column
and records temporal supports, condition-time branches,
batched denoiser calls, and latent updates.
Baselines. Our central comparison is the two-temporal-
support ChordEdit estimator (Lu et al., 2026) on the
same one-step backbone.

4.3
System Efficiency
Compared with matched ChordEdit rows in Tables 1 and 2,
ECT reduces synchronized latency by 15.7–20.0%, time-
weighted CPU-process utilization by 42.1–48.0%, and
time-weighted process RSS by 17.0–21.4%. Peak reserved
VRAM changes by only 0.24% (7,480 versus 7,498 MiB).
These paired measurements use common instrumentation
and are not comparable across hardware or process scopes
(Appendix E). The reductions are consistent with halv-
ing condition-time branches, reducing input preparation,
transfers, and intermediate activations. Temporal supports
and branch counts therefore complement batched-call and
latent-update counts when reporting efficiency.
VAE encoding/decoding and other shared work remain
after reducing the branches. Two instead of four conditional
branches therefore need not halve the total latency. Peak re-
served VRAM includes cached device allocations, whereas
process RSS measures host memory; their different scopes
help explain why memory savings need not move together.

background fidelity and semantic editing on SD-T.
Parameter sensitivity. Figure 7(a) compares the LPIPS–
CLIP trade-offs at four evaluation noise levels. Over their
shared CLIP range, t = 0.75 achieves lower LPIPS than t =
0.45, 0.6, and 0.9, supporting its use as the default. Panel (b)
similarly favors the intermediate correction coefficient ζ =
0.7 over 0.5 and 0.9. These plots measure the balance
between preservation and alignment rather than PSNR or
a single metric optimum. Appendix J reports the seed and
remaining parameter controls. In particular, Table 10 shows
that λ = 1 has the best preservation in the tested sweep,
while λ = 2 gains 0.50 CLIP points at a cost of 2.690 dB
PSNR and 37.636 scaled LPIPS units. Thus, λ = 1 favors
preservation over maximizing CLIP alone.
The seed control tests dependence on a favorable noisy
state. Across eleven seeds, the worst result for each preser-
vation metric still outperforms matched ChordEdit (Ap-
pendix J.1), supporting stable benchmark averages without
implying identical edits across seeds.
5
Conclusion
We presented Endpoint-Calibrated Transport (ECT), a
training-free approach to inversion-free image editing that
uses one shared noisy state, one batched denoiser evaluation,
and one latent update. The method turns the source–target
prediction difference into a schedule-aware angular trans-
port and uses the clean endpoint displacement from the
same query to calibrate that transport, so semantic motion
and structural control are obtained without inversion, a
second temporal support, ODE integration, or iterative
optimization. Across the supplied benchmark summaries
and matched backbones, ECT improves PSNR in every
one-step comparison, improves all reported preservation
metrics on SNR-Bench, raises the judged image-edit scores
throughout, and exposes the PIE-Bench SwiftBrush-v2
preservation trade-off rather than hiding it. At the same
time, the matched tables show substantial reductions in
synchronized latency, CPU-process utilization, and process
RSS. These properties make single-temporal-support edit-
ing a compact and practical operating point while keeping
the method’s assumptions and quality–efficiency trade-off
explicit.
Limitations and future work. The current derivation
assumes epsilon-prediction VP one-step backbones and a
shared noisy state, and therefore does not yet cover non-VP
schedules, velocity-prediction models, or spatially adaptive
constraints. The reported efficiency numbers also depend
on hardware, process scope, and measurement protocol.
Future work will extend the formulation to these settings
and evaluate broader controls once the corresponding runs
are available.

User-Study Protocol
Design.
Participants evaluate assigned subsets of PIE-Bench cases; the number of assigned cases need not be identical
across participants. Each case contains the source image, edit instruction, and outputs from TurboEdit, SwiftEdit, ChordEdit,
and ECT. The comparison uses cases with all four outputs. Each participant rates the four methods on the three criteria
below, and the analysis averages the available participant ratings within each case before comparing methods. Consequently,
cases have equal weight in the final win rate even when they receive different numbers of ratings.
Blinding and task.
The four outputs are presented anonymously, with method identities withheld. Each output receives
independent integer scores from 1 (worst) to 5 (best) on the three criteria in Table 7, giving 12 ratings for a completed
participant–case record. A participant may assign the same integer score to multiple outputs; the final ranking uses the mean
across participants, and no ties for the highest case-level mean occurred in the reported results.
