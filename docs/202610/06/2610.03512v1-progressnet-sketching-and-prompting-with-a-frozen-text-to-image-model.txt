ABSTRACT
Humans draw progressively: a few strokes, a look at the result, a stroke erased,
a prompt revised. Image generators do not work this way. They typically take
a finished sketch and produce the image in a single pass, so every edit starts the
picture again, and the models that do keep state across turns are driven by text,
cannot take a stroke, and are too slow to draw with. We present ProgressNet, a
training-free framework that lets a frozen text-to-image model follow a drawing
session as it unfolds: strokes are added and erased, the prompt is revised, and the
image keeps up at about a second per turn. It needs no new parameters because the
frozen model already has what a progressive generator needs, a pathway through
which the previous turn can be remembered, layers that can carry appearance
forward without freezing structure, and an internal signal of how far to trust an
unfinished sketch; three inference-time mechanisms (Previous-Concept Memory,
Layer-Selective K/V Injection and Banded Adaptive Control) use each in turn. As a
sketch fills in, every existing method degrades, the FID of the FLUX+ControlNet
baseline doubling between 10% and 100% completion on FS-COCO, while Pro-
gressNet’s barely moves; it maintains strong fidelity and progressive coherence
across three sketch domains and is preferred by users over five competitors, most
widely on erasure.

INTRODUCTION
Humans are visual creatures. We drew before we wrote, and drawing remains the most natural
way to think through a visual idea and share it. Generative AI has opened a remarkable channel
for this: a sentence now produces a picture (Rombach et al., 2022; Black Forest Labs, 2024), in
well under a second with few-step distillation (Luo et al., 2023; Sauer et al., 2023), and because a
sentence is a poor description of a picture, control has steadily grown beyond text, to edges, depth
and layout (Zhang et al., 2023; Mou et al., 2024), to reference images (Ye et al., 2023; Black Forest
Labs et al., 2025), and most recently to a sketch drawn beside the prompt box (OpenAI, 2026).
Sketch is the natural end of that road: it is how people convey shape and intent when words run
out (Eitz et al., 2012; Koley et al., 2024). Yet every one of these systems takes the sketch as a
finished input and produces the image in a single pass, and nobody draws that way. A drawing is
made over time: a few strokes, a look, a stroke erased, a line redrawn, often a change of mind about
what the picture is of (Fig. 1).
What a person needs is not a picture from a sketch but a picture that keeps up with the sketching:
one that follows each added stroke, lets go of each erased one, absorbs a revised prompt, leaves ev-
erything else untouched, and answers fast enough to feel like a response rather than a resubmission.
This paper is about that interaction. ProgressNet lets a frozen text-to-image model support all of it,
drawing, erasing and prompt revision, at about a second per turn and with no training.

To the best of our knowledge, no existing system does
all. Sketch-conditioned generation (Zhang et al., 2023;
Koley et al., 2024; Li et al., 2024) is stateless: each
turn regenerates the scene from nothing, so appearance
drifts with every stroke and an erased stroke leaves a
ghost, because nothing withdraws the evidence it added
(Fig. 2). Interactive sketch systems lower the cost of re-
generation without removing its statelessness (Sarukkai
et al., 2024; Song et al., 2024), and Subjective Cam-
era (Chen et al., 2025b) follows a drawing order by la-
tent optimisation that takes minutes per scene. Image
editing models (Black Forest Labs et al., 2025; Zhou
et al., 2025; Liao et al., 2026; Xu et al., 2026) do han-
dle turn-by-turn interaction, but for a different kind of
turn: the image enters as a second token stream in joint
attention and the change is given in words, so there is
no pathway for a stroke map and no way to say that a stroke is gone, and tens of denoising steps
over a doubled sequence make each turn take seconds rather than the fraction of a second a draw-
ing person will wait. ProgressNet therefore builds on the architecture that accepts strokes, a frozen
flow-matching backbone with a ControlNet, and stays in the few-step regime that makes interaction
possible.
Following a drawing session with a frozen model raises three problems, and each has a simple an-
swer inside the model. Remembering. The model must remember what it generated last turn. The
ControlNet’s tile branch accepts an RGB image, so the previous output can be fed back as an explicit
appearance memory, and because that memory lives in pixel space the user’s operations map onto it
directly: an added stroke keeps it, an erased stroke white-fills its region, a new prompt relaxes the
regions the user has not committed to (Previous-Concept Memory, PCM; Section 4.1). Carrying
forward. The model must carry that memory without freezing what should change. Training-free
editors inject cached attention at the layers most vital to forming the image (Avrahami et al., 2025);
under progression those are exactly the layers that must absorb new strokes, and locking them yields
images that stop responding as the drawing grows. Injecting at the least vital layers instead keeps
appearance while structure stays free (Layer-Selective K/V Injection, LS-KVI; Section 4.2). Trust-
ing the sketch. The model must balance adherence to an unfinished sketch with preservation of
previous appearance. We regulate this balance indirectly, using changes in cached ControlNet mem-
ory features between the two preceding completed turns to modulate memory strength across layer
bands and solver steps, while structural control remains fixed (Banded Adaptive Control, BAC;
Section 4.3). Together the three add 0.068 s to a two-step FLUX generation.

The result is a generator whose quality does not decay as the drawing fills in: on FS-COCO (Chowd-
hury et al., 2022) the FID of FLUX+ControlNet doubles between 10% and 100% completion and
every baseline degrades the same way, while ProgressNet’s moves by under one point. Because
frame-to-frame similarity alone rewards a method that ignores the new stroke, we introduce a pro-
gressive fidelity-coherence score (PFC) combining temporal coherence with completion-level dis-
tributional quality. Our contributions are (i) the first training-free system for progressive generation
from sketch and text that supports stroke addition, erasure and prompt revision at interactive rates;
(ii) PCM, LS-KVI and BAC, with the findings that vital-layer injection over-locks under progres-
sion and that inter-turn ControlNet feature redistribution is a usable online control signal, validated
across two backbones and four sketch domains; (iii) a protocol and score for progressive genera-
tion, with results on three sketch domains and user study in which ProgressNet is preferred over five
competitors, most widely on erasure. None of the three mechanisms is sketch-specific.

RELATED WORK
Controllable image generation spans ControlNet-style residual conditioning (Zhang et al., 2023),
multi-condition control (Zhao et al., 2023), lightweight adapters (Mou et al., 2024; Ye et al., 2023),
layout grounding (Li et al., 2023b; Bar-Tal et al., 2023), and training-free feature steering (Mo
et al., 2024; Tumanyan et al., 2023), recently ported to DiT and rectified-flow backbones (Esser
et al., 2024; Black Forest Labs, 2024; Tan et al., 2025). Inversion-based editing (Hertz et al., 2023),
attention injection (Avrahami et al., 2025), and few-step distillation (Song et al., 2024; Sauer et al.,
2023) improve editability and latency, bringing sketch interaction near real time (Sarukkai et al.,
2024). Others mitigate text-sketch conflict by re-weighting cross-attention (Chefer et al., 2023;
Phung et al., 2024) or rescaling the control residual (Liu et al., 2024; Cohen et al., 2025). All operate
single-shot: none tracks how sketch–text conflict evolves across turns, nor preserves prior content
as the condition changes. ProgressNet targets this non-stationary setting via training-free temporal
memory, selective feature reuse, and ControlNet-driven adaptive control, detailed in Appendix E.

PRELIMINARIES
ProgressNet builds on flow-matching backbones (Black Forest Labs, 2024; Bandyopadhyay et al.,
2025; Lipman et al., 2023) with Union ControlNet. Joint attention over latent and context tokens
exposes the K/V features later reused by LS-KVI (Section 4.2), while spatial conditions such as
Canny edges and tile memory are introduced through ControlNet residuals, h′ = h + λr. This
formulation also makes the control strength λ a critical factor under few-step inference, where over-
or under-conditioning cannot be corrected over many denoising steps (Section 4.3).
Our pilot studies (Figs. 3a and 3b) show that existing formulations struggle to maintain a coherent
progressive state: appearance can drift between turns, a fixed control strength can either imprint
partial strokes too strongly or under-enforce them, and erased content may persist when the sketch
is revised. ProgressNet addresses these coupled failures through explicit concept memory, selective
temporal feature reuse, and adaptive control modulation.

PROPOSED METHODOLOGY
Overview.
Given a sketch St, prompt Pt, and previous output It−1, we generate It
=
G(St, Pt, It−1; θ) while keeping pretrained weights θ frozen and supporting stroke addition, re-
moval, and prompt updates. ProgressNet combines three mechanisms: (i) PCM uses operation-
aware tile conditioning to preserve prior appearance (Section 4.1); (ii) LS-KVI reuses previous-turn
features only at selected layers to maintain context while permitting structural change (Section 4.2);
and (iii) BAC applies retrospective, step- and band-specific modulation to the memory-conditioning
ControlNet stream using cached inter-turn comparisons.
4.1
PREVIOUS-CONCEPT MEMORY (PCM)
Progressive sketching is underdetermined: partial strokes constrain geometry but say little about
appearance or context, so regenerating each turn independently can alter established content (Koley
et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2025; Luo et al., 2023). Existing sketch-control methods (Zhang et al., 2023;
Zhao et al., 2023) condition only on current strokes, with no memory of previous turns.

4.2
LAYER-SELECTIVE K/V INJECTION (LS-KVI)
PCM anchors successive generations via memory conditioning, but internal representations can still
drift as attention is recomputed at each step. Training-free editors reduce such drift by reusing
attention features, either injecting source features into key DiT layers (Avrahami et al., 2025) or
reusing layer-specific K/V features (Wei et al., 2025; Dalva et al., 2025; Kim et al., 2025). However,
they target prompt-only editing, not the control-driven edits central to progressive drawing.

EXPERIMENTS
Datasets.
We evaluate on three sketch domains: FS-COCO (Chowdhury et al., 2022) (free-
hand scene sketches), Photo-Sketching (Li et al., 2019) (edge-aligned contour sketches), and
Sketchy (Sangkloy et al., 2016) (single-object freehand sketches). Together, they span scene- and
object-level generation as well as freehand and contour-based representations.
Implementation Details. We use FLUX.1-schnell (Black Forest Labs, 2024) with Union Control-
Net at 1024×1024 resolution and two denoising steps. We set αkv=0.3, fmin=0.05, and fmax=1.0.
Experiments are conducted on a single NVIDIA H200 GPU.
Evaluation Protocol and Metrics.
We evaluate generation quality at specific sketch-completion
stages (Table 1) and progressive stability across representative transitions (Table 2), while preserv-
ing stroke order and captions. We measure distributional quality with FID-I (Heusel et al., 2017)
and FID-C (Kynk¨a¨anniemi et al., 2023), semantic fidelity with DINOv2 (Oquab et al., 2024), and
text alignment with CLIPScore (Hessel et al., 2021; Radford et al., 2021) and T5-Cos (BLIP-2 cap-
tions (Li et al., 2023a) + T5 embeddings (Raffel et al., 2020)). For progression, PFC-DINO and
PFC-LPIPS measure temporal stability. We additionally report quality-adjusted cross-completion
robustness in distributional (Dist.), visual (Vis.), textual (Text), and overall dimensions, normalized
to the FLUX baseline (100). Full definitions are provided in Appendix A.2.

CONCLUSION AND LIMITATIONS
We proposed ProgressNet, a training-free framework for progressive generation that stabilizes in-
teractions between evolving strokes and prompts. PCM and LS-KVI preserve temporal consistency,
while BAC uses retrospective memory-feature divergence to adapt memory. ProgressNet enables
progressive drawing by improving fidelity and alignment across completion levels with near-real-
time performance. Our work opens avenues for more modality-agnostic progressive generation.
Limitations. (i) ProgressNet is primarily evaluated with sketch-based control; extending its memory
and adaptive-control mechanisms to other modalities, such as depth and pose, remains unexplored.
(ii) BAC uses predefined layer bands and modulation bounds; automatically identifying control-
sensitive regions on different conditioning modalities is a promising future direction.

A.1
DATASET AND PROGRESSIVE EVALUATION PROTOCOL
We evaluate three complementary sketch domains using fixed evaluation subsets and identical inputs
for every compared method. FS-COCO (Chowdhury et al., 2022) contributes 10,000 temporally
ordered freehand scene sketches with paired photographs and captions. Photo-Sketching (Li et al.,
2019) contributes 500 contour-sketch/image pairs from its 5,000 available pairs. Sketchy (Sangkloy
et al., 2016) contributes 1,250 single-object freehand sketches, stratified uniformly across all 125
categories with 10 samples per category. The resulting generation-quality evaluation contains 11,750
samples. For each sample, the ordered sketch sequence and associated text condition are fixed once
and shared across all methods; progressive states are evaluated at {10, 20, 30, 50, 70, 80, 100}%
completion.

INTERPRETING THE ABLATION PROGRESSION
FID-I and FID-C measure distributional quality in Inception and CLIP image-feature spaces, respec-
tively; DINOv2 measures paired visual-semantic fidelity, while CLIPScore and T5-Cos assess text
alignment. We therefore interpret temporal and fidelity metrics jointly, complemented by qualita-
tive and operation-level evaluations. From 30% completion onward on FS-COCO, the full system
(PCM + LS-KVI + BAC) obtains the strongest FID-C and remains at or above the other ablation
variants on CLIPScore and T5-Cos across the reported stages (Tables 4 and 7). Its FID-C remains
low and stable across 30–100% completion (0.144, 0.141, 0.142, 0.141, 0.143), while CLIPScore
and T5-Cos remain competitive throughout. Together, the full system maintains strong FID-I while
improving FID-C and textual alignment across later completion stages; the qualitative BAC analysis
separately examines difficult sketch–prompt configurations.

B
COMPONENT IMPLEMENTATION AND LS-KVI ANALYSIS
B.1
PREVIOUS-CONCEPT MEMORY IMPLEMENTATION
PCM derives its commitment mask Ct from the processed current sketch. We combine a dilated
stroke-proximity support with flood-filled enclosed regions; the resulting mask marks content that
should remain committed under subsequent interaction. At the two extremes, Ct=1 denotes fully
committed content retained from It−1, whereas Ct=0 denotes fully relaxed regions during a prompt
update; intermediate values provide a soft blend between the previous output and the neutral prior.
For stroke addition, the previous output is used unchanged as memory. For stroke removal, evidence
inside the erased region is withdrawn by neutral white filling before tile conditioning. For prompt
updates, committed content is preserved while uncommitted regions are replaced by the neutral gray
prior, as defined in Eq. (1) of the main paper. The same mask construction is used for all datasets
and all interaction sequences.
Commitment-mask construction.
The soft commitment mask combines two complementary
supports. For stroke-proximity support, pixels with grayscale intensity below 200 are treated as
18


sketch pixels, dilated by 24 pixels using an elliptical kernel, and Gaussian-smoothed with a 41 × 41
kernel. For enclosed regions, we first morphologically close sketch gaps using a 17 × 17 elliptical
kernel, corresponding to an 8-pixel closing radius. We then flood-fill the exterior from the image
boundary and retain the non-exterior region as the enclosed support, followed by Gaussian smooth-
ing with a 21 × 21 kernel. The final commitment mask is the pixelwise maximum of the proximity
and enclosed-region supports and is clipped to [0, 1]. These parameters are held fixed throughout all
reported experiments.
B.2
CHOICE OF VITAL AND NON-VITAL LAYERS
The mechanism is made visible in Fig. 10, which extends the single-sample comparison of Fig. 5
across four diverse FS-COCO scenes. In each case, vital-layer injection progressively overrides
appearance attributes that should remain stable: the clock tower’s warm sunset illumination darkens
into an unnatural palette; the horse rider’s clothing, skin tone, and pose drift across completions; and
the banana flower saturates in color as strokes accumulate.
In contrast, non-vital-layer injection (bottom rows) maintains consistent lighting, identity, and color
across the four examples. Empirically, injecting at these lower-vitality layers preserves appearance
more effectively while allowing structural adaptation. The giraffe retains its pose and forest back-
drop; the clock tower preserves warm tones and adapts precise details correctly; the horse rider
maintains consistent attire and posture; and the banana flower evolves structurally without color
shift. This is precisely the property exploited by LS-KVI under progression: temporal smoothing at
layers whose individual absence leaves generation quality intact (Section 4.2, main paper), enabling
the concept to structurally evolve with new strokes while preserving what the image was. We there-
fore inject at non-vital layers throughout. Under the FLUX vitality analysis, the six lowest-vitality
layers selected for injection are {5, 6, 7, 10, 13, 14}, with αkv=0.3 in all reported experiments. The
cache is refreshed after each generation and cleared on stroke removal so that correspondence path-
ways associated with erased content are not reintroduced.

EXTENDED RELATED WORK
Controllable Image Generation.
ControlNet (Zhang et al., 2023) established spatial condition-
ing through auxiliary branches attached to frozen diffusion backbones, and subsequent works ex-
panded controllability through unified multi-condition models (Zhao et al., 2023; Qin et al., 2023),
lightweight conditioning adapters (Mou et al., 2024; Ye et al., 2023), feedback-based control ar-
chitectures (Li et al., 2024; Zavadski et al., 2024), grounded and regional generation (Li et al.,
2023b; Xie et al., 2023; Bar-Tal et al., 2023), and training-free feature manipulation (Mo et al.,
2024; Tumanyan et al., 2023). Sketch-based conditioning has similarly progressed through ded-
icated sketch-to-image and interactive systems (Sarukkai et al., 2024; Song et al., 2024) toward
increasingly general conditional generators. These developments largely assume a condition that is
already sufficiently specified before generation begins.
The transition from UNet diffusion to DiT and rectified-flow architectures (Peebles & Xie, 2023;
Esser et al., 2024; Black Forest Labs, 2024) has further changed how spatial conditions are in-
jected. Ctrl-Adapter (Lin et al., 2025) bridges pretrained ControlNets to newer diffusion backbones,
OminiControl (Tan et al., 2025) introduces lightweight universal control for FLUX, and Semantic-
Draw (Lee et al., 2025) supports real-time multi-region interaction. ControlNet-XS (Zavadski et al.,
2024) and ControlNet++ (Li et al., 2024) likewise revisit the interaction between the conditioning
pathway and the generator. While these methods considerably expand what can be controlled, their
conditioning remains predominantly generation-centric rather than progression-centric: the model
receives the current condition, but does not explicitly reason about what should remain stable from
the preceding interaction turn. ProgressNet instead treats the previous concept as part of the state of
generation and explicitly distinguishes stroke addition, removal, and prompt reinterpretation.
Real-Time and Iterative Image Editing.
Diffusion editing has been extensively studied through
inversion and attention manipulation (Hertz et al., 2023; Meng et al., 2022; Couairon et al., 2023;
Kawar et al., 2023; Mokady et al., 2023), inpainting (Avrahami et al., 2023; Lugmayr et al., 2022;
Suvorov et al., 2022), instruction-based editing (Brooks et al., 2023), and personalization (Hu et al.,
2022; Ruiz et al., 2023; Gal et al., 2023; Kumari et al., 2023). Such methods enable semantic or
regional modification but generally solve each edit as a fresh optimization, inversion, or generation
problem rather than maintaining a persistent state across a long interaction sequence.
Few-step and distilled generation substantially reduce this cost. Latent Consistency Models (Luo
et al., 2023), SDXS (Song et al., 2024), NitroFusion (Chen et al., 2025a), SDXL-Turbo (Sauer et al.,
2023), and StreamDiffusion (Kodaira et al., 2025) push inference toward interactive rates. However,
low latency alone does not imply temporal consistency: regenerating from a changed sketch can
alter previously established identity, appearance, geometry, or background even when the newly in-
troduced stroke is local. Among interactive sketch systems, Block-and-Detail (Sarukkai et al., 2024)
stabilizes generation through coarse-to-fine re-noising, whereas SDXS (Song et al., 2024) provides
one-step image-conditioned synthesis; both remain vulnerable to drift when the conditioning evolves
progressively.
Recent training-free editing methods are particularly related to our temporal component. Stable-
Flow (Avrahami et al., 2025) identifies vital transformer layers and reuses internal features to pre-
serve content during editing, while FluxSpace (Dalva et al., 2025) exposes semantic directions in
rectified-flow representations and FLUX.1 Kontext (Black Forest Labs et al., 2025) enables itera-
tive in-context editing. Related feature-reuse mechanisms have also been explored for consistency
through cross-image or temporal attention (Alaluf et al., 2024; Cao et al., 2023; Qi et al., 2023).
These works demonstrate that internal attention features provide a strong substrate for consistency,
but progressive sketching requires a different balance: preserving all influential layers too strongly
can suppress newly drawn structure. This motivates our Layer-Selective K/V Injection, which pre-

serves temporal information only through non-vital layers while leaving structurally important layers
free to adapt.
Condition Misalignment and Adaptive Control.
A separate literature studies the failure of text
and visual conditions to remain semantically aligned. Text-to-image models exhibit semantic ne-
glect, incorrect attribute binding, and word-to-concept mismatch (Rassin et al., 2022; Wang et al.,
2024), with such failures analyzed through cross-attention (Tang et al., 2023) and latent-space repre-
sentations (Ramos et al., 2025). Structured Diffusion (Feng et al., 2023) decomposes prompts for im-
proved compositionality, while Attend-and-Excite (Chefer et al., 2023), Linguistic Binding (Rassin
et al., 2023), and Attention Refocusing (Phung et al., 2024) intervene on attention to improve se-
mantic correspondence.
More directly related to ProgressNet, SmartControl (Liu et al., 2024) adapts ControlNet behavior
under rough or unreliable visual conditions, while Text-Anchored Score Composition (Wang et al.,
2024) explicitly addresses conflict between textual and spatial conditions. Conditional Balance (Co-
hen et al., 2025) controls competing conditioning signals through selective feature manipulation, and
training-free alternatives such as Ctrl-X (Lin et al., 2024) and FreeControl (Mo et al., 2024) regulate
structure and appearance without conventional task-specific training. Collectively, these methods
show that a single fixed global control scale is insufficient when text and structural evidence dis-
agree.
Progressive sketching makes this conflict substantially more difficult because the disagreement it-
self changes over time. A sparse early contour may be highly ambiguous or even contradict the
prompt, whereas later strokes can progressively resolve the intended geometry. The appropriate
strength of control can therefore vary across interaction turns, architectural layers, and denoising
steps. Our pilot analysis further shows that ControlNet perturbations are step- and band-asymmetric
and that large inter-turn feature redistribution accompanies structurally disruptive stroke updates.
ProgressNet exploits this behavior through Banded Adaptive Control (BAC), deriving step- and
band-specific correction factors from retrospective comparisons of cached tile-memory ControlNet
features across preceding turns. Unlike learned control policies (Liu et al., 2024) or manually se-
lected control locations (Cohen et al., 2025), BAC derives its modulation directly from the evolving
control representation and requires no additional training.
