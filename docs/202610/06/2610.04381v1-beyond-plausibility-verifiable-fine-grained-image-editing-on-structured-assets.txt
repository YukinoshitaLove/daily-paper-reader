Abstract 

Fine-grained image editing requires more than producing a visually plausible result: an editor must execute the re-quested attribute change precisely while leaving everything else intact. However, existing benchmarks leave a critical gap between realism and verifiability: benchmarks built on realistic images typically rely on human or vision–language model judgments, while deterministic evaluation has largely focused on synthetic shape canvases, with application-oriented extensions primarily limited to charts. This makes it difficult to determine precisely how much of a requested edit was executed, where unintended changes occurred, and whether small differences between models reflect gen-uine editing capability or evaluator uncertainty. To bridge this gap, we present VeriEdit-Bench , a benchmark for fine-grained, instruction-faithful image editing across realistic structured assets with deterministic, four-axis evalua-tion. Its 1,740 cases are compiled from the source code of 153 Scalable Vector Graphics (SVG) graphics, charts, web interfaces, and presentation slides. Controlled source-code edits preserve the original visual context while yielding exact target images, pixel-level edit masks, and explicit edit specifications, enabling reproducible scoring along four axes: edit fidelity, preservation, localization, and magnitude. Evaluating eleven editors, we find that even the strongest model remains far from full credit; rankings for the same recoloring operation reverse between charts and SVG graph-ics; and outputs with similar pixel-accuracy profiles can still differ substantially in localization and change magnitude. This decomposition yields graded, verifiable feedback and exposes model-specific capability and failure profiles that holistic scores or evaluator-dependent judgments may obscure. 1arXiv:2610.04381v1 [cs.CV] 3 Oct 2026 1 Introduction 

Image editing has rapidly evolved from coarse semantic manipulation toward instruction-following systems capable of modifying increasingly specific visual attributes while preserving the surrounding content. This progress has motivated a parallel line of work on image editing benchmarks. Early and widely used benchmarks evaluate editing models on real images through human judgment, perceptual similarity, or vision–language models (VLMs), covering object addition, removal, replacement, and attribute modification (Zhang et al., 2023; Basu et al., 2023; Ma et al., 2024). More recent benchmarks broaden this scope toward complex, multi-turn, and reasoning-intensive editing (Ye et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2025b; Han et al., 2025). Meanwhile, increasingly sophisticated VLM-based evaluators decompose editing quality into fine-grained factors such as instruction fidelity, preservation, localization, and perceptual quality (Liu et al., 2026). Together, these benchmarks have substantially improved the coverage and interpretability of image editing evaluation. Despite this progress, a central challenge remains in combining realism with verifiability . Existing benchmarks already provide reference images, spatial grounding, and fine-grained assessments of edit correctness and content preserva-tion (Zhang et al., 2023; Qian et al., 2025). For open-ended edits of realistic images, however, multiple outputs may satisfy the same instruction, and evaluation commonly relies on human judgments, learned perceptual measures, or VLM judges. Such scores do not necessarily provide direct, deterministic verification against an exact reference, so it remains difficult to determine precisely how much of a requested change was executed, where unintended changes occurred, or whether small differences between models reflect capability or evaluator uncertainty (Kumar et al., 2026). Deterministic benchmarks offer an important step toward direct verification. PaintBench constructs precisely spec-ified operations and evaluates their outputs against pixel-level ground truth, primarily on synthetic shape canvases, with application-oriented extensions to charts (Xu et al., 2026). StructBench extends evaluation to structured visual generation and editing, assessing factual correctness through VLM-based questions (Zhuo et al., 2025). PaintBench establishes deterministic verification of specified edits, including a chart extension; StructBench uses rendered targets to assess structured facts primarily through VLM questions. What remains underexplored is how target execution and unintended changes vary across realistic structured assets under a common deterministic protocol. We introduce VeriEdit-Bench to bridge this gap. Our key insight is that structured visual assets provide editable source representations that explicitly define their visual attributes—a property typically unavailable in natural-image editing. Rather than synthesizing isolated geometric scenes, we construct 1,740 editing cases by editing 153 con-structed source assets: Scalable Vector Graphics (SVG) graphics, charts, web interfaces, and slides. Each edit modifies a specified attribute while preserving the remaining source context. Rendering the original and edited sources yields exact input–target pairs and pixel-level edit masks, accompanied by explicit edit specifications. The source is used only to construct and score tasks; the evaluated editor receives the rendered image and the instruction alone, so the benchmark measures raster editing rather than code manipulation. These annotations enable deterministic evaluation along four complementary axes: edit fidelity , whether the requested result is achieved; preservation , whether unre-lated content remains intact; localization , whether modifications are confined to the intended region; and magnitude ,whether their extent matches the requested change. This formulation turns fine-grained editing into directly verifiable measurements, providing graded feedback even when an edit is only partially successful. Finally, evaluating eleven contemporary image editors shows that precise editing remains challenging: the best open-weight model reaches a composite of 22 out of 100. The same recoloring operation ranks two models in opposite order on charts and SVG graphics; outputs with nearly identical PaintBench accuracy curves differ sharply in localization and magnitude; and strong target-color attainment coexists with damage to surrounding content. Controlled pixel- and source-level perturbations confirm that each axis responds to the error it is designed to detect. VeriEdit-Bench thus provides a diagnostic account of both the capabilities that current models already possess and the failures that still prevent faithful execution. Our contributions are summarized as follows: • We introduce VeriEdit-Bench , a benchmark for fine-grained, instruction-faithful image editing containing 1,740 cases constructed from 153 SVG graphics, charts, web interfaces, and presentation slides. By applying con-trolled edits to source representations, each case provides an exact target image, pixel-level edit mask, and explicit edit specification. • We propose a deterministic four-axis evaluation framework that decomposes image editing performance into edit fidelity , preservation , localization , and magnitude , enabling reproducible and graded measurement of partially 2correct edits without relying on human or VLM judgments for the core evaluation, and validate its response with controlled pixel-level and source-level perturbations. • We conduct a systematic evaluation of eleven contemporary image editing models and reveal distinct capabil-ity and failure profiles that can be obscured by aggregate or evaluator-dependent scores, providing diagnostic evidence for where current models succeed and where fine-grained editing remains challenging. 

2 Related Work 

2.1 Benchmarks and Evaluation for Image Editing 

Image editing benchmarks have expanded both task coverage and evaluation granularity. MagicBrush (Zhang et al., 2023) provides human-annotated editing triplets, while I 2EBench (Ma et al., 2024) introduces multidimensional eval-uation validated against human perception. ImgEdit (Ye et al., 2025) and Complex-Edit (Yang et al., 2025b) extend evaluation to multi-turn editing and compositional instruction complexity, respectively. GIE-Bench (Qian et al., 2025) separates functional correctness from preservation through visual question answering (VQA) and object-aware mea-surements. LMM4Edit (Xu et al., 2025) and factorized VLM judges (Liu et al., 2026) further provide fine-grained assessment of instruction fidelity, edit quality, and preservation. What these benchmarks do not provide is determin-istic verification of what changed, where, and by how much against an exact reference. PaintBench (Xu et al., 2026) offers a complementary approach by evaluating precisely specified operations through deterministic pixel-level com-parison, primarily on procedurally generated shape canvases, with extensions to charts. VeriEdit-Bench extends this measurement principle to fine-grained attribute changes embedded in realistic structured assets. 

2.2 Generation and Editing of Structured Visuals 

Research on structured visuals examines factual correctness, reasoning, and visual fidelity. StructBench (Zhuo et al., 2025) evaluates factual correctness through decomposed VLM questions, while DisciplineGen (Wang et al., 2026b) studies data construction and model training for academic and scientific visuals. PlanViz (Li et al., 2026) targets spa-tial and procedural reasoning; VDE Bench (Yi et al., 2026) and OCRGenBench (Zhang et al., 2025) emphasize text modification and document or structural fidelity. ChartSync (Yu et al., 2026a) uses programmatically rendered targets to study coupled changes in chart values and geometry, combining objective measurements with VLM-based judg-ments. FigEdit (Li et al., 2025) shares our specification-to-target rendering foundation, covering single, composite, and interactive chart edits. It combines image similarity, text recognition, and model-based evaluation; our focus is a common deterministic capability profile across asset types. Table 29 summarizes asset coverage, editing operations, and verification in related datasets. Structured representations also provide an interface for generation and editing. SVGEditBench V2 (Nishina & Matsui, 2025), VectorEdits (Kuchar et al., 2026), and Vector-Bench (Gupta & Hebbar, 2026) evaluate SVG editing; Vector-Bench uses deterministic specifications to assess target attributes and protected structure. DiagramGenBenchmark and DiagramAgent (Wei et al., 2025), alongside VCG-Bench (Su et al., 2026), formulate diagram generation and refinement through code. For presentations, object-model-based editing (Jung et al., 2026) directly modifies structured slide representations, while I2E (Yu et al., 2026b) reconstructs an editable environment from image pixels. These approaches therefore edit through a structured representation, either given or recovered from pixels. VeriEdit-Bench instead evaluates editors that operate directly on the rendered image; the source is used only to construct and score tasks.

4 Experiments 

4.1 Experimental Setup 

We evaluate eleven editors. Ten open-weight models: FireRed-Edit (Qiao et al., 2026), FLUX.2-dev and FLUX.2-klein (Black Forest Labs, 2025), FLUX.1-Kontext (Black Forest Labs et al., 2025), Qwen-Image-Edit and Qwen-Image-Edit-2511 (Wu et al., 2025a) 1, BAGEL (Deng et al., 2025), Step1X-Edit (Liu et al., 2025), OmniGen2 (Wu et al., 2025b), and InstructPix2Pix (Brooks et al., 2023); GPT-Image-2 (OpenAI, 2026a) provides a closed-source comparison. We evaluate one output per model and case using model-specific inference settings. Images are centered on a white square canvas with their aspect ratio preserved; outputs are mapped back to the reference canvas for scoring. Ap-pendix B.4 specifies the checkpoints, processing resolutions, and coordinate mapping. All analyses use the same model outputs. The primary score, cell-mean H, averages per-case composites within each asset–operation cell and weights the 22 cells equally; individual-axis entries in Table 1 average cases equally. Confidence intervals for H come from 10,000 source-level bootstrap resamples within each asset type, keeping all operations of a source together and sharing draws across models, reported as 95% bias-corrected and accelerated (BCa) intervals (Appendix A.13). Seed and instruction-wording controls are reported in Appendix A.8. 

Scorer validation. We check that each axis responds to the error it is designed to detect. On all 1,740 cases, the rendered target scores (100 , 100 , 100 , 100) and the unchanged input (0 , 100 , 0, 0) . Controlled perturbation results appear in Appendix A.6. Constructed source-level editing errors give complementary mean responses: omitting one color group lowers F to 49 and L to 70 while preservation stays at 100; editing an extra group keeps F = 96 but lowers P to 72; and swapping target colors keeps L ≈ 100 but drops F to 3. Each failure mode is thus visible on the axis that measures it.

Measuring it alongside preservation and localization reveals whether progress toward the requested attribute damages the existing design.

Aggregation changes what the summary rewards. We compare harmonic, geometric, and arithmetic means of each output’s four axes, followed by the same cell weighting (Figure 7). 5 Conclusion 

We introduced VeriEdit-Bench to bridge the gap between realism and verifiability in fine-grained image editing. Controlled source-code edits across SVG graphics, charts, web interfaces, and slides yield exact targets, edit masks, and specifications for deterministic evaluation of fidelity, preservation, localization, and visual change magnitude. Our evaluation of eleven editors shows that precise editing remains challenging. Similar pixel-accuracy profiles can conceal distinct failures, strong target-color attainment can coexist with poor preservation or localization, and model advantages can reverse across asset types for the same recoloring operation. VeriEdit-Bench supports reproducible diagnosis of capabilities and the contexts in which gains hold, beyond aggregate scores. 9References

Local errors and small perturbations. Setup. A fixed panel takes five cases per asset–operation cell, prioritizing source diversity. Each condition in Table 8 uses all 110 cases. We vary the fraction of target-region pixels edited, the pixel-change amplitude, and background 21 color, and apply blur, JPEG compression, shifts, 1% centered rescaling, and deterministic reconstruction through the Stable Diffusion v1.4 variational autoencoder (VAE). Shifting the entire target moves protected content; shifting only the target-minus-input difference isolates displacement of the requested change. Rescaling uses bilinear interpolation and center cropping or edge padding; the VAE uses the latent mode in float32. 

Results and analysis. Replacing half the allowed pixels with target values preserves the background and earns partial fidelity and localization. A background color offset lowers preservation and localization even when the target region is correct; fidelity can also fall near the boundary because its comparison is smoothed. Editing half the region and applying half the change amplitude produce distinct profiles, separating spatial coverage from change strength. Mild blur leaves the scores near the reference, whereas compression, displacement, and rescaling lower appearance and spatial agreement (Table 8). VAE reconstruction preserves magnitude more closely than target appearance: fidelity has a median of 91.57, with 8 of 110 cases below 40. These responses distinguish agreement with the reference appearance from the strength of the visual change, and quantify their sensitivity to image transformations. The scoring stage itself applies no resampling: editor outputs are restored to the reference coordinates by the shared wrapper before scoring (Appendix B.4), and the constructed variants inherit the canvas dimensions. These rows therefore quantify transformations that arise editor-side in deployed editors, not artifacts of the evaluation pipeline. B Dataset Construction and Scoring Details

B.1 Source Asset Construction 

For SVG assets, we adapt the template generation stage of Chat2SVG (Wu et al., 2025c), and generation components of IntroSVG (Wang et al., 2026a), with simplified variants and custom outline templates. Chart generation uses ref-erence code from ChartMimic (Yang et al., 2025a), alongside LLM-based and programmatic synthesis inspired by ChartLlama (Han et al., 2023) and FigureQA (Kahou et al., 2018), respectively. For web assets, we adapt OpenUI 2

system prompts, draw on WebGen-Bench (Lu et al., 2025) for instruction design, and use a custom two-stage gener-ation workflow with layout categories informed by Open Design 3. For slides, we adapt AutoPresent (Ge et al., 2025) code generation prompts and convert the resulting slides to HTML. The source counts refer to constructed assets, not deployed websites or collected presentation files. The SVG pool has 12 sources from each of four construction variants (Chat2SVG, a simplified variant, IntroSVG, and custom outlines). Charts comprise 10 ChartMimic-inspired, 10 ChartLlama-inspired, and five FigureQA-inspired sources. Web assets comprise 25 custom two-stage, 13 OpenUI-prompt, and 13 WebGen-inspired sources. 

Task verification. 

We require paired renders to have matching canvas dimensions, a visible requested change, preserved surrounding layout, and agreement between the instruction and the edited objects and parameters. Repeated rendered answers for the same source and operation are deduplicated. AI-assisted screening uses Kimi K3 (Kimi Team et al., 2026), GLM-5.3-Flash (Z.ai, 2026), and GPT-6 (OpenAI, 2026b). All retained cases have an AI inspection record.An author with a computer vision research background reviews all retained tasks and resolves reported ambiguities against the editable sources. Object references are stated in visible terms, and rendered targets preserve background cutouts and other protected content. Inputs, targets, masks, and edit metadata are retained together. 

Source-level variation. We describe the 153 sources before counting their edit variants (Figure 21). For these de-scriptors only, source images are resized to at most 512 pixels on the longest side; color entropy uses eight bins per RGB channel, and edge density uses fixed Canny thresholds of 80 and 160. These pixel descriptors capture variation in palette and visual structure; sources differ within each asset type. Model-specific image preparation is specified in Appendix B.4.

B.2 Image Preparation and Scoring Details Image preparation. After the inference wrapper restores the reference canvas (Appendix B.4), the scorer compares RGB images at the target dimensions. Its fallback for mismatched dimensions uses bicubic interpolation; when the absolute difference in aspect ratio exceeds 0.02, centered black padding precedes resizing. No content-based regis-tration is applied. The allowed region in Equation 1 follows rendered change, including vacated and newly occupied pixels, while unchanged pixels inside an object’s bounding box remain protected. 

Fidelity and preservation. For fidelity, each image is smoothed by a 3×3 Gaussian filter with σ = 0 .6, and the mask is dilated by a 3×3 rectangular kernel, giving eI, eT , eO and E+. Both the prediction–target agreement and unchanged-input baseline use the same channel and tolerances {5, 10 , 20 }. A baseline b > 0.995 is flagged as uninformative and receives zero fidelity; an empty mask also receives zero fidelity. Preservation instead uses unblurred images, the original mask, and tolerances {2, 5, 10 , 20 }. When no pixels lie outside E, preservation is 100 because there is no protected region to evaluate. 

Spatial change maps. We use a localization threshold of 6 if at least max(16 , 0.05 |E|) input–target pixels exceed that distance, and 2 otherwise. Thresholding input–target and input–output differences gives G and the candidate output change map; an empty G falls back to E. All candidate pixels inside E are retained. Outside E, only eight-connected components with at least max(16 , ⌊0.0005 N ⌋) pixels survive, where N is the canvas area. This produces D without erasing coherent thin bands. Empty detected changes or zero recall/precision give L = 0 . The precision denominator caps background change at 4|E| to keep widespread redraw from overwhelming spatial diagnosis; preservation still measures agreement across the full protected region. B.3 Separate Color-attainment Measurement 

CIEDE2000 accounts for perceptual nonuniformity in Lab color space; the color-agreement tests and change-region thresholds instead use Euclidean Lab distance (CIE76). For the 621 covered color-editing cases, we compare predicted colors with target references inside stable object cores. These cores follow the stored object metadata: two erosions 35 with a 3 × 3 kernel, or distance-transform ridges for thin objects. A pixel matches its nearest target color within CIEDE2000 tolerance min(5 , 0.4dsrc ,tgt ), where the source–target separation is recorded during construction; the tolerance is 5 when that separation is unavailable. Target references use these same cores. We retain a single-color reference if it covers the core sufficiently for full credit. Otherwise, and for multicolor references, we augment the stored colors with the most frequent uncovered target colors until every core pixel is within tolerance. Target shares use the same nearest-color assignment as prediction shares; unused color centers are removed. Reference construction never uses model outputs.
