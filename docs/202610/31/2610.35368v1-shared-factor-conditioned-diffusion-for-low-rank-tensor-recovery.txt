Shared-Factor-Conditioned Diffusion for Low-Rank
Tensor Recovery
Zhengyun Cheng , Guanwen Zhang , and Wei Zhou
Abstract—Tensor recovery spans heterogeneous data structures
and observation processes, calling for a representation that can
adapt across modalities and tasks. We propose DiffTR, an unsu-
pervised framework that unifies tensor completion, denoising, and
hyperspectral super-resolution through coordinate-conditioned
diffusion. Shared factors indexed by tensor coordinates condition
a compact score network, and score matching jointly learns the
factors and an implicit tensor representation from the available
observations. Scalar and vector-valued outputs accommodate in-
dividual entries and entire tubes, enabling a common formulation
for tabular tensors, images, and videos. The framework separates
representation learning from task-specific observation modeling:
reverse diffusion recovers missing values; estimation via Tweedie’s
formula, soft thresholding, and optional spatial consistency
support mixed-noise denoising; and measurement-guided sampling
transfers a prior learned from low-resolution hyperspectral
observations to high-resolution reconstruction, followed by post-
sampling refinement with data consistency and spectral sub-
space regularization. This separation makes the representation
compatible with different corruption models and permits task-
specific constraints to be incorporated within the same recovery
framework. Experiments across multiple tensor modalities and
recovery tasks demonstrate competitive reconstruction quality and
the flexibility of a unified conditional generative representation.
The code is available at https://github.com/CZY-Code/DiffTR

I. INTRODUCTION
Tensor recovery estimates an underlying tensor from in-
complete, corrupted, or indirect measurements. This problem
arises in sparse multiway tables, continuous-time records, RGB
images, multispectral images, hyperspectral images, and videos.
Applications span completion [1], [2], denoising, spectral
super-resolution, factor analysis [3], sequence modeling [4],
model compression [5], [6], and generative modeling [7],
[8]. A general recovery framework must accommodate both
heterogeneous structure and heterogeneous observations. Sparse
tables encode interactions among discrete entities, whereas
visual tensors exhibit spatial, spectral, and temporal correlations.
Missing entries, missing tubes, mixed noise, and resolution
loss impose different constraints on these structures. We use
MSI and HSI to denote multispectral and hyperspectral images,
respectively.
Classical low-rank tensor recovery combines a tensor repre-
sentation, an observation model, and an optimization procedure.

are contracted into tensor entries. Structure search improves
the representation by adapting network topology and mode
assignments [15], [16], but the resulting model still relies
on an explicit contraction. Many methods based on implicit
neural representations (INRs) extend tensor models with fixed
contraction structures to continuous domains [17], [18]. Their
effectiveness therefore depends on matching the contraction
structure to the data, which can be challenging when spatial,
spectral, temporal, and entity-level dependencies differ across
modalities.
CoSTCo and POND address the restrictions of multilinear
interactions through convolutional networks and Gaussian
processes with neural kernels, respectively [19], [20]. Energy-
based tensor models learn entry–factor compatibility without
prescribed contractions, but primarily operate on scalar entries
[21], [22]. Extending these formulations to joint tube recovery
and indirect measurements requires corresponding treatment
of vector-valued outputs and observation constraints. Building
on these advances, we seek a common conditional diffusion
framework that accommodates heterogeneous tensor modali-
ties, scalar entries and vector-valued tubes, and task-specific
observation models across completion, denoising, and HSI
super-resolution.
We introduce DiffTR, a diffusion framework conditioned
on coordinate-indexed tensor factors. For each mode, an R-
dimensional factor vector is associated with every index or
coordinate, either retrieved from a factor matrix for discrete
modes or generated by an implicit neural representation for
continuous modes. Atoms that share an index within the
same mode reuse the corresponding factor vector, so that
their observations jointly inform its learning. The resulting
factor vectors are concatenated to condition a score network.
Here, an atom refers to a scalar entry or a vector-valued tube,
indexed by its positions across the modes. Denoising score
matching jointly learns the factors and the conditional score,
and reverse diffusion recovers the target atoms. We define
the representation rank R as the feature dimension of each
factor vector. By learning interactions among factors, the score
network provides a flexible alternative to prescribed multilinear
contractions.
DiffTR adopts a unified coordinate-conditioned diffusion for-
mulation for completion, denoising, and HSI super-resolution,
with learning and recovery procedures tailored to the available
observations. For completion, it learns from observed entries
or tubes and generates the missing ones. For denoising, it
alternates score learning, clean-value estimation via Tweedie’s

formula, and sparse-noise separation, with optional spatial
consistency enforcement. For HSI super-resolution, it learns
from the upsampled LR-HSI, guides reverse diffusion with

The main contributions are summarized as follows:
• We formulate tensor recovery as conditional diffusion
over scalar entries or vector-valued tubes, where shared
mode-wise factors condition a score network that learns
factor interactions.
• We develop an extensible, unsupervised framework that
learns from each tensor’s own observations and supports
completion, denoising, and HSI super-resolution across
tabular, image, and video tensors via task-specific obser-
vation models.
• We evaluate DiffTR across tensor modalities and recovery
tasks, demonstrating competitive reconstruction quality
and analyzing the effects of key components and compu-
tational cost.
II. RELATED WORK
A. Tensor Completion
Tensor completion exploits dependencies among modes to
infer missing values. Classical approaches use CP, Tucker,
and tensor-network factorizations [9], [12], [13], unfolding-
based trace norms [23], or tensor-tensor-product models [24].
Recent work combines low-rank Tucker factor priors with
spatial–spectral graph regularization to capture global corre-
lations and nonlinear manifold structure in HSI completion
[25]. Bayesian and nonparametric methods incorporate rank
adaptation, nonlinear interactions, and uncertainty [1], [20],
[26], [27], while continuous-time models capture temporal
variation through splines or neural dynamics [28]–[30]. Neural
methods learn factor-to-value mappings, continuous tensor
functions, frequency-aware representations, and deep latent
parameterizations [17]–[19], [31], [32]. EnergyTD and ScoreTR
further introduce implicit distributional modeling [21], [22].
Existing formulations typically target specific output units or
coordinate domains, making it difficult to jointly support scalar
entries, vector-valued tubes, and side information. Prescribed
contractions can also restrict expressiveness when their structure
poorly matches the dependencies in different tensor modalities.
DiffTR addresses these limitations by supporting scalar and
tube-valued atoms within a unified conditional diffusion
formulation. Mode-wise factors are retrieved for discrete
indices or generated by INRs for continuous coordinates,
including timestamps. A conditional score network learns their
interactions without prescribing a multilinear contraction.
B. Tensor Denoising
Tensor denoising combines structural priors with noise
models. Tensor robust principal component analysis separates
low-rank structure from sparse outliers [33], while Schatten-p
regularization provides a nonconvex low-rank prior [34]. A
concave singular-value penalty combined with gradient tensors
has been proposed to balance low-rankness and smoothness
in sparse-noise removal [35]. Learned factorizations and
continuous tensor functions provide flexible representations
[17], [36], while total variation (TV) and coordinate-Jacobian
regularization promote spatial smoothness [18]. Score matching
offers a distributional approach by estimating gradients of noise-
perturbed log densities [37], [38].
The effectiveness of these methods depends on how well
their structural priors and noise assumptions match the data.
Mixed-noise recovery requires coordinated treatment of dense
noise and sparse outliers, since fitting corrupted values directly
can embed noise in the learned representation. DiffTR alternates
score learning, clean-value estimation via Tweedie’s formula,
and sparse-noise separation by soft thresholding. An optional
spatial-consistency loss penalizes score differences at nearby
coordinates, incorporating local smoothness into the same
conditional representation.
C. Hyperspectral Image Super-Resolution
HSI super-resolution combines the spectral information
of an LR-HSI with the spatial information of an HR-MSI.
Coupled tensor methods share latent structure between the two
observations [39], [40]. Other approaches employ generalized
tensor nuclear norms [41], nonlinear transforms [42], or self-
supervised decomposition networks [43]. Fang et al. estimate
spatial coefficients in an LR-HSI-derived spectral subspace
using a spatial-frequency network and data-fidelity refinement
[44]. Diffusion and score-based models offer generative priors
[45], [46], while diffusion posterior sampling uses measure-
ments to guide reverse diffusion [47].
Generative priors describe plausible HSI structure, while
reconstruction must also remain consistent with both the LR-
HSI and HR-MSI observations. Spatial blur and downsampling
couple neighboring locations, requiring observation constraints
that account for the full spatial and spectral degradation
operators. DiffTR learns a conditional HSI score from the
upsampled LR-HSI and uses HR-MSI measurements to guide
reverse diffusion. Post-sampling refinement combines data
consistency under both full observation operators with spectral
subspace regularization, extending the common tensor recovery
formulation to HSI super-resolution.

IV. EXPERIMENTS
We evaluate DiffTR on tabular completion, visual inpaint-
ing, mixed-noise denoising, and HSI super-resolution. The
experiments measure recovery accuracy across modalities and
examine how coordinate conditioning, task-specific observation
models, and computational budget affect performance.
A. Tensor Completion
1) Sparse Tensor Completion: We evaluate DiffTR on
two sparsely observed tensors [50]. Alog is a file-access log
with dimensions 200 users ×100 actions ×200 resources and
approximately 0.33% nonzero entries. ACC is constructed from
a code-repository management system and has dimensions
3k users ×150 actions ×30k resources, with approximately
0.009% nonzero entries. We follow the split of [21] and report
five-fold cross-validation results.
The baselines cover deterministic, probabilistic, neural, and
undirected generative tensor models: CP-WOPT [49], GPTF
[1], HGP-GPTF [27], POND [20], CoSTCo [19], EnergyTD
[21], and ScoreTR [22]. We evaluate factor dimensions
R ∈{3, 5, 8, 10} for all applicable methods. POND is omitted
from ACC because of its reported computational and memory
requirements in this setting.
Table I shows that DiffTR achieves the lowest mean RMSE
in all eight dataset–rank comparisons and the lowest mean MAE
in seven; ScoreTR leads MAE on Alog at R = 3. At R = 10,
DiffTR reduces RMSE relative to ScoreTR, the strongest base-
line, by 3.2% on Alog and 8.8% on ACC. Its RMSE decreases
as R increases on both datasets, showing that the learned factor
interactions effectively use additional representation capacity.
These results demonstrate the effectiveness of DiffTR’s shared-
factor-conditioned diffusion for recovering sparsely observed
multiway data.

mobile applications, and contains approximately 5 × 104

B. Noise-Aware Denoising and Proximal Guidance
We compare two fitting procedures on matched 96 × 96
crops of WDC and PaviaU: BCD alternates sparse separation
and model updates (Algorithm 2), whereas Likelihood fits a
deterministic estimate using the noise model in Section III-C.
Proximal then refines the Likelihood output with fixed model
parameters; g controls the correction strength. Two-stage
diffusion uses the fitted estimate as its training target and applies
the correction during sampling. Each configuration uses one
training run; Table XII reports an additional independent Case 2
run. Objectives and protocols are specified in Appendix B.
Table IX shows that likelihood fitting improves PSNR over
BCD in five of six cases. Post-fit proximal refinement with g =
1 further raises WDC Case 2 PSNR from 35.84 to 36.96 dB,
while the initial estimate remains best in Case 1 on both datasets.
In two-stage diffusion (Table X), changing g from 0 to 1 raises
WDC Case 2 PSNR from 34.14 to 36.17 dB. Increasing g to
4 reduces PSNR throughout both tables, showing that stronger
proximal correction is not consistently beneficial.
Table XI compares MSE and Huber fitting with diffusion
trained directly on noisy observations. Huber fitting performs
best on both WDC cases, highlighting the importance of
handling corruption in the fitting objective and training targets.
The additional Case 2 run in Table XII reproduces the gains
from likelihood fitting over BCD and from proximal refinement
with g = 1 over the initial estimate on both datasets.
C. Sampling Depth
We examine the reverse-sampling stage of Algorithm 3

VI. CONCLUSION
We introduced DiffTR, a unified tensor recovery framework
built on shared-factor-conditioned score learning. The same
representation supports scalar entries and vector-valued tubes,
while task-specific observation models extend recovery from
completion to mixed-noise denoising and HSI super-resolution.
Likelihood fitting and proximal correction accommodate cor-
ruption models, and optional spatial consistency provides a
coordinate-based alternative to TV. Experiments across tabular
tensors, images, and videos demonstrate the framework’s broad
applicability, competitive recovery quality, and efficient neural
representation. This separation of representation learning and
observation modeling provides a foundation for extending
DiffTR to additional tensor structures and inverse problems.

E. Baseline Budgets and Resource Measurement
Baselines receive DiffTR’s measured initialization-and-training time for each scene and setting. CS2DIPs selects the lowest
observed-data Huber loss within 5,000 updates; GTNN and GNLTN allow up to 100 and 50 iterations. Their implementations
use Lloyd clustering, bicubic initialization, matched degradation operators, and CPU LAPACK SVD for selected operations.
EDIP-Net allocates 2/9 of its budget to stage two (at most 4,000 updates) and the remainder to stage three; learning rate 0.004
is constant for half of each phase, then decays linearly. Allocation rules and architectures are fixed, and retained outputs satisfy
both time and iteration limits.
Profiling uses RTX 3090 GPUs, two CPU threads, one process per device, a three-second warm-up, and CUDA-synchronized
timing. Baselines have Trec = Topt; DiffTR also includes basis estimation, operator setup, sampling, and refinement. Training
and inference may use different GPUs of the same model. Loading, evaluation, and file I/O are excluded. Parameter counts
cover network weights, including both EDIP-Net networks, but exclude optimized inputs, EMA copies, optimizer states, and
buffers. Peak GPU memory is the largest allocated value per dataset; DiffTR uses the larger training or standalone-inference
peak after releasing the optimizer. CUDA context memory, unused allocator reservations, and CPU SVD memory are excluded.
