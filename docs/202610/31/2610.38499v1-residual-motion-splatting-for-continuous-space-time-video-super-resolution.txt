ABSTRACT
Continuous space-time video super-resolution produces a high-resolution, high-
frame-rate video from a low-resolution, low-frame-rate one at arbitrary spatial
scales and temporal instants. Classical spatial multi-frame super-resolution has
shown that detail beyond a single frame’s sampling limit can be recovered by
aligning complementary observations with sub-pixel precision and accumulating
them on a finer grid. Existing learning-based methods largely depart from this
principle: some align and fuse frames only at low resolution before regressing
a continuous high-resolution representation, while others interpolate each frame
to the output resolution before splatting. In both cases, the per-frame samples
are resampled before fusion, so their sub-pixel positions are lost before frames
are combined. Restoring this principle in a learned continuous model raises two
challenges: estimating sub-pixel motion at unobserved times, and handling holes
and occlusions when splatting a coarse grid onto a finer one. We address the
first by predicting per-pixel continuous trajectories that refine linear optical-flow
paths with smooth residual motion, using features integrated over the full video
sequence. We address the second by directly splatting consecutive low-resolution
features in a learned latent space onto a high-resolution grid at their predicted
sub-pixel positions with predicted visibility weights, before a decoder resolves
remaining ambiguities. This makes temporal interpolation and multi-frame super-
resolution a single operation. Our method improves both reconstruction fidelity
and perceptual quality on multiple standard benchmarks over the state of the art,
while running up to an order of magnitude faster.

INTRODUCTION
Spatial frequencies above a single frame’s Nyquist limit become recoverable when several obser-
vations of the same scene fall at distinct locations between integer pixel positions and accumulate
on a finer grid. Classical multi-frame super-resolution (Tsai & Huang, 1984; Irani & Peleg, 1991;
Farsiu et al., 2004) is built on this mechanism, and video supplies motion that places every frame
at a different spatial phase. Unlike conventional methods (Haris et al., 2020; Xiang et al., 2020; Xu
et al., 2021), which fix the spatial and temporal upscaling rates s and r once and for all at training
time, continuous space-time video super-resolution (C-STVSR) algorithms learn a single model that

can be used at inference time for any values of s and r, including ones not seen during training.
Recent C-STVSR models depart from the classical mechanism of splatting low-resolution pixels
directly to their high-resolution sub-pixel positions. Implicit neural representation (INR) methods
predict high-resolution representations of each frame before splatting them to the interpolated time
steps (Chen et al., 2022; 2023b; Kim et al., 2025); recent sinusoidal representation based methods
predict over spatio-temporal voxels a parametric representation of a high-resolution video with no
explicit motion model (Becker et al., 2026); Gaussian splatting based methods rasterise primitives
whose position, spread and colour are regressed from features fused under linear motion (Shi et al.,
2026). None of them carries the original low-resolution samples to their own sub-pixel positions
under an explicit motion model. We reinstate this mechanism, which SPMC (Tao et al., 2017) intro-
duced for fixed-scale video super-resolution at observed frames, and extend it to unobserved instants:
a single splat maps the encoder features onto their sub-pixel coordinates on the high-resolution grid,
along continuous trajectories predicted per location.
Our method works in four stages: (1) a video encoder produces latent features that capture temporal
context over multiple frames; (2) given off-the-shelf forward and backward optical flow estimates,
cubic splines with learned parameters are used to predict sub-pixel residual motions at every low-
resolution location in temporally interpolated frames; (3) visibility estimates are also predicted at
each location to account for effects such as occlusion and disocclusion; (4) the learned features
are splatted onto the high-resolution interpolated frames, and mapped back onto pixel values by a
learned decoder.
In this formulation, restoration fidelity depends on trajectory and visibility accuracy. While classi-
cal spatial super-resolution pipelines estimate motion between discrete observed frames, continuous
temporal models must predict motion at unobserved intermediate times. To model complex mo-
tion, we refine coarse linear flow estimates by predicting residual splines. Leveraging feature maps
over a broad temporal context enables the network to adjust trajectories according to scene content.
Unlike closed-form quadratic approaches (Xu et al., 2019; Liu et al., 2020) that impose a uniform
constant-acceleration assumption everywhere, our model can learn varying acceleration dynamics.
Our contributions are:
• We bring back the classical multi-frame super-resolution principle in a learned continuous model.
Low-resolution features are splatted directly onto the high-resolution grid at their own sub-pixel po-
sitions, at any instant between input frames, and weighted by a visibility weight predicted together
with their trajectory. Nothing is upsampled or warped at high resolution before fusion, so multi-
frame super-resolution and temporal interpolation become a single operation. Existing C-STVSR
methods instead fuse either before upsampling or after interpolating each frame to the output reso-
lution.
• Accumulating observations this way requires sub-pixel motion at instants that were never sampled.
At each low-resolution coordinate, we predict a continuous cubic B-spline residual on pretrained
optical flow, from features propagated across the input sequence. An inference-time experiment
(Appendix A) shows that this trajectory benefits from context beyond the two frames around the
queried instant: with four frames it recovers most of the full-sequence gain and exceeds a closed-
form quadratic model given the same frames.
• On GoPro and Adobe240, our method improves both fidelity and perceptual quality over the state
of the art. It generalises to unseen space-time scales, is the best C-STVSR method on arbitrary-
scale video super-resolution and in video frame interpolation, and is the most robust to unseen input
noise. Because no stage runs at high resolution before the splat, it is 6–11× faster than the C-STVSR
methods with public weights.

RELATED WORK
Classical Multi-Frame Super-Resolution. Super-resolution from several shifted observations pre-
dates learned methods. Frequency-domain registration and reconstruction (Tsai & Huang, 1984),
iterative back-projection (Irani & Peleg, 1991) and robust regularised estimators (Farsiu et al., 2004)
all rest on the same premise: the observations sample the scene at distinct sub-pixel offsets, and
accumulating them on a finer grid recovers aliased frequencies. Burst photography applies the same
principle with kernel regression on a fine grid (Wronski et al., 2019), and early learned VSR kept
it explicit: the SPMC layer (Tao et al., 2017) maps low-resolution pixels onto the high-resolution
grid at their flow-displaced sub-pixel positions, performing motion compensation and upsampling in

In this work, we combine the strengths of both paradigms: we leverage implicit temporal feature ex-

robust non-linear trajectories for explicit sub-pixel splatting at unobserved instants.
Fixed-Scale Space-Time Video Super-Resolution.
Video Super-Resolution (VSR) and Video
Frame Interpolation (VFI) traditionally address spatial and temporal upsampling as independent
tasks. VSR frameworks aggregate information across frames with recurrent or transformer back-
bones (Wang et al., 2019; Chan et al., 2021a; 2022a; Liang et al., 2022; Shi et al., 2022; Tran
et al., 2025), aligning them with optical flow, deformable attention (Chan et al., 2021b; 2022a) or
cross-attention (Xu et al., 2024). VFI methods synthesise intermediate frames along the temporal
axis using flow-based backward warping (Jiang et al., 2018; Bao et al., 2019; Sim et al., 2021) or
kernel-based motion compensation (Niklaus et al., 2017). Unified fixed-scale Space-Time Video
Super-Resolution (STVSR) models (Haris et al., 2020; Xiang et al., 2020; Xu et al., 2021) fuse the
two into single-stage architectures, avoiding the error propagation and flicker of cascaded pipelines.
All of them are bound to pre-defined upsampling factors, through fixed pixel-shuffle heads (Shi
et al., 2016) or rigid temporal modulation blocks: the output scale and frame rate are decided when
the model is trained.
Arbitrary-Scale Spatial Super-Resolution. Continuous spatial scaling was pioneered through im-
plicit neural representations (Chen et al., 2021; Lee & Jin, 2022), which parameterise images as
continuous functions over coordinate grids using MLPs or local Fourier bases (Pak et al., 2023).
Continuous 2D Gaussian Splatting (Peng et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2025) has since emerged as an
efficient alternative, rendering arbitrary spatial grids by forward rasterisation of fitted primitives
rather than by point-wise implicit coordinate queries. Extending continuous evaluation to video,
Arbitrary-Scale Video Super-Resolution (AVSR) frameworks (Li et al., 2024; Shang et al., 2024)
incorporate temporal guidance features, but address spatial scaling only and cannot synthesise inter-
mediate frames.
Continuous Space-Time Video Super-Resolution (C-STVSR). VideoINR (Chen et al., 2022)
scales both axes with decoupled spatial and temporal implicit functions, but predicts a backward
flow at unobserved times: that field cannot be measured, and at occlusion boundaries it is uncon-
strained by the observations. MoTIF (Chen et al., 2023b) replaces it with forward trajectories and
softmax splatting (Niklaus & Liu, 2020), regressing the displacement at time t from a SIREN; BF-
STVSR (Kim et al., 2025) keeps that pipeline and gives the time axis a B-spline basis. Both learn
the trajectory outright, unlike the closed-form fits of the VFI lineage, which are cheap and stable
on smooth motion but fixed in order: linear (Jiang et al., 2018), quadratic with an acceleration term

recovered from a third flow (Xu et al., 2019), and least-squares-rectified quadratic (Liu et al., 2020).
Both also evaluate the trajectory at the high-resolution output coordinates, so the splat carries NHR
feature tokens, and BF-STVSR estimates it from two successive frames. A pair fixes a displacement
and says nothing about how it changes: acceleration is not observable, so whatever curvature the
spline expresses comes from the learned prior rather than from the input. Other approaches have
been explored such as using 3D Fourier Fields in V3 (Becker et al., 2026) to map space-time signals
globally to bypass explicit motion, at the cost of detail under large displacement which can exhibit
chunk-boundary artefacts from the voxel partitioning their memory footprint requires, or Gaussian
splatting approaches such as GS-STVSR (Shi et al., 2026) which regresses Gaussian primitives from
features already fused under linear motion rather than transporting the observations. In our work,
we instead predict the trajectory at each of the NLR low-resolution coordinates, from features prop-
agated across the whole sequence, and as a residual on the flow estimate rather than as the full
displacement.

METHOD
Given a low-resolution image sequence y ∈RT ×H×W ×3 with T RGB frames of size H ×W, a spa-
tial upscale factor s and a temporal upscale factor r, C-STVSR aims to reconstruct high-resolution
frames at continuous positions in space and time.
Our method solves this problem in four stages. We first estimate bidirectional optical flow across
the input RGB sequence with an off-the-shelf estimator (Teed & Deng, 2020); motion in both tem-
poral directions is needed to capture asymmetric dynamics and to resolve regions occluded in either
the past or the future. These flows guide feature propagation in a video super-resolution back-
bone (Liang et al., 2022), which returns latent features Y ∈RT ×H×W ×C: every frame is encoded
at its original spatial resolution but carries context from the whole sequence (Section 3.1). For each
feature map Yt, a network predicts (from its neighbouring feature maps Yt+1 and Yt−1), at every
low-resolution position and in both temporal directions, two continuous functions of time: a B-
spline correction to the flow-induced linear motion (Section 3.2), and a visibility weight modelling
whether the content is still visible at that instant or has been occluded. Both are predicted once
per input frame, independently of the output frame rate, and are then evaluated at the intermediate
times t + τ and t −1 + τ; to produce r frames per interval we take τ ∈{ j

places the encoded features onto a fixed 4H × 4W grid at the positions their corrected trajectories
reach at time τ, weighted by the predicted visibility (Section 3.3), so that temporal interpolation and
multi-frame super-resolution happen in a single operation. A decoder finally maps the fused features
to pixels, and for s̸ = 4 the RGB frame is bicubically interpolated up or down to reach the factor
s (Section 3.4). Two properties characterise this design. First, every quantity is estimated in the
low-resolution source domain, so the trajectory and visibility networks run at the input resolution.
Second, the output is continuous in both dimensions: time enters through the spline evaluation at any
τ in [0, 1] and space through the final resampling, so one trained model serves arbitrary space-time
upsampling.
3.1
VIDEO-AWARE FEATURE EXTRACTION
Estimating interframe trajectories from only two adjacent temporal frames restricts motion mod-
elling to constant velocity. Without access to broader temporal context, the network cannot observe
higher-order motion dynamics such as acceleration or curvature; as a result, any non-linear trajec-
tory it predicts relies on learned inductive biases rather than observed evidence. While parameter-
izations such as the B-spline motion model in BF-STVSR (Kim et al., 2025) provide an effective
continuous trajectory formulation, estimating its parameters from adjacent two-frame windows re-
mains a key bottleneck. We retain the continuous B-spline parameterization but estimate it from
features that aggregate long-range temporal context and use it to correct flow-based linear motion
estimates. Enlarging the temporal context for the trajectory estimator improves reconstruction qual-
ity, indicating that the additional frames provide usable motion evidence (Appendix A). We adopt
RVRT (Liang et al., 2022), a recurrent video super-resolution backbone, to propagate features bidi-
rectionally across the entire sequence using optical flow maps estimated with RAFT (Teed & Deng,
2020). We replace RVRT’s original fixed-scale upsampling block with our proposed residual motion
multi-frame splatting.

EXPERIMENTS
4.1
EXPERIMENTAL SETUP
Implementation. We follow Becker et al. (2026) and use their hyper-parameter choices for the
RVRT (Liang et al., 2022) video encoder, with the embedding dimension set to 90 and 12 attention
heads, and train it from scratch end-to-end. The encoded features have 64 channels. For the tra-
jectory estimator gθ we use a three-layer SIREN (Sitzmann et al., 2020) with layer dimensions set
to 64,64, 256. Following the choice of Kim et al. (2025), we choose K = 64 for the size of the
B-spline basis. We use a Residual U-Net (Zhang et al., 2018c) to parameterise the decoder network
Dϕ.
Training Details. We train the model end-to-end and optimise the model parameters with AdamW
(β1 = 0.9, β2 = 0.99) and use a cosine annealing scheduler over 500k iterations with an initial
learning rate of 10−4. A training sample is an input sequence of 14 frames at 64 × 64 with a fixed
temporal scale factor of r = 8 and spatial scale factor of s = 4. We choose a batch size of 3 and
train on a single RTX PRO 6000 Blackwell GPU. The pretrained RAFT flow estimator is kept frozen
throughout the first 90% of the iterations and then fine-tuned with a ten-times-smaller learning rate.
This first lets the trajectory model train stably and then allows the optical-flow model to specialize
for the task in an end-to-end learning setting.
Datasets. We train our model using the Adobe240 (Su et al., 2017) dataset, a set of 133 RGB videos
captured at 240 frames per second. Following the split from Chen et al. (2022) that is used in the
works that followed (Chen et al., 2023b; Kim et al., 2025; Shi et al., 2026; Becker et al., 2026), we
have 100 videos for training, 16 for validation and 17 for testing. The dataset contains various scenes
and camera motion, and is the standard training set for continuous space-time video super-resolution.
Low-frame-rate inputs are synthesised by keeping one frame in eight such so that consecutive input
frames contain 30 fps motion. We also use Vid4 (Liu & Sun, 2011) and GoPro (Nah et al., 2017)
datasets as test sets for the spatio-temporal task and the REDS (Nah et al., 2019) dataset for the
spatial-only task.
4.2
C-STVSR PERFORMANCE
We first evaluate in the training setting (×4 space, ×8 time), then on unseen scales. Spatial SR is bet-
ter posed than temporal SR: motion provides sub-pixel-shifted views of the same content, whereas
intermediate instants are never observed. A model can only predict a plausible trajectory, and pixel
metrics penalise a sharp but offset frame more than a blurred one. We therefore report PSNR com-
puted over luminance (PSNR-Y) alongside LPIPS (Zhang et al., 2018a) (SSIM over luminance is
reported in Appendix E). FPS is measured on full GoPro frames (Appendix E.1).
Our method is best on GoPro and Adobe240 datasets on both subsets and metrics (Table 1), im-
proving on V3 by up to 0.6 dB and on V3-Large by up to 0.5 dB with 20% fewer parameters. The
gain is largest in LPIPS: 12–17% lower than V3, whereas V3’s ≈2 dB gain over BF-STVSR brought
only 3–10%. Replacing the trajectory at inference changes PSNR by up to 1.5 dB but LPIPS by un-
der 0.006 (Appendix A): the trajectory controls where content lands, while our reconstruction stage
(sub-pixel splatting and the HR decoder) controls how sharply it is rendered.
Trajectory errors therefore cost PSNR, not sharpness; in one clip our sharper crops trail V3 in PSNR
until a 4–6 pixel shift is compensated (Appendix F). V3 shares our RVRT encoder and RAFT fine-
tuning, and trains with far more compute (2.5M iterations, batch 16, 16 GPUs vs. 500k, batch 3, 1

CONCLUSION
Discussion. We showed that the principle of classical multi-frame super-resolution, accumulating
several frames at their sub-pixel positions on a finer grid, carries over to learned continuous
space-time video super-resolution. What we splat, however, are not raw observations but features
that a video encoder has propagated across the whole sequence, so each splatted sample already
carries temporal context beyond its own frame before a decoder maps the fused features to pixels.
This combines the two paradigms that learned video super-resolution had separated: implicit
feature propagation supplies temporal context, while explicit sub-pixel accumulation keeps each
sample at its own position until frames are fused. As a result, a single splat both interpolates in
time and gathers detail from neighbouring frames, improving fidelity and perceptual quality at a
fraction of the computational cost. Natural next steps are a visibility head decoupled from the
motion basis, and more samples per target pixel, by splatting more frames or several trajectories
9


Preprint
per pixel (Hu et al., 2022), to go beyond our fixed ×4 splat, which is limited by the two frames
it combines (Baker & Kanade, 2002; Wronski et al., 2019). Edge-aligned splat kernels (Wronski
et al., 2019) could also fill holes without blurring sub-pixel detail.
Limitations. (1) Visibility is read from the trajectory’s B-spline basis, following BF-STVSR (Kim
et al., 2025), so it is as smooth in time as the motion and tied to the point’s own trajectory rather
than to its occluder. This limits its gain (Appendix B) and leaves errors at the boundaries of moving
objects. (2) Under complex motion, trajectories can place content a few pixels off (Appendix F),
which costs PSNR even when the frame is sharp. (3) Like all evaluated methods, ours does not
recover detail from compressed inputs (Appendix D.3).

IMPORTANCE OF TEMPORAL INFORMATION FOR MOTION ESTIMATION
We examine whether the trajectory estimator uses temporal context beyond the two frames brack-
eting the interpolated instant. To test this, we vary only the temporal context seen by the trajectory
estimator gθ at inference. For each keyframe pair, we run a second encoder pass restricted to T
keyframes centred on the pair, and feed these features to gθ; the splines it predicts then drive the
splatting step. All other features, including the splatted ones, keep the full input window and are
therefore identical across all cases. To isolate the trajectory, we use the variant of our model without
visibility estimation (uniform splatting weights, Appendix B), trained under the same setting as the
full model. We compare against two closed-form references built on the same optical flow: Linear
Motion, which scales the flow along straight lines, and Quadratic Motion, the constant-acceleration
model of QVI (Xu et al., 2019), which uses the same four frames as T=4.
Figure 5 reports the results. With only a two-frame context, our learned trajectory already improves
over linear motion on every clip. Performance then jumps once each keyframe of the pair sees a
neighbour on its far side: at T=4, our trajectory recovers 96% of the full-window gain over linear
motion, against 52% for QVI given the same frames, and outperforms it on every video. From T=6
on, results start to have diminishing returns. The trained estimator therefore relies on context beyond
two frames.
Across all trajectory models, including linear and quadratic motion, LPIPS stays between 0.1318
and 0.1373, while PSNR varies by up to 1.48 dB (Table 3): replacing the trajectory at inference
leaves perceptual quality essentially unchanged, and the differences in PSNR reflect where content
is placed rather than how it is rendered.

B
VISIBILITY MAPS
When features from two input frames are warped to the same intermediate time, the frames can
disagree about what is visible: an object may hide part of the background in one frame and reveal
it in the other. Without visibility weights, the overlapping features are simply averaged. Because
this averaging happens in feature space, the decoder can still resolve many of these conflicts, which
already makes the model fairly robust to such conflicts. Visibility weights make the choice explicit,
letting the model rely on whichever frame actually sees each region. Averaged over whole frames
the improvement is small, partly because occlusions cover only a small part of each image, but it
is consistent across all datasets (Table 4). We therefore also evaluate on occlusion-boundary areas
alone, selected where a forward–backward consistency check on RAFT flow fails. In these areas,
the gain is about twice as large; this is where the mechanism is expected to act.

D.3
C-STVSR UNDER DEGRADATIONS
Following Becker et al. (2026), we evaluate how C-STVSR methods trained without degradations,
apart from the space-time downscaling, handle noise and compression. Since the codec settings
of Becker et al. (2026) are not specified, we re-run the study for all methods under a common
protocol. Zero-mean Gaussian noise of standard deviation σ is added to the input frames normalised
to [−1, 1], which are then clipped and quantised to 8 bits. In the compressed setting, the same noisy
frames are encoded as an H.264 stream (libx264, CRF 23, medium preset, YUV 4:2:0) containing
only the low-frame-rate input. Only the input is degraded; outputs are compared with the clean
ground truth.
All methods are fairly robust to noise (Table 7), but not equally so. The three splatting-based meth-
ods, MoTIF, BF-STVSR and ours, lose at most 0.17 dB up to σ=0.01 and degrade by almost the
same amount at every noise level, whereas V3, which decodes from the features of the nearest input
frame without warping them, loses 0.5 dB at σ=0.01. Since V3 and our method share the same
RVRT encoder, this points to the reconstruction stage. Splatting forms each output feature as a
weighted average of observations from several frames, which share the scene content but carry inde-
pendent noise, so the average keeps the content and partly cancels the noise. As a result, our margin
over V3 grows from 0.11 dB on clean inputs to 0.62 dB at σ=0.025. At σ=0.05, all methods lose
1.3–1.6 dB.
Compression dominates the degradation (Table 8): all methods lose more than 2 dB and end up
on par. H.264 quantises the high-frequency transform coefficients most coarsely, so at this rate
much of the fine detail that multi-frame super-resolution relies on is removed from the input, and
blocking and ringing artefacts are added in its place. A model trained only on clean inputs has
no way to tell these artefacts apart from real content, so it reconstructs them rather than removing
them. Recovering detail from compressed video requires training on compressed inputs, as done
by dedicated compressed-VSR methods (Li et al., 2021; Chan et al., 2022b); none of the evaluated
methods, ours included, is trained this way. The codec also explains why the noise level has almost
no further effect: quantisation removes most of the added noise along with the detail, so every noise
level produces nearly the same encoded input.

ADDITIONAL METRICS
E.1
RUNTIME MEASUREMENT
FPS is measured during the GoPro evaluation itself, on full-resolution test clips (320×180 input,
1280×720 output) rather than crops, on a single RTX 6000 Ada GPU. Each clip is processed in
temporal windows, and only the model’s forward pass is timed: frame loading, host-to-device trans-
fer, metric computation and output writing all fall outside the timer. A warm-up before the first clip
absorbs one-off costs such as compilation and autotuning. Context frames recomputed at window
boundaries are included in the model time (for V3 and ours). FPS is the total number of output
frames divided by the accumulated model time over the whole test set.
V3 reports a speed advantage over prior C-STVSR methods, measured on 14×80×80 crops. We
could not reproduce this advantage on full frames: its tiled encoder and per-patch decoder must then
cover the entire 320×180 input, and its throughput drops to 1.3 FPS.
E.2
PERCEPTUAL AND TEMPORAL COHERENCE METRICS
Following BF-STVSR (Kim et al., 2025), Table 9 reports four additional video metrics, com-
puted offline on the saved output frames with the same protocol. VFIPS (Hou et al., 2022) and
FloLPIPS (Danier et al., 2022) are perceptual metrics designed for frame interpolation: VFIPS is
computed on non-overlapping 12-frame segments, and FloLPIPS on all consecutive frame pairs af-
ter conversion to 8-bit YUV 4:2:0. tOF (Chu et al., 2020) measures temporal consistency as the
difference between the optical flows of the output and ground-truth videos. VMAF (Li et al., 2016)
is a video quality metric fitted to human opinion scores.
Our method is best on every metric except tOF on GoPro, where it is within 0.007 of V3 (0.377 vs.
0.370); on Adobe240 it is also best in tOF (0.395 vs. 0.401). Temporal coherence is therefore on par
with V3, even though V3 represents the video with space-time Fourier fields, which vary smoothly
in time by construction, whereas we synthesise each frame by splatting along predicted trajectories.
The perceptual metrics favour our method more clearly: FloLPIPS is 11–12% lower than V3 on both
datasets, consistent with the LPIPS gains of Table 1.
Video encoding for VMAF measurement. Following the protocol from Kim et al. (2025), we
encode both the reconstructed and the ground-truth sequences to H.264 with libx264 at 30 frames
per second in yuv420p, at ffmpeg’s default rate factor, and compute VMAF on the luma plane
of the decoded streams. The codec is therefore part of the measurement for this metric: the score
reflects the reconstruction as it would be delivered, not the network output in isolation.

ADDITIONAL RESULTS
IMG 0012 (Figure 7) contains difficult motion: the camera pans non-uniformly while a large bus
passes close to it. Our results look clearly sharper than those of V3, but their PSNR is lower at t= 1
2:
15.3–20.0 dB for ours against 17.8–21.3 dB for V3 over the three crops, whereas our LPIPS is about
40% lower (0.185–0.243 against 0.304–0.396). The reason is position, not appearance: our output
is shifted by 4–6 pixels from the ground truth, and PSNR strongly penalises a small shift of sharp
edges, while a blurred output is penalised less. To measure this, we search, for each crop and each
method, for the integer translation within ±12 pixels that maximizes PSNR against the ground truth,
and compute PSNR after applying it. Our PSNR then rises to 26.9–28.3 dB, while V3 only reaches
20.5–24.8 dB, since shifting cannot recover blurred detail. On IMG 0015 (Figure 3) and IMG 0004a
(Figure 4), the best shift is zero, and our method has the highest PSNR without any correction.
The same pattern appears at the dataset level (Table 1): on GoPro and Adobe240 at ×8, our PSNR-Y
gain over V3 is moderate (0.28–0.64 dB), while our LPIPS is 12–17% lower. This is consistent with
sharp outputs that PSNR penalises for small displacements but that a perceptual metric, which is
more tolerant to such shifts, rewards.
