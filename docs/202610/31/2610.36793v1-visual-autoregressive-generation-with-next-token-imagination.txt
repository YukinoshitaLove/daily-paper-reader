ABSTRACT
The space of possible visual content is enormous, and arguably unbounded. Any training set, how-
ever large, therefore covers only a tiny fraction of it. Yet generative models are typically trained to fit
only the images they are given. In visual autoregressive (AR) generation, for example, each token is
predicted from the preceding ground-truth (GT) tokens and supervised solely by the GT next token.
Confining learning to observed data in this way limits how much of the visual distribution a model
can capture. In this work, we hypothesize that letting a model imagine beyond its training data,
by exploring plausible visual realizations it has never observed, can ease this limitation. We use
visual AR generation as a proof of concept, since its fixed, discrete token space makes imagination
easier to steer and constrain. We propose Next Token Imagination (NTI), a method that allows
the model to explore beyond the GT next token, subject to a carefully designed criterion that keeps
this imagination bounded by the underlying visual distribution. Extensive experiments on ImageNet
show that NTI substantially and consistently improves the fidelity of generated images, approaching
state-of-the-art diffusion and AR models while using a smaller model and lower training cost. Code
and checkpoints are available at https://github.com/JackRuihang/NTI.

Introduction
Autoregressive (AR) models, trained to predict each token from its preceding context, form the basis of modern
language generation (Brown et al., 2020). Inspired by their success in text, AR modeling has been extended to image
generation through discrete visual tokenization (Esser et al., 2021; Sun et al., 2024). However, standard visual AR
models still lag behind leading diffusion and flow-matching models in generation quality (Peebles & Xie, 2023; Ma
et al., 2024; Yu et al., 2025b). We therefore revisit visual AR training to identify what constrains its generative
capability.
The standard visual AR pipeline consists of a visual tokenizer and a causal Transformer. The tokenizer encodes
each image into a sequence of discrete codebook indices and decodes the corresponding codebook embeddings to
reconstruct the image (van den Oord et al., 2017; Esser et al., 2021; Lee et al., 2022). The Transformer models this
sequence, typically in raster order, through next token prediction (Parmar et al., 2018; Esser et al., 2021; Sun et al.,
2024). Advances in visual AR have largely focused on tokenizer design (Yu et al., 2024a; Xiong et al., 2025), token
representation (Yu et al., 2024b; Bachmann et al., 2025), generation order (Yu et al., 2025a; Pang et al., 2025), and
model scaling (Sun et al., 2024; Han et al., 2025). In contrast, generator training still follows the standard paradigm.
During training, each position is supervised by the corresponding ground-truth (GT) next token, with the prediction
conditioned on the GT prefix (Bengio et al., 2015; Sun et al., 2024). At inference, however, the model samples a token
from its predictive distribution at each position, so non-GT tokens can accumulate through AR generation, causing the
generated sequence to depart from the GT sequence.
In this work, we revisit standard visual AR training from a distributional perspective. A non-GT next token does
not necessarily move the generation outside the space of plausible visual realizations. Instead, it can lead subsequent
generation toward another valid visual realization beyond the GT sequence. Through controlled experiments, we
examine this hypothesis, motivating the model to explore plausible non-GT next tokens during training.

First, standard visual AR training imposes a restricted next token space. Given a context, the GT next token repre-
sents only one observed choice within a much larger space of plausible continuations. As illustrated in Figure 1(a), the
generator predicts token 32 instead of the GT token 5, while the resulting sequence still produces a visually plausible
cat. This indicates that plausible non-GT next tokens exist beyond the GT target, motivating the model to explore a
broader next token space during training.
Second, a restricted next token space further leads to a restricted context space during training. Since each sam-
pled token becomes part of the prefix for later positions, sampling a non-GT token naturally creates an off-GT context.
Related work on exposure bias (Bengio et al., 2015; Wang & Sennrich, 2020) typically treats such contexts as conse-
quences of prediction errors and focuses on improving model robustness. We instead view off-GT contexts as natural
states induced by the model’s next token choices. The same example in Figure 1(a) further illustrates this process.
Once the model samples token 32 instead of the GT token 5, the next position is conditioned on an off-GT context.
Given this prefix, token 34 still produces a visually plausible cat, whereas token 73 leads to structural distortion. This
suggests that broadening the next token space alone is insufficient; in other words, training must distinguish plausible
non-GT predictions from implausible ones.
Motivated by these observations, we propose Next Token Imagination (NTI), a plug-and-play training paradigm that
breaks the constraint of GT-only next token supervision. Specifically, NTI uses Context-Regulated Imagination
(CRI) to assign probability to plausible non-GT tokens according to the current context. Ideally, such imagination
should be incorporated directly into the AR process, where each imagined next token becomes part of the context for
subsequent positions. However, performing this sequential rollout during training is extremely expensive. We therefore
introduce Snapshot-based Context Approximation (SCA) to efficiently construct model-generated contexts without
explicit sequential rollout. Together, these two mechanisms move visual AR training beyond the observed GT sequence
while keeping imagination regulated.
Experiments on ImageNet establish NTI as a strong training paradigm for visual AR generation. With a standard
raster-order AR backbone, NTI reduces FID from 3.02 to 1.67 and improves IS from 256.20 to 312.72, with consis-
tent gains across model scales. NTI further outperforms representative AR methods including reAR (He et al., 2026),
RAR (Yu et al., 2025a), LlamaGen (Sun et al., 2024), and BitDance (Ai et al., 2026), while remaining competitive
with substantially larger or more specialized generative models. NTI also transfers effectively to non-standard tok-
enizers such as GigaTok (Xiong et al., 2025) and AliTok (Wu et al., 2026), demonstrating that its gains are not tied
to a particular tokenizer design. Notably, these improvements are achieved without changing the inference pipeline.
Collectively, these results establish NTI as a general and effective training paradigm for substantially advancing visual
AR generation.
Our contributions are threefold. (1) We expose a fundamental limitation of visual AR training: GT-only next token su-
pervision unnecessarily restricts valid continuations beyond the observed GT sequence. (2) We introduce NTI, a plug-
and-play training paradigm that enables context-regulated exploration beyond the GT sequence. (3) We demonstrate
that NTI consistently improves visual AR generation across model scales and tokenizer designs, without modifying
the tokenizer, generation order, or inference pipeline.

Preliminaries
Visual autoregressive generation follows a three-stage pipeline. (1) A visual tokenizer encodes images into latent se-
quences. (2) An AR generator models the distribution over these sequences and generates new sequences at inference.
(3) The generated sequences are decoded back into images by the tokenizer decoder.

contexts during training but continue to supervise each position with the GT token zt. Such supervision implicitly
regards off-GT contexts as states arising from accumulated prediction errors and trains the model to return toward the
GT sequence. This view is natural for conventional compression, where the objective is to recover a given source.
Generation is different: the plausible visual space is enormous, while the GT sequences in the training data cover only
a small fraction of it. Forcing every off-GT context back toward the GT sequence therefore suppresses the diversity
that a generative model should capture. Valid non-GT next token predictions should instead be encouraged, allowing
the model to recursively explore valid continuations beyond the observed GT sequences and thereby cover a broader
plausible visual space.
We quantitatively analyze the effects of deviations in next token predictions and their induced contexts on visual
generation in Section 3.1. Based on these results, we propose NTI, a plug-and-play training paradigm for visual AR
models. Specifically, CRI extends the supervision from the GT token to plausible non-GT ones. To improve training
efficiency, instead of autoregressively rolling out contexts, we introduce SCA to construct model-generated contexts
in a single forward pass. Together, they allow the model to explore beyond the visual space covered by the training
data.
3.1
Understanding Restricted Next Token Space
Given a GT context z<t and a generated context ez<t, we consider a context window Wt ⊆{1, . . . , t −1} of length
L = |Wt|. Let A = {i ∈Wt : ezi̸ = zi} denote the positions where the two contexts differ. We measure their deviation
using Ground Truth Token Ratio (GTR) and Ground Truth Token Similarity (GTS); specifically, GTR = 1 −
|A|/L and GTS = |A|−1 P
i∈A sim(cezi, czi), with GTS = 1 when A = ∅, where sim(·, ·) denotes cosine similarity.
We further use LPIPS (Zhang et al., 2018) and a class-conditional DINOv2 similarity score (Oquab et al., 2024) to
evaluate perceptual deviation and class-conditional semantic similarity in the decoded images. The following two
controlled experiments show that NTI is feasible but requires regulation.
Feasibility of NTI. To examine whether NTI can move beyond the GT sequence while remaining within a plausible
visual space, we study how contexts induced by non-GT next token predictions affect subsequent generation. We
construct perturbed contexts from GT contexts under two settings: varying GTR at fixed GTS, and varying GTS while
fixing the replacement positions and GTR. Starting from each perturbed context, we autoregressively generate the
remaining tokens and measure the perceptual deviation of the decoded images using LPIPS.
Figure 3 shows that context deviations influence subsequent generation: lower GTR and lower GTS generally lead
to higher LPIPS, indicating larger perceptual deviations in the decoded images. However, off-GT contexts do not
necessarily result in failed generations. As shown in the cat examples, the facial features become progressively blurred
as the context deviates further from the GT context, while in the dog examples, moderate deviations still preserve
coherent leg structures before severe distortions emerge. These results demonstrate that contexts induced by non-GT
next token predictions can remain within a plausible visual space, supporting NTI beyond the GT sequence.

Regulation of NTI. Having established that NTI can move beyond the GT sequence, we further investigate how NTI
should be regulated. Specifically, we examine (1) whether non-GT tokens can serve as plausible supervision targets
under off-GT contexts, and (2) whether suitable targets vary with the current context. We conduct two comparisons
under generated contexts and evaluate the resulting images using DINO score.
For the first aspect, we compare GT and non-GT tokens under the same generated context. Across different GTR
values, non-GT tokens achieve comparable or even higher mean DINO scores than GT tokens. Figure 4(a) shows this
trend, while the snow leopard examples demonstrate that both choices can produce visually plausible images. These
results demonstrate that the GT token is not the only plausible supervision target under an off-GT context, supporting
NTI beyond the GT token.
Based on this observation, a straightforward approach is to include non-GT tokens according to codebook similar-
ity (Hu et al., 2025; Tang et al., 2025). However, such targets remain fixed across different contexts. We therefore
compare fixed selection, which uses the same non-GT token across contexts, with context-adaptive selection. As
shown in Figure 4(b), the two perform similarly at high GTR, while context-adaptive selection performs better as
GTR decreases. When GTR decreases further, both non-GT choices degrade, indicating that the imagination range
should contract toward the GT token. These results demonstrate that NTI must be regulated by the current context.
3.2
Training with Next Token Imagination
We realize NTI with a simple and efficient training procedure. SCA constructs model-generated contexts without AR
rollout. Using predictions under the GT and model-generated contexts, CRI directly determines which non-GT tokens
should be included in supervision and how much probability they should receive.
Snapshot-based Context Approximation. Based on the above findings, the ideal NTI training procedure would
construct each context and its corresponding supervision through step-by-step AR rollout. However, performing AR
rollout for every sequence is computationally expensive. Previous work uses scheduled sampling (Bengio et al., 2015;
Zhan et al., 2022) to construct model-generated contexts. Such online construction, however, requires an additional
prediction pass during training. We therefore introduce SCA, which uses a periodically refreshed frozen snapshot p¯θ
of the current generator pθ to approximate model-generated contexts for NTI while preserving a single forward pass
during training.

Experiments
4.1
Experimental Setup
Dataset and evaluation. We conduct class-conditional generation experiments on ImageNet-1K (Deng et al., 2009)
at a resolution of 256 × 256. Following the ADM evaluation protocol (Dhariwal & Nichol, 2021), we generate 50,000
images with classifier-free guidance (Ho & Salimans, 2022) and report Fr´echet Inception Distance (FID) (Heusel et al.,
2017) and Inception Score (IS) (Salimans et al., 2016).
Model configuration. We adopt a pretrained MaskGIT-VQGAN tokenizer (Chang et al., 2022) with 1,024 codebook
entries and a spatial downsampling factor of 16, encoding each image into 256 discrete tokens. NTI-S/B/L consist of
20/24/24 causal Transformer layers with hidden dimensions of 768/768/1024, respectively, and all variants use 16
attention heads.
Training. We train all models for 400 epochs with a global batch size of 2,048, using AdamW (Loshchilov & Hutter,
2019). The learning rate is linearly warmed up to 4 × 10−4 during the first 100 epochs and then decayed to 10−5 with
a cosine schedule over the remaining 300 epochs. We apply gradient clipping with a maximum norm of 1.0 and drop
class labels with probability 0.1 for classifier-free guidance (Ho & Salimans, 2022).
NTI implementation. We set the affinity temperature to τ = 0.05. The maximum replacement probability first
increases to 0.3 and then decreases to zero by epoch 320, after which the remaining 80 epochs use only GT sequences.
SCA contexts are regenerated every 16 epochs. Further experimental details are provided in Appendix A.

Class-conditional generation. Table 1 shows that NTI achieves strong performance with a standard raster-order AR
backbone. On the same AR-L backbone, NTI-L reduces FID from 3.02 to 1.67 and improves IS from 256.20 to
312.72. Figure 5 further shows consistent gains across NTI-S/B/L as model size increases. NTI-L also outperforms
reAR-L, RAR-L, LlamaGen-L, and BitDance-B-1x in FID while retaining a standard discrete tokenizer and raster-
order generation. With only 461M parameters and 400 training epochs, it remains competitive with substantially larger
or more expensive state-of-the-art generative models, showing that NTI can markedly improve visual AR generation
without specialized architectures, tokenizers, or decoding strategies.
Generalization. We further evaluate NTI on GigaTok (Xiong et al., 2025) and AliTok (Wu et al., 2026), keeping
their original tokenizers and generators unchanged and only adding NTI training. NTI improves FID from 3.94 to
3.66 on GigaTok and from 1.61 to 1.51 on AliTok, demonstrating that NTI generalizes across different visual AR
configurations.

epoch training times of vanilla AR, NTI, and online two-pass construction are 11.62, 12.80, and 22.90 minutes,
respectively. The NTI estimate includes the cost of periodic context construction using a frozen snapshot, averaged
over the epochs between refreshes. Thus, NTI adds only about 10% training overhead, whereas online two-pass

Related Work
Visual autoregressive generation. Early visual AR models perform sequential prediction directly over image ele-
ments, conditioned on preceding context (Parmar et al., 2018). To reduce the cost of pixel-level modeling, tokenizers
compress images into discrete code sequences (van den Oord et al., 2017; Esser et al., 2021), enabling scalable causal
Transformers for image generation (Sun et al., 2024). Recent work has improved components of this pipeline. Tok-
enizer design has progressed toward more compact and scalable representations (Yu et al., 2024b; Xiong et al., 2025),
flexible one-dimensional sequences and causality-aligned dependencies (Bachmann et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2026), and
larger discrete vocabularies with sub-token prediction (Luo et al., 2024). Other studies relax the fixed raster order
through randomized sequence modeling (Yu et al., 2025a; Pang et al., 2025), while alternative formulations replace
discrete next token prediction with continuous representations (Li et al., 2024), next-scale prediction (Tian et al.,
2024), or more flexible prediction units (Ren et al., 2025). In contrast, NTI keeps the tokenizer, generation order, and
prediction unit unchanged, and instead revisits the supervision of standard next token prediction.
Exposure bias. Exposure bias has long been studied in autoregressive sequence modeling, where scheduled sampling
exposes the model to its own predictions during training while retaining the GT token as supervision (Bengio et al.,
2015). Similar strategies have been adopted in visual AR models: RQ-Transformer introduces stochastic sampling
during training (Lee et al., 2022), IQ-VAE mixes GT and predicted tokens through Gumbel-softmax (Zhan et al.,
2022), and reAR introduces noisy contexts while continuing to predict the GT next token (He et al., 2026). More
recently, autoregressive video generation methods have reduced the training–inference gap through inference-like
8

contexts or self-generated rollouts (Zhou et al., 2025; Huang et al., 2025a). These approaches reduce the training–
inference mismatch through different context-construction and supervision mechanisms. NTI instead focuses on the
supervision under off-GT contexts: an off-GT context can still represent a plausible visual state, and its next token
need not be restricted to the GT token. NTI therefore regulates plausible non-GT supervision according to the current
context, enabling the model to explore valid continuations beyond the observed GT sequence.
Supervision beyond the GT token. Beyond-GT supervision has been explored in discrete autoregressive modeling.
OCD derives context-dependent valid next token targets from optimal completions of generated prefixes using dy-
namic programming (Sabour et al., 2019), while DIST2Loss replaces one-hot targets with distance-aware distributions
defined by token metrics (Chung et al., 2026). In visual AR generation, IAR constructs cluster-oriented supervision
from codebook similarity (Hu et al., 2025), and DCPE extracts discriminative priors from the visual codebook (Tang
et al., 2025). However, DIST2Loss, IAR, and DCPE determine non-GT supervision from token relationships without
adapting it to the current AR context. NTI instead treats plausible non-GT next tokens as context-dependent valid
continuations and regulates their supervision according to the current context. Additional discussion is provided in
Appendix D.
6
Conclusion
In this paper, we identify a key limitation of visual AR training: standard supervision treats the GT token as the
sole target, even though plausible non-GT next tokens can lead to valid continuations. To address this, we propose
Next Token Imagination (NTI), which reframes next token prediction from GT-only supervision to other plausible
continuations. With CRI and SCA, NTI substantially improves standard visual AR models, and generalizes across
different tokenizer and generator designs. We hope this work encourages future visual AR models to move beyond the
visual space covered by the training data and explore a broader space of plausible visual content.

Additional Experimental Details
Code and checkpoints.
Our training and evaluation code, together with the NTI-S/B/L inference checkpoints, is
publicly available at https://github.com/JackRuihang/NTI. The repository includes model configurations and
instructions for sampling and computing FID and IS.
A.1
Dataset, Backbone, and Optimization
Dataset and evaluation.
We evaluate class-conditional image generation on ImageNet-1K at 256 × 256 resolu-
tion (Deng et al., 2009). For each evaluation, we generate 50,000 images and compute FID and IS following the
ADM evaluation protocol (Dhariwal & Nichol, 2021; Heusel et al., 2017; Salimans et al., 2016). We use the same
ImageNet-256 reference archive, VIRTUAL imagenet256 labeled.npz, for all evaluations.
Tokenizer and generator.
For the standard raster-order experiments, we use a frozen MaskGIT-VQGAN tok-
enizer (Chang et al., 2022) with a downsampling factor of 16, producing a 16×16 token grid and a sequence length of
256. The tokenizer has a codebook of 1,024 entries with an embedding dimension of 256. NTI-S/B/L use 20/24/24
causal Transformer layers with hidden dimensions of 768/768/1024, respectively. All variants use 16 attention heads,
learned positional embeddings, class-conditioned adaptive layer normalization, query–key normalization, an MLP
expansion ratio of 4, and dropout of 0.1 in the attention and feed-forward layers.
Optimization.
We train the standard raster-order models for 250,000 optimization steps (approximately 400 epochs)
with a global batch size of 2,048. We use AdamW (Loshchilov & Hutter, 2019) with (β1, β2) = (0.9, 0.96) and weight
decay 0.03. The learning rate is linearly warmed up to 4 × 10−4 during the first 62,500 steps and then decayed to
10−5 with a cosine schedule. Training uses bfloat16 mixed precision and gradient clipping with a maximum norm of
1.0. Class labels are dropped with probability 0.1 for classifier-free guidance (Ho & Salimans, 2022).

C
Qualitative Evidence of Expanded Visual Space
The controlled analyses in Appendix B characterize deviations from the GT sequence through pixel, perceptual, fea-
ture, and likelihood spaces. We further examine the generated images directly to investigate whether the broader
supervision introduced by NTI translates into a broader range of plausible visual realizations at inference.
Figure 7 compares samples generated by AR-L and NTI-L under the same class conditions. The difference is partic-
ularly visible in object morphology and composition. For volcanoes, AR-L predominantly generates distant conical
mountains with similar eruption patterns, whereas NTI-L produces substantially more varied realizations, including
crater lakes, explosive ash plumes, lava eruptions, snow-covered volcanoes, and close-up volcanic structures. For
guitar picks, AR-L mainly generates isolated picks with similar centered compositions, while NTI-L exhibits greater
variation in appearance, arrangement, viewpoint, and interaction with surrounding objects.
The same effect appears in object pose. AR-L hippopotamuses are frequently generated in similar partially submerged
configurations, whereas NTI-L produces markedly different poses and viewpoints, including frontal close-ups, open-
mouth poses, and standing or on-land configurations. Wallabies generated by NTI-L likewise span a broader range of
viewpoints and poses, including close-up, frontal, side-view, standing, walking, and crouching configurations.
Together with the controlled experiments in Appendix B, these qualitative results show that NTI expands the range of
plausible visual realizations produced by a standard visual AR model. The broader supervision encountered during
NTI training therefore translates into a broader visual space at inference.
D
Additional Discussion on Related Work
This section expands the comparisons in Section 5. We discuss approaches that improve visual AR generation through
generation order, tokenization, alternative prediction formulations, and additional training supervision. Across these
directions, we focus on how they differ from NTI, which extends next token supervision beyond the GT token and
regulates non-GT supervision according to the current context.
D.1
Randomized Generation Order
RandAR. RandAR (Pang et al., 2025) introduces position-instruction tokens to specify the spatial locations of tokens
to be generated. This allows a decoder-only Transformer to generate image tokens in randomized orders rather than a
fixed raster order. By providing multiple position instructions, RandAR also supports parallel decoding. Its approach
changes both the sequence organization and the generation procedure.
RAR. RAR (Yu et al., 2025a) incorporates target-position information into autoregressive prediction and trains with
randomized token orders. The degree of randomization decreases during training, eventually recovering raster-order
modeling. This exposes the generator to different spatial contexts during training while retaining raster-order autore-
gressive generation at inference.
ARPG. ARPG (Li et al., 2026) separates positional queries from content representations to support randomized par-
allel generation. Its shared KV cache allows previously computed content representations to be reused when querying
multiple positions. ARPG therefore improves both generation-order flexibility and parallel decoding efficiency.
Summary. Randomized-order methods vary which spatial tokens precede each prediction in the autoregressive se-
quence, exposing the generator to contexts beyond a fixed raster order. NTI instead retains raster-order generation
and modifies the supervision under a given context by allowing plausible non-GT next tokens according to the current
context.
D.2

TiTok. TiTok (Yu et al., 2024b) represents images with compact one-dimensional token sequences rather than a fixed
two-dimensional token grid. This substantially reduces the number of visual tokens required for reconstruction and
generation.

GigaTok. GigaTok (Xiong et al., 2025) studies the scaling of visual tokenizers for autoregressive generation. It intro-

length. Its coarse-to-fine representation allows different token budgets to capture different levels of visual detail,
supporting reconstruction and generation at varying sequence lengths.
AliTok. AliTok (Wu et al., 2026) addresses the dependency mismatch between visual tokenization and autoregressive
generation. It uses a bidirectional encoder constrained by a causal decoder to learn representations that are more com-
patible with forward sequence modeling. A two-stage tokenizer training procedure further improves reconstruction
while preserving this dependency structure.

CaTok. CaTok (Chen et al., 2026) develops a one-dimensional causal image tokenizer whose token dependencies are
explicitly aligned with autoregressive generation. It uses a MeanFlow decoder to construct causal representations over
ordered token intervals.
SpectralAR. SpectralAR (Huang et al., 2025b) constructs ordered spectral tokens from low- to high-frequency image
components. This produces a causal coarse-to-fine visual sequence for autoregressive generation.
Hita. Hita (Zheng et al., 2025) introduces a holistic-to-local tokenization scheme, placing learned holistic tokens
before local patch tokens and using causal attention to align the representation with autoregressive generation.
Summary. These approaches improve token compactness, representation quality, or compatibility with causal gen-
eration. NTI instead modifies the supervision used to train the generator on a given representation. Table 2 shows
that NTI reduces FID from 3.94 to 3.66 on GigaTok and from 1.61 to 1.51 on AliTok without changing their tokeniz-
ers or generator architectures. These results show that tokenizer improvements and context-regulated supervision are
complementary.
D.3
Alternative Autoregressive Formulations
MAR. MAR (Li et al., 2024) extends autoregressive image generation to continuous visual tokens. It models per-
token conditional distributions with a diffusion loss and further generalizes the formulation to masked autoregressive
generation. Its prediction mechanism therefore differs from categorical next token prediction over a discrete codebook.
VAR. VAR (Tian et al., 2024) replaces next token prediction with next-scale prediction. Starting from a coarse repre-
sentation, it progressively generates finer token maps conditioned on previously generated scales, with tokens within
each scale predicted in parallel. This changes both the prediction unit and the autoregressive factorization of the image.
xAR. xAR (Ren et al., 2025) generalizes the prediction unit from individual tokens to flexible entities such as cells,
subsamples, scales, or entire images. It further replaces discrete token classification with continuous regression using
flow matching and introduces noisy-context training.
Other prediction formulations. Other baselines in Table 1 adopt different prediction spaces or generation mecha-
nisms. MaskBit (Weber et al., 2024) performs masked generation over binary token representations. SphereAR (Ke &
Xue, 2026) performs continuous-token autoregressive generation with hyperspherical latents. BAR (Tang et al., 2026)
learns an end-to-end transformation of the token basis for autoregressive generation. BitDance (Ai et al., 2026) uses a
binary diffusion head for token prediction and supports parallel next-patch generation.
Summary. These methods modify the prediction space, prediction unit, or autoregressive factorization. NTI instead
retains discrete raster-order next token prediction and constructs a context-regulated supervision distribution over the
existing vocabulary.
D.4
Training Regularization and Supervision
REPA. REPA (Yu et al., 2025b) improves diffusion Transformer training by aligning intermediate representations with
features from a pretrained visual encoder. This provides an additional representation-learning signal during denoising,
acting on intermediate features rather than directly modifying the output target distribution.
reAR. reAR (He et al., 2026) improves consistency between the autoregressive generator and its tokenizer. It in-
troduces token-wise regularization based on tokenizer representations and trains the generator under noisy contexts,
while retaining GT next token supervision.
RQ-Transformer. RQ-Transformer (Lee et al., 2022) introduces soft labeling and stochastic sampling for visual AR
training. Its soft targets are constructed from distances between codebook embeddings, while stochastic sampling
exposes the generator to different discrete code sequences.
IQ-VAE. IQ-VAE (Zhan et al., 2022) uses reliability-based Gumbel sampling to mix predicted tokens with GT tokens
during training, reducing the discrepancy between training and autoregressive inference.
MAGI. MAGI (Zhou et al., 2025) studies exposure bias in masked autoregressive video generation and introduces
training strategies that expose the model to inference-like conditions.
Token-aware supervision. IAR (Hu et al., 2025) and DCPE (Tang et al., 2025) exploit codebook structure and
discriminative priors to construct supervision beyond one-hot GT targets. DIST2Loss (Chung et al., 2026) similarly
introduces distance-aware supervision based on token metrics. These methods use relationships among tokens to shape
the supervision distribution.

Generated-context supervision. OCD (Sabour et al., 2019) derives valid next token targets from optimal continua-
tions of generated prefixes under an edit-distance objective. Self Forcing (Huang et al., 2025a) trains autoregressive
video models using self-generated rollouts and sequence-level distribution matching. Both consider supervision be-
yond standard GT-context training, but operate under different sequence objectives and prediction spaces.
