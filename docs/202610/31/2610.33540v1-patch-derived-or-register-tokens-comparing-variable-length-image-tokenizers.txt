Abstract. Image tokenizers convert pixels into compact latent sequences for efficient generative modeling. Most approaches arrange latents on a downsampled two-dimensional grid, tying sequence length to image resolution. We compare two variable-length strategies: computationally efficient patch-derived tokens, which inherit explicit spatial organiza-tion, and more demanding register tokens, which have no predefined spatial layout. Our reconstruction results reveal that patch-derived to-kens perform substantially better under extreme compression, whereas register tokens achieve slightly better reconstruction quality at interme-diate budgets. At high token budgets, both approaches achieve similar reconstruction quality. For downstream generative tasks, register tokens outperform patch-derived tokens. Adding representation alignment with external pretrained visual encoders substantially improves the generative performance of patch-derived tokens, allowing them to match or outper-form register tokens depending on the token budget. We further find that both approaches learn spatially localized representations. Patch-derived tokens largely retain their explicitly imposed spatial organization, while register tokens develop an implicit two-dimensional spatial structure de-spite having no predefined layout. Representation alignment reduces this spatial organizational constraint, enabling individual tokens to exert a stronger non-local influence.

## 1 Introduction 

Image tokenization plays a crucial role in reducing the computational costs of subsequent generative models [3, 20, 22]. An image tokenizer [16] encodes an image into a compact latent token representation. Most tokenizers preserve the spatial structure of the image by arranging the tokens on a downsampled grid, with each token corresponding to a fixed image region [6, 14]. Consequently, the number of tokens grows with the image resolution, resulting in prohibitively long sequences in the case of high-resolution image generation, video generation, and world modeling. Recent work such as TiTok [30] has challenged these grid-structured latent representations by encoding images into sequences whose length is not deter-mined by the number of input patches. This decoupling enables substantially shorter representations while maintaining high reconstruction fidelity and sup-porting competitive generative modeling. A common approach introduces K

learned tokens, also called register tokens, concatenates them with the P image-patch embeddings, and jointly processes the resulting P + K tokens in the en-coder. The updated K register tokens are retained as the image representation and passed to the decoder, while the patch tokens are discarded. Although effec-tive, this design increases the model’s sequence length from P to P + K, adding computational overhead. Flexible-length tokenizers extend this idea [1, 15] by enabling a single model to operate under different token budgets. A commonly used approach is nested dropout [19], in which the latent sequence is randomly truncated during train-ing. The decoder must therefore reconstruct images from sequences of varying lengths, allowing the tokenizer to support multiple representation sizes at infer-ence time. An alternative design, which we refer to as patch-derived tokens, avoids reg-ister tokens altogether and derives its latent tokens from spatially arranged en-coder features. These range from directly retaining or transforming patch tokens to initializing a shorter query sequence through spatial pooling of the encoder feature map [8, 27, 28]. Nested dropout [19] can be applied to the resulting en-coder sequence to obtain ordered representations of varying lengths. Although both bottlenecks have been analyzed in prior work, they have been evaluated under different model architectures, training objectives, quantization schemes, token budgets, and downstream generative models. These differences make it difficult to determine which observed effects can be attributed specifi-cally to the bottleneck design. We address this gap by isolating the bottleneck design and systematically evaluating its effects on reconstruction quality, gener-ation quality, computational cost, and the spatial organization of the resulting representations. Specifically, our contributions are as follows: 

– We present, to our knowledge, the first controlled comparison of patch-derived and register bottlenecks for flexible-length image tokenization utiliz-ing the same backbone, quantizer, objective and training regime. This allows us to isolate a design choice that is otherwise confounded by differences in model architecture and training setup. Comparing Variable-Length Image Tokenizers 3

– We characterize the trade-offs among reconstruction quality, generation qual-ity, and computational cost across a range of token budgets. 

– We analyze the spatial influence of individual latent tokens and identify con-sistent spatial organization in both designs. Learned token representations include a subset of spatially diffuse tokens, whereas patch-derived represen-tations exhibit substantially stronger spatial locality. 

## 2 Related Work 

Vector-quantized autoencoders Vector-quantized autoencoders [6,16,18] learn discrete compressed representations of images by mapping continuous encoder outputs to entries in a learned codebook. The resulting discrete codes are then passed to the decoder for reconstruction. These models provide an effective framework for learning compact image representations and have become a com-mon basis for generative image models. 

Register tokens Recent work has explored representing images using compact sets of latent tokens rather than spatially arranged feature maps. TiTok [30] represents images using a fixed number of latent tokens, which serve as a mem-ory bank that the image tokens of the decoder can retrieve information from via attention. This design decouples the size of the latent representation from the spatial resolution of the input image. One-D-Piece [15] builds on this idea by introducing an ordered and flexible-length one-dimensional latent represen-tation. This is achieved using tail-token dropout, which can be interpreted as a variant of nested dropout. By dropping suffixes of the latent sequence dur-ing training, the model is encouraged to arrange information according to token importance, enabling reconstruction from variable-length prefixes. They further propose a token-contribution analysis which we apply to a controlled comparison of patch-derived and learned-register bottlenecks. FlexTok [1] uses learned regis-ter tokens with nested dropout and causal attention to produce ordered discrete sequences of variable length. It reconstructs images using a rectified-flow decoder and demonstrates that the resulting tokens support downstream autoregressive generation. SEMANTICIST [25] similarly resamples spatial image features into a sequence of learned concept tokens, but imposes a PCA-like causal ordering and reconstructs images using a diffusion-based decoder. Unlike the discrete repre-sentations considered in our study, SEMANTICIST produces continuous latent tokens. Both approaches demonstrate the effectiveness of learned latent queries, but their generative decoders and training objectives differ substantially from deterministic vector-quantized autoencoders. 

Patch-derived tokens ElasticTok [27] instead derives its latent representation directly from spatially arranged encoder tokens and applies nested dropout to support reconstruction from variable-length prefixes. ReTok [8] uses a related but more elaborate spatially initialized bottleneck: its query tokens are initialized from the encoder’s two-dimensional feature map through multi-level average pooling and are subsequently refined by a Q-Former [2, 11]. Existing methods therefore differ not only in how variable-length represen-tations are trained, but also in how their latent tokens are initialized, how they 4 P. Schenkel et al. 

interact with spatial encoder features, which quantization schemes and decoders they use, and how semantic alignment is imposed. These differences make it difficult to isolate the effect of the bottleneck construction itself. Our work ad-dresses this gap by comparing patch-derived and independently learned register tokens under a shared encoder-decoder architecture, quantizer, nested-dropout schedule, training objective, and downstream autoregressive modeling setup. 

## 3 Method 

We train autoencoders with variable-length, ordered one-dimensional latent rep-resentations. The encoder maps an input image to a sequence of latent tokens, which is discretized using vector quantization. To induce an ordering over this sequence, we apply nested dropout to the latent tokens during training. The au-toencoder architecture itself is based on a standard Vision Transformer encoder-decoder backbone. 

3.1 Information Flow 

For autoencoders with adaptive-length 1D latent representations, two main de-sign choices are used to control the information flow between the encoder and decoder: Patch-derived tokens and register tokens. An overview of both config-urations is shown in Figure 2. 

Patch-derived tokens In this configuration, the truncated representation of the encoder, consisting of r tokens, is copied directly to the decoder, initializing the first r decoder tokens. The decoder must learn to transform these tokens into the corresponding image patches. For the remaining zero-initialized tokens, the decoder must infer information by attending to the first r tokens before mapping them to RGB patch representations. Note that the underlying 2D grid structure imposed by rearranging the image patches into a sequence is disrupted only through nested dropout. 

Register tokens In this approach, the encoder processes the embedded image patches alongside a set of learned register tokens acting as a memory bank that the decoder can attend to. As only the register tokens are passed to the decoder, the encoder is encouraged to store relevant image information in them. As in patch-derived tokens, nested dropout is used to facilitate variable-length decod-ing. The decoder uses positional embeddings as query tokens that attend to the register tokens to incorporate information about the image. These embeddings are then transformed into RGB patch representations. Because the register to-kens are learned parameters rather than spatially arranged image tokens, they do not impose the same inherent 2D grid structure as patch-derived tokens.

4.1 Inference Speed 

We measure inference throughput on 256 × 256 images using a single NVIDIA A100 GPU and a batch size of 128. Results are reported in Table 1. Although both architectures support variable-length ordered representations, their com-putational costs differ substantially. For patch-derived tokens, image patches are directly mapped to latent tokens and back to patch representations. Consequently, both the encoder and decoder process a fixed sequence of 256 tokens, independent of the retained token budget, resulting in constant throughput. In contrast, the register token encoder jointly processes 256 image-patch embeddings and 256 learned register tokens yielding a substantially lower throughput. The register token decoder processes the retained register tokens together with 256 output queries, such that its sequence length ranges between 257 and 512 tokens. As a result, its throughput is comparable to that of the patch-derived token decoder at small token budgets, but decreases substantially as the token budget grows.

4.4 Latent Space Analysis 

To characterize the spatial influence of individual latent tokens, we perform a token-corruption analysis. We replace one token at a time with a randomly sam-pled codebook entry and measure the squared per-pixel difference between the corrupted reconstruction and the clean reconstruction. The resulting sensitivity maps indicate which output regions are most affected by perturbing each token. Qualitative examples are shown in Figure 3. As shown in Figure 4, patch-derived tokens predominantly exhibit localized effects, suggesting that they retain the spatial specialization induced by their initial patch-based organization. Nested dropout partially relaxes this spatial bias, with some of the earliest tokens developing broader spatial influence. Reg-ister tokens also frequently develop spatially localized influence, despite having no predefined token-to-patch correspondence. This indicates that spatial special-ization can emerge given the architecture design and reconstruction objective. REPA largely preserves the localized behavior of patch-derived tokens, but leads to more spatially diffuse effects for many register tokens. ## 5 Conclusion 

We investigated two approaches for learning compact visual token representa-tions: Patch-derived tokens, which inherit an explicit spatial organization, and register tokens, which are free to develop a latent structure during training. By combining both approaches with nested dropout, we obtain representations that support variable token budgets and can be used for both reconstruction and downstream autoregressive generation. Our generative evaluation shows that increasing the token budget improves performance up to approximately 128 – 192 tokens, after which gains largely sat-urate. REPA further improves generative quality when the downstream model has sufficient capacity and is particularly effective for patch-derived tokens, which perform strongly even at small token budgets. Register tokens achieve comparable performance with a sufficiently large generative model, but their combination with REPA is less robust when the generative model’s capacity is limited. The token-corruption analysis provides additional insight into the learned latent organization. Patch-derived tokens largely retain spatially localized in-fluence, while register tokens also develop spatial specialization despite having no explicitly imposed two-dimensional layout. REPA can produce tokens with broader spatial influence, especially among register tokens. Overall, our results suggest that both structured and unconstrained tokenization schemes can learn effective compact image representations, while their inductive biases lead to dif-ferent trade-offs in spatial organization and downstream generative performance. Although most aspects of the experimental setup are tightly controlled across tokenizer variants, the comparison is not compute-matched. Register tokens re-quire more computation due to the increased number of tokens processed per image. An explicit compute-matched comparison could disentangle the effects of bottleneck design from differences in training compute and clarify whether the additional compute contributes to the performance gap observed without REPA. Further, our work focused entirely on autoregressive models for the down-stream generative task, as nested dropout encourages the learning of an ordered representation. It remains open whether non-autoregressive models can effec-tively exploit such an ordering.
