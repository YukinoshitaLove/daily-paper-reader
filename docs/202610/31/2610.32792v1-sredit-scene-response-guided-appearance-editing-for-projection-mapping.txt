ABSTRACT
Projection-based appearance editing changes selected visual at-
tributes of a physical object through projection mapping (PM). To
display a desired appearance, PM typically uses projector compen-
sation, which computes a projector input that counteracts the geo-
metric and photometric distortions of the scene. Compensation can
only approximate this appearance within the available projection
range, and it cannot revise the appearance itself. Pretrained LLM-
based image editors provide a direct way to produce the desired
appearance from a text prompt or reference image; projector com-
pensation can then attempt to reproduce the edited result through
projection. However, these editors do not have an accurate mea-
sure of the scene geometry or scene response. Therefore, their out-
puts may contain geometric misalignment, inconsistent shading, or
changes outside the available projection range, which downstream
projector compensation cannot correct. To address this problem,
we introduce SREdit, an efficient response-guided framework for
projection-based appearance editing. SREdit estimates a scene re-
sponse map from the reconstructed scene that reflects how strongly
projected illumination can affect different surface regions. SREdit
uses the source appearance to constrain the early sampling process
and preserve registration with the fixed physical object, while the
scene response map adapts the amount of editing across surface
regions. Experiments across two pretrained image editors show

INTRODUCTION
Projection-based appearance editing changes selected visual at-
tributes of a physical object through projection. Most projection-
mapping research instead assumes that a desired appearance is al-
ready available and focuses on reproducing it through projector
compensation, which counteracts the geometric and photometric
effects of the surface and projector–camera setup [6,19,28]. Com-
pensation can approximate this appearance only within the avail-
able projection range and does not create the appearance itself. A
user who wants to edit an existing object must therefore prepare this
desired appearance separately. Pretrained LLM-based image edi-
tors provide a direct way to produce the desired appearance from a
text prompt or reference image; projector compensation can then
attempt to reproduce the edited result through projection.
This
pipeline reduces the need to prepare projection content manually,
but it also exposes a mismatch between image editing and physical
projection. However, these editors do not have an accurate mea-
sure of the scene geometry or scene response. Here, scene response
describes how strongly projected illumination can affect different
surface regions. Without this information, the edited target may
contain geometric misalignment, inconsistent shading, or changes

outside the available projection range, all of which should be ad-
dressed during editing because downstream projector compensation
cannot revise the target itself.
Existing approaches can incorporate scene-related constraints
through test-time gradient-based guidance or optimization [13,39].
They require gradients during generation, which adds computation
and memory on top of the generator for each edit. Other methods
inject geometry through additional control modules [46, 66], but
each module must be paired with a compatible model architecture,
and many pretrained image editors do not provide such modules.
These restrictions make it difficult to apply the same scene-related
control across different editors.
To address this problem, we introduce SREdit, an efficient
response-guided framework for projection-based appearance edit-
ing (Fig. 1). SREdit estimates a scene response map from the re-
constructed scene that reflects how strongly projected illumination
can affect different surface regions, and introduces this information
into the editing process through response-guided sampling. During
sampling, SREdit uses the source appearance to constrain the early
sampling process and preserve registration with the fixed physical
object, and adapts the amount of change according to the scene
response, preserving lower response regions more strongly while
allowing greater changes in higher response regions. It requires no
additional training or control module for the image editor and does
not backpropagate through the editor during generation. Down-
stream projector compensation remains unchanged.
Experiments across two pretrained image editors show that
SREdit improves projected appearance quality and reproduction fi-
delity while requiring little additional computation and memory.
Participants also consistently preferred the results produced with
SREdit in our user study. Our contributions are:
• To the best of our knowledge, SREdit is the first framework
to incorporate scene response into pretrained image editing
for projection mapping, while separating appearance editing
from downstream projector compensation.
• We introduce response-guided sampling, which uses a scene
response map estimated from the reconstructed scene to adapt
the amount of change across surface regions without back-
propagating through the editor during generation.
• SREdit requires no additional training or control module for
the image editor and is evaluated with two pretrained image
editors across six physical setups. Our experiments demon-
strate improved projected appearance quality and reproduc-
tion fidelity with little additional computation and memory.

RELATED WORK
2.1
Projection mapping and appearance editing
Projection mapping (PM) alters the perceived appearance of physi-
cal surfaces by superimposing projected imagery, and prior surveys
have reviewed its geometric, photometric, hardware, and applica-
tion aspects [6,19,28]. Projection has been used to augment picto-
rial artwork [5], guide artists using traditional media and tools [16],
manipulate perceived softness [51], augment physical avatars with
projected shading [3], and dynamically alter the appearance of hu-
man faces [4]. Dynamic PM further extended these effects to de-
forming non-rigid surfaces [47], markerless planar surfaces [29],
human arms with skin deformation [50], and articulated hands [15].
More recent systems address registration and display conditions in
PM. Peng et al. [49] combine high-speed facial tracking with a lens-
shift coaxial projector–camera setup to reduce misalignment in dy-
namic facial projection. Okamoto et al. [48] use a dense synthetic-
aperture projector array to suppress interaction shadows in table-
top workspaces, and Kusuyama et al. [32] combine a large-aperture
projector with a coaxial residual projector to balance shadow sup-
pression and deblurring. In brightly illuminated environments, Ya-
sui et al. [62] support PM with mixed-light-field projection, and Fu-
jimura et al. [17] maintain projection contrast with target-excluding
light-field illumination. These systems demonstrate a broad range
of appearance changes on physical targets. However, the projected
content or desired effect is usually pre-authored or produced by an
application-specific authoring and tracking pipeline.
Once a desired appearance is specified, projector compensation
computes a projector input that counteracts geometric and pho-
tometric distortions.
Practical and robust approaches simplified
projector–camera calibration [10] and improved compensation un-
der nonlinear device responses [18], steep surface-reflectance vari-
ations [45], and cooperative multi-projector configurations [53].
Perceptually motivated compensation exploits chromatic adaptation
and perceptual anchoring [27], while related work on non-negative
image synthesis accounts for the fact that projection can add
light but cannot directly remove light from a physical scene [41].
Learning-based methods subsequently formulated photometric [23]
or full [26] projector compensation as end-to-end learning prob-
lems, with related work addressing projection deblurring [30] and
high-resolution compensation [57]. More recently, Li et al. [35]
generalize full compensation to unseen projection surfaces, illumi-
nation conditions, and projector–camera poses without fine-tuning
or retraining. Together, these methods formulate the inverse prob-
lem of computing a projector input for a prescribed camera-view
appearance.
The complementary forward problem is commonly studied in
projector–camera systems (ProCams) as relighting or project-and-
capture simulation. Given a projector input and a physical scene,
it predicts the image observed by the camera. Early methods rep-
resented this relationship through light transport matrices, with ef-
ficient approximations developed for complex light transport [55].
Learned ProCams models subsequently combined geometric and
photometric mappings for relighting, compensation, and shape re-
construction [24], while neural reflectance fields enabled view-
agnostic projection mapping [14].
Since these learned models
are differentiable, they can also serve as simulators for optimiz-
ing projector inputs. For example, Huang and Ling [25] optimize
projector-based adversarial attacks through a learned project-and-
capture network, PCNet.
More recently, Li et al. [36] formu-
late ProCams simulation through differentiable path tracing, while
Deng et al. [12] represent surface geometry and material with 2D
Gaussians and jointly estimate the ProCams mappings through dif-
ferentiable physically based rendering. These models make it pos-
sible to evaluate a projection result and optimize projector inputs in
a computational space, but the semantic appearance to be displayed
must still be supplied externally.
Vision–language models have also been applied to projected
scenes. Cao et al. [9] distinguish the physical scene from projected
content for projection-aware captioning. This work addresses se-
mantic understanding rather than projection content creation. Com-
plementary work has introduced generative models into the cre-
ation of projected appearances. Erel et al. [14] use a 2D genera-
tive model to define consistent appearances across multiple views
and optimize the projected texture through a neural reflectance
field. Deng et al. [12] use generated or edited images as desired
targets for a Gaussian-based ProCams model.
Deng et al. [13]
combine language-guided style transfer with learned compensa-
tion and project-and-capture simulation; projection-consistency,
compensation-saturation, and projection-saturation losses are then
used to update the style-transfer model. However, LAPIG is not
adapted to 3D scenes.
Liu et al. [39] inject gamut, depth, and
edge priors into cooperative generation and use bounded numeri-
cal optimization for multi-projector compensation in the adversar-
ial setting. These methods reduce manual preparation of projection

content, but mainly formulate the task as target generation, surface
adaptation, or projector-input optimization. Our problem begins
with the observed scene and seeks to edit its appearance under the
current physical setup while preserving registration with the physi-
cal object.
2.2
Image editing and conditional control
Image editing provides a direct interface for deriving a desired ap-
pearance from an image of the current scene. Early diffusion-based
methods support image guides, written instructions, or visual exem-
plars [7, 44, 61], while recent unified models cover broader editing
tasks and interleaved image–text conditions [21, 60, 65]. The edit-
ing variants of Qwen-Image and LongCat-Image further provide the
pretrained image editors used in our experiments [43, 58]. A cen-
tral requirement of these methods is to apply the requested change
while preserving source content that should remain unchanged. Ex-
isting approaches control cross-attention, infer edit masks, inject
source features and self-attention maps, or retrieve source content
through mutual attention [8, 11, 22, 54]. Kulikov et al. [31] con-
struct an ordinary differential equation (ODE) that directly maps
between source and target distributions without source inversion or
per-image optimization. Zhang et al. [68] formulate image editing
as in-context generation with parameter-efficient LoRA-MoE tun-
ing. These methods derive their controls from images, text, masks,
or internal model features rather than the calibrated physical setup.
They therefore provide no direct mechanism for adapting the edit
to spatial variations in scene response.
Another line of work edits a reconstructed NeRF or 3D Gaussian
scene to maintain consistency across rendered views [20,33,56,59].
These methods update and optimize the reconstruction for each
edit, which can be time-consuming. We instead keep the recon-
structed physical scene fixed and use it to control editing of the
target appearance in the camera view before projection.
Conditional control can be introduced through trained modules
or by modifying the sampling process. Zhang et al. [66] and Mou et
al. [46] train additional branches or adapters for spatial conditions
such as edges, depth, segmentation, and pose while keeping the
original generator frozen. Yu et al. [64] and Ye et al. [63] instead
guide sampling with energy functions or target predictors without
training another conditional generator, but require target evaluation
and gradients during inference. Avrahami et al. [1, 2] and Lug-
mayr et al. [40] likewise require no additional training and apply
the user’s edit only within a specified image region. Levin and
Fried [34] use a user-provided change map to assign different edit
strengths to pixels or regions without external-loss optimization. In
these methods, the user specifies which regions should change and
by how much, whereas our scene response map describes the rela-
tive effect of direct projector illumination at each surface location.
Unlike the physically guided PM pipelines discussed above [13,
39], SREdit applies the same response-guided sampling rule to two
pretrained image editors without updating their parameters, adding
an additional control module, or backpropagating a physical loss
during editing.

3.1
Problem formulation
Given a fixed physical scene, a calibrated projector–camera setup, a
target camera view, and an editing condition cedit, our goal is to edit
the target object’s appearance according to cedit and reproduce the
resulting appearance as closely as possible through physical projec-
tion from the target view.
The condition contains a text prompt and may include a refer-
ence image when supported by the editor. We separate the task into
two stages. First, the image editor produces a camera-space edited
target from the observed object. Second, projector compensation
computes a compensation image whose captured result approaches
that target. SREdit changes how the edited target is produced and
keeps downstream projector compensation separate.

EXPERIMENTS
We organize the evaluation around two comparisons.
First, we
compare the complete SREdit pipeline with the language-guided
projection-mapping methods Nepmap [14] and LAPIG [13]. Sec-
ond, we evaluate Qwen-Image-Edit-2511 [58] and LongCat-Image-
Edit [43], hereafter Qwen and LongCat.
We refer to the origi-
nal Qwen and LongCat sampling pipelines followed by the same
downstream projector compensation as Native-Qwen and Native-
LongCat. SREdit-Qwen and SREdit-LongCat differ from their cor-
responding Native versions only by response-guided sampling. Ev-
ery projector input is physically projected and captured by the cam-
era. We measure target reproduction and agreement with the editing
request, conduct a user study, and ablate the scene response map,
early control ratio r, and control strength S.
4.1
Experimental settings
4.1.1
Physical setup
Our projector–camera system consists of an O.B.E C3 Air pro-
jector and a Nikon D3200 camera. For each scene, the projector
and camera poses, together with the camera settings, remain fixed
throughout data acquisition. All experiments are conducted using
an NVIDIA A100 GPU with 64 GB of memory.
4.1.2
Implementation details
We implement Nepmap and LAPIG following their published
pipelines. Nepmap optimizes the projector input through its learned
neural reflectance field, whereas LAPIG performs test-time opti-
mization using InstructPix2Pix [7], CompenNeSt [26], and PC-
Net [25]. For our object-centered scenes, we restrict the LAPIG
losses to the segmented object region and leave all other param-
eters unchanged. The public ConPhyG release [39] provides the
gamut-network component but not the complete generation and
multi-projector pipeline. We therefore exclude it from the quan-
titative comparison because the full method cannot be faithfully re-
produced.
We use the Gaussian-based differentiable scene renderer [12]
and iterative refinement for projector compensation. The renderer
remains frozen during appearance editing, and iterative refinement
optimizes only the compensation image used as the projector input.
Qwen and LongCat use 40 and 50 sampling steps, respectively,
and receive the same editing request. Within each editor, Native
and SREdit use the same source appearance and iterative refine-
ment. Because Nepmap and LAPIG use different conditioning in-
terfaces, we adapt their prompts while preserving the same target
concept. The prompts used for the representative qualitative results
are provided in the supplementary material.
We fix r = 0.2,S = 2.5 for Qwen and r = 0.2,S = 3.0 for Long-
Cat according to the result quality on a separate setup that is not
used for evaluation. These settings remain unchanged in the main
comparison and scene response map ablation.
4.1.3
Evaluation metrics
We evaluate reproduction fidelity using peak signal-to-noise ra-
tio (PSNR), structural similarity index measure (SSIM), learned
perceptual image patch similarity (LPIPS) [67], and CIEDE2000
color difference (∆E) [42] between the intended appearance of
each method and its camera-captured result within the object re-
gion. Since the intended appearances produced by different meth-
ods may vary in complexity, these image-based metrics measure
how faithfully each method reproduces its own target rather than di-
rectly measuring editing quality. Contrastive Language-Image Pre-
training (CLIP) [52] and the image-text matching score of Boot-
strapping Language-Image Pre-training (BLIP-ITM) [37] measure
agreement between the captured result and the requested appear-
ance; all methods are evaluated using the same concise target de-
scription. We also report wall-clock generation time and peak video
random-access memory (VRAM).

As shown in Fig. 8, however, a uniform control weight does
not produce a spatially uniform perceptual edit because the resid-
ual between the Euler-updated latent and the source anchor varies
across spatial locations. In the camera-captured results, Uniform
therefore leaves unedited patches that retain the source color while
suppressing editable content elsewhere. The scene response map
preserves regions with lower response more coherently and leaves
regions with higher response greater freedom to realize the re-
quested appearance. This spatial weighting by the response map
produces fewer unedited patches in these examples, consistent with
the higher semantic agreement measured by CLIP and BLIP-ITM.
We next examine the influence of the early control ratio r and
the control strength S. Fig. 7 shows the complete parameter grid for
both image editors and summarizes the corresponding quantitative
trends over all 18 cases from six setups.
The two parameters control complementary aspects of response-
guided sampling. The control strength S determines the overall
magnitude of correction toward the source anchor, whereas r de-
termines how its normalized temporal weight is distributed over the
early sampling stage. A smaller r concentrates the control in the
earliest steps, while a larger r distributes it over a longer portion of
the sampling trajectory. Thus, changing r redistributes the control
without changing its total control weight.
As shown in Fig. 7, a small S provides limited guidance regard-
less of r, and the results remain closer to those of the correspond-
ing Native version. Increasing S strengthens source preservation,
particularly in regions with lower response, and generally reduces
geometric misalignment and appearance variations that are difficult
to reproduce under projection. With a relatively small r, the control
is concentrated at the beginning of sampling. This stabilizes regis-
tration, coarse color, and shading early, while leaving more of the
sampling trajectory for texture refinement.
The influence of r becomes more visible as S increases. Extend-
ing the controlled stage keeps the source-anchor correction active
over a longer portion of the sampling trajectory, but an overly large
r combined with a strong S may suppress local texture and shad-
ing details. For both editors, the four reproduction metrics improve
monotonically with S at every r and are best at r = 0.5,S = 3.0,
which also gives the lowest CLIP score in the grid. In practice, we
recommend keeping r around 0.2 and adjusting S: increase it when
the captured result shows misalignment or clipped highlights, and
decrease it when the requested texture is weakened.

5
DISCUSSION
5.1
Limitations and future work
Editing and reproduction trade-off. The early control ratio r and
control strength S determine the balance between physical repro-
duction and the semantic and textural richness of the edit.
In-
creasing S or extending the controlled portion of sampling tends
to preserve registration and the observed surface appearance, but
may weaken the requested texture. Weaker or shorter control leaves
more freedom for the image editor, but may produce changes that
are difficult to reproduce through projection. Because the scene re-
sponse differs across physical setups, a single parameter pair cannot
provide the preferred balance in every case.
Fig. 9 shows this trade-off under a fixed setting: registration is
retained, but part of the requested texture is weakened and spatially
uneven. The preferred parameters therefore depend on whether the
user prioritizes closer physical reproduction or a semantically richer
edit. Future work may select r and S automatically for each setup
and editing request.
Computational requirements. SREdit does not train or fine-tune
the image editor, attach an additional control module, or backprop-
agate gradients through the editor during generation. Nevertheless,
the pretrained image editors are large and still dominate the run-
time and memory requirements of the complete pipeline. Future
work should examine how response-guided sampling can be used
with lighter image editors.
Soft constraint.
Response-guided sampling acts as a soft con-
straint: it biases the edit according to the relative scene response
but does not explicitly exclude all appearances that are difficult to
reproduce. Under extreme surface colors, a large difference be-
tween the source and requested appearances, or overly strong con-
trol, sampling may approach a degenerate solution in which the re-
quested texture is completely suppressed. Future work should seek
an efficient hard constraint that prevents this failure without gradi-
ent computation or an additional model during generation.

CONCLUSION
In this paper, we introduced SREdit, an efficient response-guided
framework that incorporates scene response into pretrained image
editing for projection mapping while separating appearance edit-
ing from downstream projector compensation. SREdit estimates a
scene response map from the reconstructed scene that reflects how
strongly projected illumination can affect different surface regions.
During early sampling, it uses the source appearance to preserve
registration with the physical object and adapts the amount of edit-
ing according to the scene response. SREdit requires no additional
9


training or control module for the image editor, does not back-
propagate through the editor during generation, and leaves down-
stream projector compensation unchanged. Experiments with two
pretrained image editors across six physical setups show consistent
improvements in projected appearance quality and reproduction fi-
delity. Response-guided sampling adds less than one second to im-
age generation with negligible VRAM overhead, and participants
preferred SREdit in 72.4% of the user-study comparisons. These
results show that scene response can guide pretrained image edit-
ing before projector compensation with little additional cost.

SREdit: Scene Response Guided Appearance Editing for Projection
Mapping
—
Supplementary Materials
—
A
INTRODUCTION
In this supplementary material, we present additional qualitative comparisons and parameter ablations across six physical setups and di-
verse text prompts. Figures S1–S20 include both generated appearances and camera-captured projection results, providing a more complete
evaluation of SREdit under real projection. We further provide the complete user study protocol and all 36 comparison trials.
B
EXPERIMENTAL CONFIGURATION
Configuration
Setting
Physical system
O.B.E C3 Air projector and Nikon D3200 camera. The projector and camera poses and the camera settings
remain fixed within each setup.
Compute
AMD EPYC 7642 CPU and NVIDIA A100 GPU with 64 GB of available memory.
Image editors
Qwen-Image-Edit-2511 (Qwen) with 40 sampling steps and LongCat-Image-Edit (LongCat) with 50 sam-
pling steps.
Main parameters
Qwen uses r = 0.2 and S = 2.5; LongCat uses r = 0.2 and S = 3.0.
Parameter sweep
r ∈{0.1,0.2,0.3,0.5} and S ∈{0.5,1.0,1.5,2.0,2.5,3.0}.
Projection pipeline
The Gaussian-based differentiable scene renderer remains frozen, while iterative refinement updates only
the compensation image.
Evaluation
Six physical setups and 18 text prompts. PSNR, SSIM, LPIPS, and ∆E are computed within the object
region; CLIP and BLIP-ITM measure agreement with the target description.
Comparison protocol. All reported projection results are obtained by physically projecting the compensation image and capturing the result
with the camera. For each image editor, the Native and SREdit versions use the same source appearance, editing request, number of sampling
steps, and compensation procedure. Qwen and LongCat receive the same editing request for each case. For Nepmap and LAPIG, we adapt
the request to the input format required by each method while retaining the same target appearance.
Scene response map construction. For each reconstructed object, we evaluate the channelwise direct-response factor, average it across the
three color channels, and normalize it over the reconstructed-object support to obtain the scene response map used during editing. The map
is computed once for each setup and remains fixed across its editing requests.
Ablation protocol. The parameter study evaluates every combination of the listed r and S values using both generated appearances and
camera-captured results. In the scene response map ablation, the uniform map takes the mean over the complete scene response map and
assigns it to every image location. The image editor, sampling schedule, control parameters, and compensation procedure remain unchanged.
C
USER STUDY CONFIGURATION
Configuration
Setting
Participants
20 participants (10 female and 10 male), aged 18–24 (mean 20.25, SD 1.37); nine reported a background
in image processing, computer graphics, or computer vision.
Study design
Anonymous, within-subject pairwise comparison conducted online.
Trials
36 camera-captured comparison pairs: 18 Qwen cases and 18 LongCat cases.
Compared results
the Native and SREdit versions, displayed anonymously as A and B; their positions were randomized and
recorded for every trial.
Evaluation criteria
Text alignment, artifacts, lighting and surface realism, visible misalignment, and overall preference.
Responses
Four-level forced choice for comparative questions; visible misalignment was judged separately for A and
B as present or absent.
Participation was voluntary and anonymous. Before beginning the study, participants reviewed the study information and provided informed
consent. Comparative responses were mapped back to the method shown on each side before aggregation. The complete instructions and all
36 formal trials are provided at the end of this supplementary material.

F
USER STUDY QUESTIONNAIRE
F.1
Instructions
Participants were told that each comparison concerned an edited appearance physically projected onto a real object and photographed by a
camera. For each trial, they were asked to consider the text instruction, the source object, and the two captured results as a whole. They were
also informed that the two methods were anonymous, that the A/B positions carried no meaning, and that there were no correct answers.
F.2
Interface and questions
Each trial showed the source object, the text instruction, and two anonymous projection results labeled A and B. The assignment of the SREdit
and corresponding Native versions to A or B was randomized and recorded for each trial. Participants answered the following five questions:
1. Text alignment: which result better matched the requested appearance?
2. Artifacts: which result showed fewer visible artifacts, such as abnormal color leakage or bleed-through inside the object region?
3. Lighting and surface realism: which result showed more natural illumination and surface appearance, with fewer abnormal highlights
or discontinuous shading?
4. Visible misalignment: did each result show visible misalignment, such as texture crossing the object boundary or object parts appearing
at implausible locations?
5. Overall preference: which result was preferable when all of the above aspects were considered together?
Text alignment, artifacts, realism, and overall preference used a four-level forced-choice scale: “A is much better,” “A is slightly better,”
“B is slightly better,” and “B is much better.” Visible misalignment was judged separately for A and B as either present or absent.
Comparative responses were converted to the method shown on each side before aggregation, so the reported preference rate always
denotes the proportion of judgments favoring SREdit. The 36 formal trials are reproduced below in their presentation order; practice trials
are not included in the analysis.
