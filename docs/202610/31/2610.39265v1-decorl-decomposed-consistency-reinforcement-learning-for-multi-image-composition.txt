Abstract 

Multi-image composition (MICo) is a critical yet challenging capability for large-scale image editing models, where consistency degrades rapidly as the number of reference images increases. While supervised fine-tuning (SFT) can equip models with basic MICo ability, it provides only implicit supervision on the composed image and often fails to learn reliable object-level correspondence across multi-ple references. We propose DeCoRL, a reinforcement-learning (RL) framework that improves MICo consistency by decomposing holistic MICo consistency into

DeCoRL first establishes instruction-relevant object corre-spondences across reference images and localizes the corresponding regions in the generated image. It then uses a VLM-based reward model trained on single-object consistency annotations to produce fine-grained, object-wise feedback, which is aggregated into a multi-object consistency reward to guide RL post-training. We further introduce DeCo-Bench, a benchmark covering compositions from 2 to 6 reference images. Experiments on DeCo-Bench and the public MICo-Bench demonstrate consistent improvements in MICo consistency. 

1 Introduction 

Recent instruction-based editing models [3, 41, 48, 32, 39, 42, 22, 16, 17] have achieved strong performance in instruction following and image quality. However, most open-source systems [22, 41] are designed for single-image editing and cannot naturally support multi-image composition (MICo), where multiple reference images must be jointly composed into a single edited result. Although recent models such as Qwen-Image-Edit-2509/2511 [48] and FLUX.2-dev [21] begin to support this setting, they still struggle to maintain consistency as the number of reference images increases. A central challenge in MICo is cross-image consistency: the model must integrate multiple visual conditions while preserving the correct identities, attributes, and semantic bindings of multiple entities in the generated image. This is fundamentally harder than single-image editing because the model must resolve many ambiguous many-to-one correspondences: each generated entity may inherit visual properties from a different reference image, and this ambiguity grows quickly with the number of inputs [57, 25, 56]. Despite this challenge, most existing MICo approaches primarily rely on large-scale (often synthetic) multi-image editing data and supervised fine-tuning (SFT) to endow models with multi-reference capability [47, 44]. However, SFT typically provides only implicit supervision on the generated image, without explicitly teaching object-level correspondence and binding across references, which often leads to identity confusion or attribute leakage when the number of conditions grows [57, 25, 56]. These limitations motivate reinforcement learning (RL) as a post-training paradigm for image editing, since it can directly optimize model preferences via reward signals rather than merely imitate paired targets as in SFT. Recent editing works have similarly adopted RL-style post-training to improve alignment and controllability beyond pure SFT [26, 54, 23, 34, 43, 14, 49, 18]. The effectiveness of RL hinges on a reliable reward. However, designing such a reward for MICo consistency is particularly challenging: as the number of reference images grows, holistic scoring must jointly resolve ambiguous correspondence and attribute-to-source binding under many competing visual conditions—an ill-posed judgment that remains unreliable even for strong VLM evaluators (e.g., GPT-4o) due to limited cross-image attention [47]. We propose DeCoRL (De composed Co nsistency Reinforcement Learning), an RL framework built on a central methodological insight: holistic MICo consistency can be decomposed into tractable consistency checks over instruction-relevant, localizable objects. This decomposition is the core reward-design principle, while the specific evaluator, grounding model, and RL optimizer are implementation choices. We first train a single-object consistency reward model using a vision-language model (VLM) [37, 24, 1, 28], with chain-of-thought (CoT) [46, 53, 8, 52] supervision, and use its yes /no token probabilities [6, 30, 7] as a continuous reward signal. We then build an automatic object alignment pipeline: a VLM identifies instruction-relevant objects across the reference images, and Grounding DINO [31] localizes them. Based on these aligned objects, we define a multi-object consistency reward that matches generated objects with their corresponding references, scores each matched pair using our trained single-object consistency reward model , and penalizes missing objects (i.e., instruction-relevant entities not present in the generated output). This multi-object reward is then used to post-train the editing model via diffusion-based RL. We further introduce DeCo-Bench , a dedicated benchmark for evaluating MICo consistency. By per-forming SFT followed by DeCoRL on the Qwen-Image-Edit2511 [48] model, we observe substantial and consistent improvements in MICo consistency on both DeCo-Bench and the public MICo-Bench, demonstrating the effectiveness of our method. Our main contributions are summarized as follows: 2• Core insight. We identify object-level decomposition as a practical reward-design principle for MICo: once instruction-relevant object correspondences are established, consistency can be evaluated locally instead of through a single holistic judgment. • DeCoRL framework. We develop an RL post-training framework that aggregates object-level consistency scores into a multi-object reward, including penalties for missing objects. VLM parsing, Grounding DINO localization, and CoT-trained reward modeling provide one modular instantiation of this framework. • Benchmark and evaluation. We introduce DeCo-Bench (2–6 reference images) and demonstrate consistent improvements on both DeCo-Bench and the independently developed MICo-Bench. 

2 Related Work 

Single-Image Editing. Instruction-based image editing has advanced rapidly with the development of text-to-image (T2I) foundation models [9, 41, 48, 20, 5, 4, 11, 10]. Representative open-source models (LongCat-Image-Edit [41] and FLUX.1-Kontext [22]) achieve strong performance in instruc-tion following and image quality. However, these methods are primarily designed for single-image editing and do not support MICo. 

Multi-Image Composition. Multi-image composition (MICo) requires jointly integrating several reference images into a single generated result, making it substantially more challenging than single-image editing. Recent open-source models such as Qwen-Image-Edit [48], FLUX.2-dev [21], and FireRed-Image-Edit [42] have begun to support this setting, while advanced closed-source systems such as Nano-Banana-Pro [13] and Seedream 4.0 [39] further highlight its practical importance. However, maintaining MICo consistency remains difficult, especially as the number of reference images increases. Most recent MICo progress relies on large-scale (often synthetic) data construction followed by supervised fine-tuning (SFT), which can efficiently imitate demonstrations but typically supervises only the final composed image and provides limited explicit signals for learning cross-image corre-spondence and consistency relations. As a result, MICo consistency often degrades in harder cases with many reference images. Notably, this issue also appears in strong closed-source systems (e.g., Nano-Banana-Pro [13] and Seedream 4.0 [39]), which sometimes produce inconsistent compositions, e.g., object identities or attributes may change in complex multi-reference settings. 

Reinforcement-Learning for Image Editing. Reinforcement learning (RL) has become an effec-tive post-training tool for diffusion-based image editing, and has been increasingly adopted in recent open-source systems. For example, Skywork UniPic 2.0 explicitly incorporates online RL to improve unified multimodal generation and editing capability [45], while LongCat-Image-Edit [41], Qwen-Image-Edit [48], Edit-R1 [26], and FireRed-Image-Edit [42] also leverage preference optimization or reward-driven training (e.g., DPO [38], GRPO [40], Flow-GRPO [29], and Diffusion NFT [55]) to enhance image quality, instruction adherence, and controllability. These successful practices suggest that RL provides a promising direction for improving editing models beyond standard SFT objectives. Motivated by this, we propose DeCoRL, which leads to substantial improvements in MICo consistency. 

3 Method 

We present DeCoRL (see Figure 2), an RL-based post-training framework for improving consistency in MICo. The key idea of DeCoRL is to avoid directly scoring MICo consistency between multiple reference images and a generated image, which is highly ambiguous and difficult to evaluate reliably (as stated in [47]), and instead introduce a principled decomposition into object-level consistency evaluation. This design is motivated by the observation that, once object correspondence is established, single-object consistency is more tractable to evaluate than global MICo consistency. 3.3 Single-Object Consistency Reward model 

We train a single-object consistency reward model on PaCO-RL-Dataset [35] under a chain-of-thought (CoT) evaluation protocol, since yes /no -only supervision is low-entropy and prone to shortcut overfitting [46, 53, 8, 52]. We adopt Qwen3-VL-32B [2] as the reward backbone and fine-tune it to answer binary consistency questions. 

Training. Each training example contains a reference crop xref , a generated crop xgen , and a textual query q about their identity and visual-attribute consistency. We fine-tune the VLM with standard autoregressive cross-entropy on annotated CoT responses containing an explanation and a binary decision ( yes /no ).

3.6 Diffusion NFT Post-Training

The decomposition-based reward is not specific to this optimizer. 4.1 Training Setup. SFT. We fine-tune Qwen-Image-Edit2511 on the multi-image datasets MICo-150K [47] and UniPic 3.0 [44] described in Section 3.2, together with Nano-consistent-150K [50] for single-image editing. We train for 24,000 steps on 32 H800 GPUs with a total batch size of 128, using AdamW ( β1 = 0 .9,

β2 = 0 .999 , ϵ = 10 −8, weight decay 10 −2) and a learning rate of 2 × 10 −5.

RL. We initialize from the SFT checkpoint and perform Diffusion NFT post-training on MICo-150K [47] using LoRA [15] ( r = 32 ). Training uses 32 H800 GPUs, a group size of 16, and AdamW (β1 = 0 .9, β2 = 0 .999 , ϵ = 10 −10 , weight decay 10 −4) with a learning rate of 3 × 10 −4.

Compared with the SFT model, DeCoRL yields clear gains in Subject Resemblance (SR) (7.01 →7.64 ), Naturalness (6.54 →8.01 ), and Artifacts (5.76 →6.64 ), indicating better identity preservation and visual quality with fewer artifacts. Meanwhile, Prompt Following (PF) is slightly lower than the SFT variant (8.76 →8.69), suggesting that the improvements are not merely due to reward hacking that over-optimizes a single criterion. Notably, SFT consistently improves all four metrics over the baseline, largely because it strengthens basic multi-image editing ability, reducing failure cases and improving instruction following in multi-image settings. 

Ablation Study of Single-Object Consistency Reward Model on DeCo-Bench. We provide an ablation study of our single-object consistency reward model mentioned in Section 3.3, as presented in Table 4. Using a vanilla VLM yields only marginal gains over SFT (overall 7.05 →7.21), suggesting limited ability to provide reliable supervision for MICo consistency. In contrast, our CoT-trained VLM substantially improves performance across 2–6 images, reaching 8.16 overall. Figure 5 further supports this finding. With the vanilla VLM reward, the multi-object consistency score remains mostly below 0.5 with limited improvement, indicating a weak optimization signal. In contrast, our CoT-trained VLM reward stays above 0.6 and steadily increases during training. The banned prompt count is also informative: a prompt is banned when its samples already obtain high and similar rewards, meaning the model has learned this reward signal. It remains zero for the vanilla VLM, while it increases over time for the CoT-trained VLM, showing that more prompts receive stable and well-learned reward signals. Overall, these results show that the CoT-trained VLM provides a more reliable and informative reward than a vanilla VLM, enabling effective DeCoRL optimization and improving MICo consistency. 94.5 Visualization

5 Conclusion 

5.1 Summary 

In this work, we introduce DeCoRL, a reinforcement-learning (RL) post-training framework that improves multi-image composition (MICo) consistency through object-level reward decomposition. DeCoRL establishes instruction-relevant object correspondences, localizes matched regions, and aggregates fine-grained single-object consistency scores into a multi-object reward for optimization. We further propose DeCo-Bench, covering MICo tasks with 2–6 reference images. Experiments on DeCo-Bench and MICo-Bench demonstrate consistent improvements, particularly in challenging many-reference settings, showing the effectiveness of decomposed consistency rewards for scalable MICo post-training. 

5.2 Discussion and Future Directions From many references to object-wise evaluation. DeCoRL is built on the principle that a difficult holistic MICo consistency judgment can be decomposed into more tractable checks. Instead of evaluating all references against the generated image jointly, we first establish instruction-relevant object correspondences, then score each matched reference–generated object pair individually, and finally aggregate these scores into a multi-object reward. This turns a many-reference judgment into a collection of simpler object-level evaluations while retaining a joint objective of preserving multiple subjects. The central methodological contribution is this decomposition-based reward design, rather than a particular VLM, grounding model, or RL optimizer. 

Generality and future directions. Our CoT-trained VLM is one instantiation of the single-object evaluator. In principle, any reliable evaluator that produces comparable object-level consistency scores can be integrated into the same framework; the reward design is also conceptually independent of Diffusion NFT. This modularity suggests opportunities for other evaluators and editing backbones, although such extensions require empirical validation. The current method targets localizable objects and depends on accurate grounding and matching. Future directions include improving correspon-dence and robustness to imperfect crops, exploring adaptive aggregation to better protect low-scoring objects, and extending the reward to relational, interaction-level, and other non-localizable consistency. Scaling beyond six references additionally requires suitable training data and further evaluation.

Grounding and object matching diagnostics. We sampled 10,000 examples from the training set, applied Grounding DINO to identify the relevant object regions, and used a VLM to judge whether the detected crops matched the assigned object labels. Under this VLM-based verification protocol, the resulting label–crop matching accuracy was 94.6%. This result is a diagnostic proxy and should not be interpreted as human-annotated bounding-box localization accuracy or same-category instance-matching accuracy. 

Behavior of best- and worst-case object rewards. Figure 9 reports the best-case and worst-case object rewards over the reported training checkpoints. The worst-case reward increases from 0.3012 to 0.7423, while the best-case reward increases from 0.6712 to 0.8412. Thus, both increase under mean aggregation, and the worst-case object shows a larger absolute increase over these checkpoints. These observations are consistent with improvements beyond the easiest objects, although they do not establish that mean aggregation is optimal among possible alternatives. 

Evaluation scope and potential reward–metric alignment. DeCo-Bench covers 2–6 reference images with 40 test cases per setting (200 cases total). It is designed to assess the localizable object-level consistency targeted by our reward and is therefore an in-domain evaluation. The separately developed MICo-Bench provides complementary evidence beyond this reward-aligned benchmark, including improvements in subject resemblance and perceptual-quality metrics. Nevertheless, this does not replace a dedicated study of possible evaluator bias. The current model was trained for settings with up to six reference images; reliability beyond this range has not been demonstrated. 

Choice of RL optimization and auxiliary reward. DeCoRL employs Diffusion NFT as a practical post-training optimizer; the object-level reward design is conceptually orthogonal to the choice of diffusion RL algorithm. Early investigations of flow-based GRPO variants suggested higher resource demands and sensitivity to optimization settings, but no matched-training controlled comparison is reported here. The general-purpose auxiliary VLM reward described in Section 3.6 is used to discourage implausible global compositions, including artifacts and incorrect object–subject interactions that a local crop-based score may miss. 

Limitations. Our decomposed reward primarily captures localizable object identity and appearance. Cropped objects cannot by themselves fully represent abstract styles, spatial relations, or interaction and binding con-straints. Grounding and same-category object matching can fail, while VLM-based evaluation may introduce bias or be vulnerable to reward hacking. DeCo-Bench is relatively small, and the main experiments use one 

19 editing backbone and 2–6 reference images. The pipeline also relies on multiple large models, increasing computational and reproducibility costs. A controlled holistic-versus-object-level reward comparison under matched RL training remains an important avenue for future work.
