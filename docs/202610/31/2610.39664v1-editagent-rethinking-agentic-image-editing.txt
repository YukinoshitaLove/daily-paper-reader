Abstract
Agentic image editors decompose complex requests into
steps, but when a step fails, they mostly retry with the same
narrow set of tools and a judge that scores each output by
eye directly. We argue that such agents are bounded by two
spaces: the action space of what the agent can do, and the
observation space of what its judge can see. On our expert-
annotated benchmark, a general-purpose editor yields no
usable result in over 40% of the cases, and no judge is
reliable across edit types. We present EditAgent, which ex-
pands both spaces. It routes each step to general editors
and specialized methods, and attaches to each candidate a
verification record. With these records, off-the-shelf VLM
judges outperform finetuned reward models. Candidates are
generated in parallel and judged in one listwise call: experts
prefer this to sequential refinement in 78.5% of decided com-
parisons, at no more than half the judge calls. EditAgent
improves two open-weight editors on three benchmarks and
serves as a data engine for training stronger editors.

1. Introduction
Instruction-based image editing models can now perform a
wide range of edits from a single text instruction [4, 25, 63].
Real requests, however, rarely ask for a single edit. Given
“move me to the left of the frame, widen the shot to show
more of the beach, remove the people walking behind me,
and make it look like sunset,” today’s editors tend to com-
plete some parts and silently drop others [57, 67]. Agentic
editing systems [15, 30, 49, 75] address this by decomposing
the request into steps and running a plan-edit-judge loop on
each. Yet when a step fails, they mostly try again. The step is
refined, retried, or rolled back with the same tools and judge.
Here we ask the question: when an editing agent fails, is
another attempt really what it needs? If not, what does
the agent really needs?
Consider how a professional retoucher would handle the
same request: pick a different tool for each part, and check
each result by zooming in and comparing before and after. A
retoucher does not simply try again but acts differently and
looks more closely. We argue that an editing agent is likewise

bounded by two spaces: its action space, what it can do,
and its judge’s observation space, what it can see. Existing
agents [15, 30, 49] keep both small. Each attempt runs one
tool, often a general editor [14, 25, 27, 43, 63], and the
judge scores that single output by eye, given only the source
image, the edited image and the instruction. To explore how
each space should be designed, we introduce a diagnostic
benchmark of 600 candidate sets over 30 edit types, labeled
blindly by professional Photoshop editors (Fig. 2). We find
that the general editor yields no usable result in 42.7% of
the sets, mostly in geometry, tone and enhancement edits,
and a specialized method recovers 175 of these 256 failures.
Meanwhile, no judge we test, including finetuned reward
models [35, 65], is the most accurate across edit groups.
We therefore rethink agentic editing by expanding both
spaces. Our system, EditAgent, shown in Fig. 1, expands
the action space by routing each step to general-purpose edi-
tors and specialized methods, including masked local editing,
object removal and movement, photometric and geometric
operators, and so on. It expands the observation space by
attaching to each candidate a verification record (evidence),
such as a change map, close-ups of the edited region, and
measured color and geometry, together with the outcomes of
programmatic checks, so that the judge knows what code has
verified and what remains for visual judgment. Instead of re-
fining one result round after round, EditAgent thus generates
candidates from all selected methods in parallel from the
same source image, and the judge compares them with their
records in a single listwise call. Unlike in text-to-image gen-
eration [17], professional editors prefer this parallel search
over sequential refinement in 78.5% of non-tied compar-
isons, with at most half the judge calls (Sec. 3.3). With
verification records, off-the-shelf VLM judges outperform
finetuned reward models without finetuning.
Instantiated with Qwen-Image-Edit-2511 [63] and
FLUX.2-klein-9B [26], EditAgent improves both backbones
on EdiVal [6], Complex-Edit [67], and ComplexBench-
Edit [57]. Because it accepts only verified edits, EditAgent
also serves as a data engine. Editors finetuned on its 25.7K
accepted sessions outperform those trained on data generated
directly by the base editor FLUX.2-klein-9B.
Our contributions are threefold.
• We identify the agent’s action space and its judge’s
observation space as two bottlenecks of agentic im-
age editing, and analyze both with an expert-annotated
diagnostic benchmark spanning 30 edit types.
• We propose EditAgent, which expands the action space
with specialized editing methods and the observation
space with verification records, and couples them
through parallel candidate generation and listwise judg-
ing. With these records, off-the-shelf VLM judges
outperform finetuned editing reward models.
• EditAgent consistently improves strong open-weight
editors on three multi-turn and complex-instruction
benchmarks, and the sessions it accepts yield training
data that further improves standalone editors.
2. Related Work
Single-turn Image Editing. Following pioneering diffusion-
based methods [16, 19, 37, 39], InstructPix2Pix [4] and
Emu Edit [50] established general instruction-based editing.
Recent proprietary [14, 43, 48, 60] and open-weight [11, 26,
27, 33, 63, 64] models now provide strong general-purpose
editing. Other methods offer more explicit control through
masked editing [2, 54], localized text-driven editing [9, 29],
or point-based manipulation [51]. EditAgent builds on some
of these editors and coordinates them together.
Multi-turn Editing and Agentic Editing. MagicBrush [76]
provides single- and multi-turn editing trajectories, making
the preservation of earlier edits an explicit target. Agentic
systems further incorporate planning and feedback, for ex-
ample by scheduling restoration tools [81], searching and
revising cost-aware tool paths [15], and guiding iterative
edits with instruction decomposition, visual feedback, or dia-
logue history [7, 28, 30, 36, 49, 62, 68, 75]. Others integrate
reflection into the editing model [73], maintain a structured
history for long-horizon editing [71], or explore editing tra-
jectories with Monte Carlo tree search [70]. These agents
retry within a fixed tool set and judge one output at a time
by eye, whereas EditAgent expands the action space with
specialized editing methods and the observation space of its
judge with evidence and side-by-side comparison.
Evaluation and Benchmarking.
Traditional protocols
measure edit alignment with CLIP-based text-image or di-
rectional similarity [12, 44] and preservation with CLIP
or DINO image similarity [5]. Benchmarks further com-
pare outputs with ground-truth target images [76] or as-
sess unedited regions [18] with PSNR, SSIM [61], and
LPIPS [77]. More recent protocols prompt VLMs such
as GPT-4o [40] and Qwen2.5-VL [3] to rate instruction fol-
lowing, preservation, and perceptual quality [20, 33, 67, 72].
Others augment VLM judgments with visual tools [6, 58]
or aggregate automatic scores into a human-aligned evalu-
ation [47]. Rather than training dedicated editing reward
models [35, 65, 78], we adapt off-the-shelf VLMs with task-
specific verification records.
3. Rethinking Agentic Editing
We evaluate both action and observation spaces on the diag-
nostic benchmark (Sec. 3.1, Fig. 2). Specifically, we address
three core questions: (1) Does a general editor need a larger
action space or only more samples (Sec. 3.2)? (2) Under a
fixed budget, should candidates be refined sequentially or
generated in parallel (Sec. 3.3)? (3) Can a judge that scores
one output at a time select a satisfactory edit (Sec. 3.4)?

3.1. Diagnostic Benchmark
We construct a benchmark of 600 editing sets covering 30
fine-grained edit types, with 20 sets per type. The types span
six groups: Content, Style & Effects, Scene, Photometric,
Geometry, and Enhancement. Each set contains a source
image, an instruction, and 2-4 candidate edits from two
sources: samples of a general-purpose editor (FLUX.2-klein-
9B) and specialized methods such as masked local editing,
object removal, and photometric operators. Five professional
Photoshop retouchers sit together and select every usable
candidate, i.e., every candidate that completes the instruction
acceptably. We call the selected candidates the winner set.
3.2. Is One Editor Enough?
We categorize the 600 sets by their winner sets (Fig. 2a). The
winner set contains only general-editor candidates in 254
sets (42.3%), only specialized candidates in 175 (29.2%),
and both kinds in 90 (15.0%). In the remaining 81 sets
(13.5%), no candidate is usable. Since every set includes
a general-editor candidate, the general editor fails in 256
sets (42.7%), and a specialized method recovers 175 of them.
The outcomes also vary strongly across edit groups. The
winner set contains only general-editor candidates in 73.8%
of Style & Effects sets, but only specialized candidates in
72.5% of Enhancement and 66.7% of Geometry sets.
More samples from the general editor do not recover
these failures. For every request, the general editor produces
several samples and one of them is delivered, i.e., parallel
sampling with a verifier [17]. A second pool also includes
the specialized methods, and the same VLM judge selects the
delivered edit in both pools. Both delivered edits are among
the labeled candidates of the set. Although the general-editor
pool contains more samples, its delivered edit is usable in
only 45.2% of the sets, compared with 68.2% for the pool
with specialized methods. In the 296 sets where exactly
one delivered edit is usable, it comes from the pool with
specialized methods in 217. These results show that one
editor is not enough, even when sampled more often.
3.3. Refine Sequentially or Search in Parallel?
While sequential refinement has been shown to improve text-
image alignment in image generation [17], we observe that it
can suffer from error accumulation in image editing, as errors
introduced in earlier iterations may propagate to later edits.
To investigate this, we compare two strategies using the same
editor (FLUX.2-klein-9B) and judge (Qwen3.8-27B), each
with a budget of 4 image generations. Sequential refinement
iteratively edits the previous result using prompts revised
based on VLM feedback, requiring 3 feedback calls and 1
final selection call. Parallel search, in contrast, generates two
candidates independently from the original image. If neither
is accepted by the judge, it revises the prompt and generates
two more candidates from the original image before selecting
the best result. This requires at most 2 VLM calls.
In a blind comparison on 144 Complex-Edit samples [67]
at complexity levels 1, 4, and 8, five professional Photoshop
editors preferred parallel search in 102 cases and sequential
refinement in 28, with 14 ties. Parallel search thus wins
78.5% of the non-tied comparisons with at most half the
VLM calls. Unlike generation, editing must stay faithful to
the source image. Sequential refinement keeps editing its
own output, so small errors can accumulate, whereas parallel
search edits every candidate directly from the source. Thus,
for editing, parallel search uses the budget better than
sequential refinement. With several candidates available,
the delivered edit then depends on what the judge can see.
3.4. Is Judging One Output at a Time Enough?
We therefore test four judges, each scores one output at a
time from the source image, the edited image and, except
for CLIP-I, the instruction. They are the editing reward
models EditScore-72B [35] and EditReward-7B [65], the
CLIP-I metric [44] (CLIP similarity to the source image),
and Qwen3.8-27B [56] with a pass/fail prompt. Winner-Set
Hit@1 is the fraction of sets whose top-scoring candidate is
in the winner set, i.e., is usable, with ties sharing credit. We
report it on the 515 sets with at least one usable candidate.

Is preference finetuning the remedy? The EditScore [35]
models are trained on large sets of detailed human prefer-

EditScore on its own EditReward-Bench [35] (Tab. 1). Ed-

each candidate alone (pointwise), off-the-shelf VLMs can
reach 71.7-73.8%. Furthermore, if we expand the observa-

call (listwise), the accuracy can be boosted to 85.2-86.1%.
Such comparative judging is not supported by EditScore
models, which are finetuned with specific input and out-
put templates. Thus, what a judge sees can matter more
than how it is trained. Here, comparative judging enables
expanded observation space, leading to better judgments.
4. EditAgent
Given a user request, a planner decomposes it into ordered
editing steps (Fig. 3). For each step, an analyzer specifies
the editing operation and its target, a grounder localizes
the target with a VLM and SAM 2.1 [46] when a method
needs a mask, and a router selects the applicable editing
methods, each of which generates at least one candidate in
parallel (Sec. 4.1). An off-the-shelf VLM judge evaluates the
candidates together with their verification records (Sec. 4.2),
either accepting one candidate or returning feedback for a
retry. Each accepted image becomes the source of the next
step, and the requirements it satisfies are carried forward so
that subsequent turns can verify that they still hold (Sec. 4.3).

4.1. Expanding the Action Space
Candidate Construction. Given an executable instruction
and its source image, the analyzer specifies the editing op-
eration and recommends candidate methods, and the router
turns the recommendation into a set of methods that each
contribute at least one candidate. Different methods explore
different realizations of the request, while repeated sampling
explores variation within a method.
Action Space. Besides the general editor, the capability
pool contains: 1) masked local editing and recoloring, which
run the general editor inside a SAM 2.1 mask, 2) object re-
moval with OSOR [80], 3) object movement with a dedicated
model, 4) subject scaling by segmentation and compositing,
5) global and local photometric operators, 6) style filters
and neural style transfer, 7) flips, rotations, and rotation to
level, and 8) zoom-in by cropping with super-resolution and
zoom-out by outpainting. Details can be found in Tab. S5.
4.2. Expanding the Observation Space
Comparative Judging. Inspired by Sec. 3.4, we provide the
judge with all candidates in one call. It only needs one VLM
call per step, whose comparison yields feedback for the next
attempt if a retry is needed.

different tools to judge an editing operation, we argue that
different edits require checks of different properties (Fig. 4).

target change and the preservation of unrelated content,

4.3. Planning and Preserving Edits across Turns
The planner splits a request into dependent steps when its
parts need separate execution, and each accepted step be-
comes the source of the next. Because the step judge sees
one step at a time, a later edit can undo an earlier one un-
noticed. After every turn, a history check therefore shows
the VLM the original image, the results of earlier turns and
the current image, and asks whether each earlier request still
holds. On a confident violation, EditAgent runs one repair
turn and adopts it only if its step judge accepts it (Fig. 6).
5. Experiments
5.1. Experimental Setup
Benchmarks.
We evaluate EditAgent on three bench-
marks covering multi-turn and complex-instruction edit-
ing. EdiVal [6] comprises 572 three-turn sessions and as-
sesses editing quality as successive requests accumulate.
Complex-Edit [67] comprises 8,496 real and synthetic ex-
amples spanning different levels of instruction complexity.
ComplexBench-Edit [57] comprises 763 examples with com-
positional instructions, including parallel constraints and
multi-step chains. Together, they assess performance across
editing turns, complexity, and compositional structure.
Backbones and Baselines. We instantiate EditAgent with
two general-purpose editors, Qwen-Image-Edit-2511 [63]
and FLUX.2-klein-9B [26]. For each backbone, we compare
standalone editing with its integration into EditAgent. We
also compare it with seven agentic editors (Tab. 5). On
EdiVal, both backbones edit at the 512 px input resolution.
Because most closed-source editors generate at 1024 px or
more, we also run Qwen-Image-Edit-2511 at 1024 px and
resize back to 512 px for fair comparison. The comparison
with agentic editors and all ablations use the 512 px setting.
Evaluation.
We follow the evaluation rubrics of each
benchmark. EdiVal uses its released judges, and Qwen3.8-
27B [56] replaces GPT-4o and Gemini 2.0 Flash Thinking
on Complex-Edit and ComplexBench-Edit. As it is also
our internal judge, to avoid potential bias, we additionally
evaluate EditAgent using Gemma-4-31B [55] (Tab. S8).
Implementation Details. Qwen3.8-27B serves as the plan-
ner, analyzer, grounder and judge, and SAM 2.1 [46] turns
predicted boxes into masks. Each attempt generates up to
three candidates, at least one per applicable method, shown
to the judge in random order. Each step allows two retries
and each turn two plan revisions.

Multi-turn Editing. Without finetuning, EditAgent im-

5.3. EditAgent as a Data Engine
Because EditAgent flags every edit that its judge did not
accept, its fully accepted sessions can serve as training data.
Using FLUX.2-klein-9B as the general editor, EditAgent
produces about 25.7K sessions, averaging four turns each.
Each session yields one training sample consisting of the
source image, a session-level instruction, and the final edited
image. For comparison, we use FLUX.2-klein-9B to directly
edit the same sessions, keeping the training inputs identical
and varying only the final images. We fully finetune Qwen-
Image-Edit-2511 and FLUX.2-klein-base-9B on each dataset
for one epoch and evaluate them as standalone editors.
As shown in Tab. 6, training on EditAgent data improves
both editors across all three benchmarks. On EdiVal-O
at turn 3, Qwen-Image-Edit-2511 and FLUX.2-klein-base-
9B gain 1.95 and 2.00 points, respectively, while their
ComplexBench-Edit averages increase by 3.17 and 2.14
points. More importantly, EditAgent data consistently out-
performs directly generated FLUX.2-klein-9B data under the
same training recipe. Notably, direct FLUX.2-klein-9B data
degrades FLUX.2-klein-base-9B on all three benchmarks,
whereas EditAgent data improves it. These results suggest
that EditAgent provides higher-quality training supervision
rather than merely additional training examples.
5.4. Ablation Study
Tab. 7 evaluates each component of EditAgent. The vanilla
agent, equipped with planning, analysis, history checking,
and repair/retry, improves EdiVal-O at turn 3 from 47.64 to
49.43. Expanding the action space yields a 2.94-point gain.
Sampling the general editor more often does not reproduce
this gain. If we replace the action space with K copies of
the general editor and keep parallel comparison, EdiVal-O
reaches 47.53, 48.90, and 49.01 for K = 2, 4, and 8. This
agrees with the diagnostic study in Sec. 3.2, where restricting
EditAgent to the general editor lowers the share of usable
delivered edits. Expanding the observation space further
improves performance by 3.09 points.

6. Conclusion
We presented EditAgent, which rethinks agentic image edit-
ing as expanding two spaces: the action space of the agent
and the observation space of its judge. It routes each step to
general-purpose and specialized editing methods, generates
their candidates in parallel, and lets the off-the-shelf VLM
judge compare them with verification records. EditAgent
improves two editing backbones across three benchmarks,
and functions as a robust data engine, leveraging its accepted
sessions to improve standalone editors through fine-tuning.
Limitations. EditAgent incurs roughly 10× the inference
time of standalone editing models, and its performance is
inherently bounded by the capabilities of its underlying edit-
ing methods. Nevertheless, EditAgent can serve as a data
engine for finetuning stronger and more efficient standalone
editors, potentially amortizing its computational cost.

A. Diagnostic Benchmark
Requests. The benchmark covers 30 edit types in six groups,
with 20 requests per type (Tab. S1). Source images are drawn
from COCO val2017 [31], and each image is used in one
set only. A VLM (Qwen3.8-27B) screens each photograph
so that every edit type is assigned only to photographs that
support it, and then writes one natural instruction of the
assigned type, following the type’s definition in Tab. S1.
Requests are written before any candidate exists.
Candidates. Candidates come from two runs of EditAgent
on each request, one with the full capability pool and one
restricted to the general editor. Both runs use FLUX.2-klein-
9B as the general editor and Qwen3.8-27B in all VLM roles.
All distinct images generated in these runs form the pool of
a set, from which we keep up to four, giving priority to the
delivered results and to methods not yet represented. This
yields 2 to 4 candidates per set and 1,995 candidates in total.
Annotation. Five professional Photoshop editors label each
set together in a shared interface and agree on one label,
so each set has one consensus label. The interface shows
the source image, the instruction and the candidates under
neutral keys (C1 to C4), in an order fixed by a hash and
without any indication of their method. For each set, the
editors select every usable candidate, i.e., every candidate
that completes the instruction acceptably. A usable candidate
is selected even when another candidate is better, so an
unselected candidate is unusable, not merely second best.
The editors can also mark the set as all usable or all unusable.
Of the 600 sets, 248 have one selected candidate, 155 have
several, 116 are marked as all usable and 81 as all unusable.
Winner-Set Hit@1. The winner set of a set contains its
selected candidates, or all candidates when the set is marked
all usable. It is thus the set of usable candidates, and Winner-
Set Hit@1 measures how often a judge’s top choice is usable.
We exclude the 81 sets without a usable candidate. Of the
remaining 519, we also exclude 4 in which one candidate has
no verification record, so that every judge is compared on
the same 515 sets. A judge that scores candidates separately
selects its top-scoring candidate. When several candidates
share the top score, the tie is broken uniformly at random,
and we count the expected outcome, i.e., the fraction of the
tied candidates that are in the winner set. An invalid output,
or a missing score for any candidate of the set, counts as 0.
Accuracy is computed per run over the fixed set of sets and
averaged over three runs. The random baseline is the mean
over sets of the winner-set size divided by the number of
candidates. It is 59.32% on the 515 sets and 47.10% on the
396 sets with at least one unusable candidate.

B. Refine Sequentially or Search in Parallel
Requests. We sample 48 requests at each of the complexity
levels 1, 4 and 8 from the real-image split of Complex-
Edit [67], each with its own source image. A request at level
k chains k atomic instructions, so the three levels range from
a single edit to a long compound request.
Policies. Both policies use FLUX.2-klein-9B, Qwen3.8-27B
as the VLM, and at most four images per request. Sequential
refinement generates the first image from the source with the
original instruction. After each of the first three images, it
asks the VLM for a corrective request for the latest image
and applies it to that image, and a final selection call picks
among the four images (3 feedback calls and 1 selection call).
Parallel search generates two images from the source with
the original instruction. If the VLM accepts neither, it re-
vises the instruction and generates two more images from the
source before a final selection, using at most two VLM calls.
The VLM sees the source, the candidate images and their
difference maps, in a shared random order. Sequential re-
finement re-edits the latest image, as iterative editing agents
commonly do, whereas the retries of EditAgent re-edit the
source of the step with a revised instruction (Sec. D).
Human Study. Five professional Photoshop editors view
the final images of the policies blindly, next to the source
and the request, in random positions and without labels,
and assess instruction following, preservation of unrelated
content, and artifacts. For each comparison of parallel search
with sequential refinement, the editors either choose the
better policy or mark the two as equal. Parallel search is

C. Observation Space
Protocol. The judges of Sec. 3.4 are the reward models
with their released inference and Qwen3.8-27B with the
pointwise judge, ranked by the probability of its pass verdict.
They score each candidate separately, and tied top-scoring
candidates share the credit. The listwise judge uses the
instructions of Listing 6 and receives the request, the source
image and all candidates of a set in one call. The pointwise
judge uses the same instructions for a single candidate. The
candidate order of each set is fixed across conditions and
models and shifts cyclically by one position per run.
Records in These Studies. The studies use the verification
records of Sec. F (Tab. S6), computed for the labeled can-
didates of each set. For a localized request, the region that
the request names is localized once in the source image, and
every candidate gets a close-up of this region.
Models and Inference. Figure 5 lists the VLMs and reward
models. Each VLM is served with vLLM [22] from its
released checkpoint and judges every set three times, with
thinking enabled and the sampling settings of its generation
configuration. A call without a valid answer counts as a
miss. EditScore uses its released inference over three runs.
EditReward-7B uses its released deterministic scorer in one
run. In the benchmark experiments, every VLM role uses
the settings of Tab. S3.
Metrics. CLIP-I [44], used in Sec. 3.4 of the main paper,
is the cosine similarity between the CLIP ViT-L/14 image
embeddings of the source and the candidate.
What Records Contribute. Records improve each of the
seven VLMs of Fig. 5, by 6.3 to 9.3 points, and raise their
mean Winner-Set Hit@1 from 71.17% to 79.25%. On the
396 sets with at least one unusable candidate, the mean
rises from 62.60% to 73.06%, against 47.10% for a random
choice. The gain is largest on Enhancement, Photometric
and Scene edits (40.7, 20.5 and 9.5 points on average).

D. Agentic Workflow Details
This section details the control flow of EditAgent summa-
rized in Secs. 4 and 4.3 of the main paper. It gives the roles
and what each receives (Tab. S2), the turn and step loops
(Algorithms 1 and 2) and the default settings (Tab. S3).
State. EditAgent stores each session as a version graph.
Every node holds one image state together with its parent, the
step that produced it, its candidates, the judge’s report, and
whether it needs review. Accepted states are never modified.
A later step adds a child node, and a user correction branches
from the parent, so every accepted state remains available.
The requirements that an accepted state satisfies, such as the
requested change and the regions that had to stay, are carried
into the prompts of the planner, the analyzer and the history
check in later steps and turns.
Turn. Algorithm 1 runs one user turn. The planner decides
the number and order of the steps (Listing 1), and each step
starts from the state accepted by the previous step. When a
step uses up its attempts, the re-planner either replaces the
failed and pending steps or keeps the best attempt, which is
then marked as needing review. After the turn, the history
check verifies every earlier turn against the current image.
On a confident violation, EditAgent runs one repair turn and
keeps its result only if the judge accepts it.
Step. Algorithm 2 runs one step. The analyzer rewrites
the step into an explicit instruction for a mask-free editor,
stating both the requested change and what must remain,
sets the parameters of the applicable methods, and suggests
how many candidates the step needs. The router selects the
methods (Tab. S4), each of which contributes at least one
candidate. Every candidate receives a verification record,

G. Benchmark Experiments
Backbones. Each backbone uses the same checkpoint and
sampler with and without EditAgent. Qwen-Image-Edit-
2511 runs 40 steps with CFG 4 in BF16, and FLUX.2-klein-
9B uses its distilled 4-step sampler. Within EditAgent, the
backbone is the general editor. It therefore also runs inside
the masked methods, the refinement and harmonization of
Subject Scaling, the Multi-Reference Editor and the fill of
Canvas Extension, which all call the general editor. OSOR,
FLUX.1-Fill for the rotation ring and the outpainting fall-
back, Swin2SR, neural style transfer and the closed-form
operators are the same for all backbones. The prompts are
used unchanged with every backbone.
Inputs. EditAgent and the standalone backbones receive
the same original benchmark instructions, EditAgent plans
internally, and all scoring uses the original benchmark anno-
tations. Standalone backbones use seed 42, and seeds 42, 43
and 44 for the three EdiVal turns.
Scoring. EdiVal is scored with its released code and its re-
leased evaluators GroundingDINO [32], Qwen2-VL-7B [59]
and DINOv3 [52], all unchanged. Its instruction following is
cumulative, so a session passes turn t only if the instruction
of every turn up to t is fulfilled by the output of that turn.
EdiVal-O is the geometric mean of instruction following and
content consistency. On Complex-Edit [67], Qwen3.8-27B
replaces GPT-4o in the official prompts. Instruction follow-
ing and identity preservation are rated from the source and
the output with the official rubric, and perceptual quality
from the output alone, each averaged over five samples on a
scale from 0 to 10. The overall score is the mean of the three
dimensions. The 8,496 requests comprise 531 instruction
chains in each of the real and synthetic splits, at eight com-
plexity levels. On ComplexBench-Edit [57], Qwen3.8-27B
replaces Gemini 2.0 Flash Thinking and rates each atomic
instruction from the source and the output with the official
rubric of its edit type. With atomic scores a, b and c in
[0, 1], parallel requests score (a + b + c)/3, two-step chains
(ab + c)/2 and three-step chains abc, reported ×100. The
reported average is the mean over its five groups of requests
in Tab. 4, from single-object edits to three-step chains.
Agentic Baselines. Table S7 lists how each agent of Tab. 5
is run. All agents edit through one service that runs the
standalone backbone with its recipe, so they differ only in
their policy. A masked or inpainting tool becomes a back-
bone edit composited inside a SAM 2.1 mask. Each agent
receives the original EdiVal instruction of the current turn
and edits its own previous output. Attempt a of turn t uses
seed 42+100a+t−1, so a first attempt uses the seed of the
standalone backbone. The standalone rows of Tabs. 3 and 5
come from the same service, so all agents are compared
against one standalone result of each backbone.

Generation and Filtering. EditAgent executes each session
turn by turn with the settings of Tab. S3 and FLUX.2-klein-
9B as the general editor. Qwen3.8-27B serves as planner,
analyzer, grounder and judge. Of the 40,726 executed ses-
sions (163,806 turns), the judge accepts 91.4% of the turns
(Tab. S10). Because a session is kept only if the judge ac-
cepts every one of its turns, 28,673 sessions (70.4%) pass
this filter, and no kept session contains a result marked as
needing review. After we also remove the 591 sessions that
are both in the lowest 15% of quality and average more than
two attempts per turn, the remaining 28,082 sessions, 69.0%
of those executed, contain 111,755 turns.
Compute. Generation runs on nodes of 8 H200 GPUs. With
speculative decoding (DFlash2 [1]), the 40,726 sessions take
about 400 GPU-hours. Every 10,000 sessions take about 100
GPU-hours, and about 70% of them are kept as high quality.
Training Pairs. Each kept session gives one training pair,
made of the source image, one instruction for the whole
session and the final image. A pair ends before the first turn
that changes the image size and needs at least two applied
edits. This gives 26,251 pairs with 3.9 edits per pair on
average. Qwen3.8-27B composes the instruction from those
of the delivered steps, resolving the final intent by dropping
overridden or cancelled edits. The pairs are split by source
image into 25,753 training and 498 validation pairs. The
same pairs train the students for all three benchmarks.
Baseline Data. For the matched baseline, FLUX.2-klein-
9B edits every turn of the same sessions directly with the
original per-turn instructions, each turn on its own previous
output, without planning or judging. Its pairs use the same
kept sessions, split and instructions as the EditAgent pairs,
with the final image taken from the FLUX.2-klein-9B chain,
so the two datasets differ only in their images.
Finetuning. We finetune all transformer weights of FLUX.2-
klein-base-9B (9.1B parameters) and Qwen-Image-Edit-
2511 (20.4B), with the text encoder and VAE frozen, us-
ing DiffSynth-Studio [38] with DeepSpeed ZeRO-3 [45] in
BF16 on 8 H200 GPUs. Training uses a global batch of 8
(one per GPU), AdamW [34] with a learning rate of 10−5 for
FLUX.2-klein-base-9B and 2 × 10−6 for Qwen-Image-Edit-
2511 (after a five-update warm-up at one third of the rate),
weight decay of 0.01 and 0.05, respectively, gradient clip-
ping at 1.0, gradient checkpointing, images of up to 10242
px at their native aspect ratio, and a fixed data order. Each
model is trained for one epoch (3,220 steps). For evaluation,
FLUX.2-klein-base-9B is sampled with 50 steps and CFG
4, and Qwen-Image-Edit-2511 with 40 steps and CFG 4.
On EdiVal, the students and their untuned baselines receive
rewritten instructions (Tab. 6).

Planner.
Listing 1. Planner instructions (system), one call per turn.
You are the planner of a photo−editing agent. You receive
the user's new instruction, the edit history and
carried_constraints, the requirements of earlier accepted
edits that must still hold. Turn the instruction into an
ordered list of editing steps.
⟨shared rules of Listing 2⟩
Steps. Give an edit its own step when it needs a
different operation or method from the other edits, or
when it could disturb another edit. Otherwise use the
fewest steps, each summarized as one concrete action.
Order. Keep the written order unless another order is
clearly more robust. As a rule, edits of the scene's
structure (composition, objects, geometry) precede edits
of its appearance (lighting, color, material), and an
edit of the whole frame follows the local edits, so that
no edit is overwritten by a later one. Never reorder past
a dependency: an edit that acts on, refers to, or applies
on top of the result of another edit comes after it.
State in notes whether and why you reordered.
Output only a JSON object with this schema:
{
"goal": "<at most 12 words>",
"steps": [

"op": "<operation>",
"summary": "<one action, at most 10 words>",
"target_desc": "<referring expression or ''>",
"protect_desc": ["<what must stay unchanged>"],
"executor": "masked_composite|edit_model",
"derived_constraints": ["<new requirement>"],
"params": {}
}
],
"notes": "<optional>"
}
op is one of recolor, restyle, remove, replace, add,
move, scale_subject, relight, harmonize, filter, zoom_in,
zoom_out, camera_view, flip, rotate and generic.
target_desc names the target with the words of the
instruction, including the attributes or relations that
single out one instance, and is '' for a global edit. The
analyzer, which sees the image, refines it.

Listing 2. Rules shared by the planner, analyzer and re-planner,
covering the executor, the operations and their parameters, the
editing methods of the capability pool and preservation.
Executor. Recommend one executor per step:
−"masked_composite": for an edit confined to a nameable
region or subject, namely recolor, a localized restyle
and scale_subject. The target is segmented, the general
editor edits it, and every pixel outside the target is
restored from the source image. Set target_desc to a
concise referring expression for the target.
−"edit_model": the mask−free general editor, for edits
without a localizable target and for remove, replace and
add, which need freedom beyond the target's mask.
Whichever executor you recommend, the router adds the
specialized methods that serve the operation (Table S4),
and every masked method competes with the general editor,
so the judge can fall back on it when a mask is imperfect.
Operations and parameters.
−recolor, restyle: change the color, or the material and
appearance, of a target.
−remove: delete an object with what belongs to it,
including its shadow and reflection, and fill in what
lies behind it.
−replace, add: swap an object for another, or insert one.
−move: relocate an object by the signed fractions of the
frame in params.dx_frac and params.dy_frac.
−scale_subject: resize a person or object by
params.scale_factor (above 1 enlarges).
−relight: a change of tone or lighting. When a global
adjustment can express it, quantify brightness, contrast,
saturation and color temperature in params. A change of
the light itself, such as its source, direction or time
of day, is a semantic edit for the general editor.
−harmonize: match an inserted or edited element to the
lighting and color of the scene.
−filter: a global photographic look, tone grade, drawing
style or artistic style, with params.filter_name or
params.artist_style when the look is in the catalog.
−zoom_in, zoom_out: move the camera closer or widen the
view by params.zoom_factor (above 1), centered on
target_desc or extended toward the side in params.extend.
−camera_view: show the scene from another viewpoint.
−flip, rotate: mirror the image (params.direction), or
rotate it by params.degrees (positive is clockwise) or
toward a stated orientation, with
params.rotate_mode=align and params.align_target.
−generic: any other edit.
Methods. The operation, executor, target_desc and params
of a step are your recommendation of editing methods. The
router turns them into the applicable methods of the
capability pool, each contributing at least one candidate.

Analyzer.
Listing 3. Analyzer instructions (system). The analyzer turns a
planned step into an executable instruction with acceptance criteria
and recommends the methods for its candidates.
You are the analyzer of a photo−editing agent. You
receive one planned step, the source image of the step,
carried_constraints, the reports of the steps already
done in this turn and, on a retry, the judge's notes on
the earlier attempts. Turn the step into an executable
package for the editing methods and the judge.
⟨shared rules of Listing 2⟩
Instruction. The general editor is mask−free and reads
only the instruction and the image. State precisely what
to change and what to keep, with one edit per
instruction, and ground it in what you see, such as the
target's location, the scene's lighting and its scale, so
that the result fits the scene. The instruction asks for
exactly the user's edit, with the same scope and no added
requirement, and an unchanged image cannot satisfy it.
Acceptance criteria. Write at most 4 conditions that a
correct result meets. Each follows from the user's
instruction, carried_constraints or the preservation
rule, and each can be checked from the images or the
verification record. The judge applies them as written.
Target. Leave target_desc empty when the step adjusts the
whole frame. Otherwise refine the planner's target_desc
until it singles out the intended instance in the image.
Candidates. Set candidate_count from 1 to 3 according to
how uncertain the outcome is, with one for a
deterministic operation and more for a semantic edit.
Retries. When the judge's notes on earlier attempts are
given, revise the wording, the operation or the
parameters to address what the notes report, keep the
requested scope, and never repeat a failed attempt.
Output only a JSON object:
{

"instruction": "<what changes and what stays>",
"executor": "masked_composite|edit_model",
"op": "<operation>",
"target_desc": "<referring expression or ''>",
"protect_desc": ["<what must stay unchanged>"],
"acceptance_criteria": ["<checkable condition>"],
"params": {},
"candidate_count": 2

Listing 5. Mask review, with the original image, the image with a

extended with the named missing parts or replaced by the corrected

You verify a segmentation mask before an image edit. You
receive the original image, a copy with a thin cyan
outline of the mask, and the binary mask (white =

Edit instruction: ⟨edit instruction⟩
Target: ⟨referring expression⟩

Judge.
Listing 6. Judge instructions (system) for one step. The candidates
follow in a shuffled order, each with its verification record.
You are an exacting judge of image edits. You receive the
BEFORE image, the requested edit with its acceptance
criteria and the regions to keep, and several candidate
edited images, TRIAL 1, TRIAL 2 and so on, each with its
verification record. Compare all trials in one decision.
Judge each trial against BEFORE as a whole, and use the
differences between trials to see what each one changed.
Consider all of the following together:
−Instruction following: the requested change visibly
happened, is correct and complete, and meets the
acceptance criteria. A clean image without the edit is a
failure, and a change that you cannot see earns no credit.
−Preservation: everything outside the requested scope
stays intact, in particular the regions to keep.
−Visual quality: the result is one coherent photograph,
without seams, halos, distortion or other artifacts.
Verification records. Code computes each record from the
16


pixels of BEFORE and the trial, in the same way for every
trial, and the record names no method. It contains:
−The share of pixels that differ visibly from BEFORE.
−A CHANGE−MAP, where brighter means a larger change on a
scale shared by all trials. It locates change but is not
a photograph, so never judge color or realism from it.
−For a localized request, a CLOSE−UP, BEFORE | AFTER of
the region that the request names, at the same place and
scale for every trial.
−Measurements chosen for the request, with definitions.
−VERIFIED: properties that code has checked for this
trial. Treat each as established within its stated scope.
−TO JUDGE: properties that code cannot decide. Judge
them from the photographs.
The request, not the amount of change, decides what is
right, so favor neither more nor less change. Preserved
surroundings do not make up for an incomplete or
implausible edit, and changes that the request implies
for the scene to stay consistent are not defects.
Give every trial:
−note: one or two sentences citing the visible evidence.
For a trial that does not pass, the note says what is
wrong, so that the next attempt can fix it.
−pass: "yes" when the edit can be delivered as it is,
with the requested change done, the regions to keep
preserved and no material artifact, and "no" otherwise.
−score: one integer for the overall quality of the edit,
using the whole range from 1 (the edit is absent or the
image is ruined) to 10 (complete, faithful and clean).
Return exactly one JSON object with 1−based trial
numbers. ranking lists every trial from best to worst and
starts with best_trial, and every passing trial ranks
above every failing one. If no trial passes, best_trial
is the least bad one.
{
"trials": [
{"trial": 1, "note": "<visible evidence>",
"pass": "yes|no", "score": 1}
],
"best_trial": 1,
"ranking": [<trial numbers, best first>]
}
Re-Planner.
Listing 7. Re-planner instructions (system), used when every at-
tempt of a step has failed, to plan the rest of the turn.
You are the planner of a photo−editing agent, called
again in the middle of a turn because the judge passed no
candidate of one step within its attempts. You receive
the user's instruction for the turn, the steps already
done with their reports, the failed step with the judge's
notes and scores on all its attempts, the pending steps
and carried_constraints. Plan the rest of the turn.
⟨shared rules of Listing 2⟩
Infer from the judge's notes why the step failed, and do
not repeat a strategy that has already failed. In this
order of preference, you may:
1. Rethink the failed edit with a different operation,
executor or decomposition, or with materially different
wording. If no attempt came close, favor methods whose
results code can verify over unconstrained generation.
2. Run a pending step first when the failed edit depends
on its result.
3. Merge or split pending steps when that is more robust.
4. Give up on the step (decision "give_up") when the
notes show that every applicable method fails in the same
way. Its best attempt is kept and marked as needing
review, and the remaining steps continue.
The new steps follow the planner's schema with the ids
r1, r2 and so on, and replace the failed step and all
pending steps. Steps already done are not run again.
Output only a JSON object:
{
"decision": "replace_remaining|give_up",
"goal": "<at most 12 words>",
"steps": [<steps in the planner's schema>],
"notes": "<why the new plan should succeed>"
}
History Check.
Listing 8. History-check instructions (system), run after every turn
from turn 2 on to verify all earlier turns.
You check the earlier turns of a multi−turn photo−editing
agent. The step judge accepted each step by comparing it
only with its source image, so a later edit can undo an
earlier one unnoticed, as when "let in more daylight" in
turn 2 clears the glass that turn 1 frosted. Verify that
every earlier request and the requirements it established
still hold in the CURRENT image.
You see the ORIGINAL image, the accepted results of up to
five earlier turns (AFTER TURN 1, AFTER TURN 2, ...), the
CURRENT image, carried_constraints and, for localized
earlier edits, THEN | NOW close−ups of the edited region
from that turn's result and from CURRENT, which are the
primary evidence that a small edit survived.
For each earlier turn i, judge the CURRENT image:
−instruction_ok: whether the change made by turn i, seen
by comparing its result with the state before it, still
shows in CURRENT together with its requirements.
−superseded: true when a later request deliberately
changed the same property, in which case the earlier
request counts as satisfied (instruction_ok=true). A
change that no later request asked for is a violation,
with superseded=false and instruction_ok=false.
−score in [0, 1]: how fully the request still holds.
−confidence in [0, 1]: how sure you are, low when the
evidence is weak.
−evidence: one short sentence on what you see.
consistent is true when every earlier turn is ok, and
violations lists the turns that are not. For violations,
write repair_instruction, one editing instruction that
restores the lost effect in the CURRENT image without
undoing any later edit, such as "Frost the window glass
again and keep the room bright". Set hard_to_judge=true
when you cannot tell, and say why in notes.
Output only a JSON object with 1−based turn numbers:
{
"turns": [{"turn": 1, "instruction_ok": true,
"superseded": false, "score": 0.0,
"confidence": 0.0, "evidence": "<short>"}],
"consistent": true,
"violations": [<turn numbers>],
"repair_instruction": "<'' when consistent>",
"hard_to_judge": false,
"notes": ""
}
