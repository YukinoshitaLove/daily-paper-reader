# Abstract 

Diffusion transformers (DiTs) have become the dominant architecture for high-fidelity image syn-thesis, yet they suffer from slow training con-vergence and high computational cost. Two re-cent lines of work address this from complemen-tary angles: Representation Alignment (REPA) accelerates DiT training by aligning intermedi-ate features with a pretrained external represen-tation model (e.g., DINOv2), while Represen-tation Auto-Encoders (RAE) replace the pixel-space VAE with a compact representation-space tokenizer learned from self-supervised features. However, these approaches are developed and ap-plied in isolation: REPA still relies on a pixel-space VAE for latent diffusion, and RAE does not exploit the alignment signal that accelerates the diffusion backbone. We present UniRA , a unified framework that couples representation alignment and representation auto-encoding into a single objective. UniRA learns a representation-space tokenizer whose output serves simultaneously as the diffusion target and the alignment anchor, so the tokenizer, the diffusion backbone, and the ex-ternal representation model are co-optimized. We give a theoretical result showing that this joint optimization preserves the representation quality of the external model while accelerating diffusion convergence, and we derive a principled weight-ing that balances the reconstruction, alignment, and generation objectives. On ImageNet class-conditional generation at 256 ×256 and 512 ×512 ,UniRA matches or surpasses state-of-the-art DiTs (SD3, REPA, RAE) in FID while reducing train-ing steps by up to 5×. # 1. Introduction 

Diffusion models have become the leading paradigm for high-fidelity image and video generation (Ho et al., 2020; Rombach et al., 2022; Peebles & Xie, 2023; Esser et al., 2024; Brooks et al., 2024). The shift from U-Net backbones to diffusion transformers (DiT) (Peebles & Xie, 2023) has enabled principled scaling, but training these transformers from scratch remains expensive: convergence typically re-quires millions of optimizer steps and large-scale compute. This cost motivates a growing interest in injecting repre-sentation learning signals into the diffusion backbone to accelerate training and improve sample quality. Two complementary lines of work have emerged. First, Representation Alignment (REPA) (Yu et al., 2024) and its variants (Yu et al., 2025a;b) accelerate DiT training by projecting the diffusion backbone’s intermediate features into the embedding space of a pretrained self-supervised representation model (e.g., DINOv2 (Oquab et al., 2024)) and aligning them with a cosine loss. This simple signal can cut training steps several-fold and improve FID. Second, Representation Auto-Encoders (RAE) (Li et al., 2024) and related representation-space tokenizers (Wang et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2024) replace the pixel-space VAE (Kingma & Welling, 2014; Rombach et al., 2022) with a tokenizer whose encoder and decoder operate in a learned represen-tation space, yielding a more compact and semantically meaningful latent for diffusion. These two ideas are developed in isolation: REPA still relies on a pixel-space VAE for latent diffusion, inheriting its reconstruction–perceptual trade-off, while RAE does not exploit the alignment signal that accelerates the backbone. We observe that they are two halves of a single problem: the representation space used for tokenization should also serve as the alignment target, so the tokenizer, the diffusion backbone, and the external representation model are co-optimized rather than trained separately. # 2. Related Work 

Diffusion transformers and latent diffusion. Latent dif-fusion (Rombach et al., 2022) trains diffusion models in the latent space of a VAE (Kingma & Welling, 2014; van den Oord et al., 2017; Razavi et al., 2019) for efficiency. DiT (Peebles & Xie, 2023) replaces the U-Net with a transformer backbone (Vaswani et al., 2017; Dosovitskiy et al., 2021), enabling scalable image synthesis. Rectified-flow variants such as Stable Diffusion 3 (Esser et al., 2024) and flow matching (Lipman et al., 2023) further improve sample quality and training stability. Score-based formulations (Song et al., 2021), EDM (Karras et al., 2022), and classi-fier guidance (Dhariwal & Nichol, 2021; Ho & Salimans, 2022) provide the theoretical and practical grounding. Gen-erative adversarial approaches (Goodfellow et al., 2014; Karras et al., 2019) offer an alternative paradigm. Our work is orthogonal to the choice of sampler and focuses on the representation space and the alignment signal. 

Representation alignment. REPA (Yu et al., 2024) projects DiT intermediate features into the DINOv2 (Oquab et al., 2024) embedding space and aligns them, accelerating training and improving FID. Extensions include REPA-E (Yu et al., 2025a) for efficient alignment and U-REPA (Yu et al., 2025b) for a unified alignment across layers. Related work studies subspace consistency (Bardes et al., 2024) and representation alignment in vision-language models (Wang et al., 2024). Our framework generalizes REPA by making the alignment target the output of a learnable representation tokenizer, co-optimized with the backbone. 

Representation tokenizers and auto-encoders. VQ-VAE (van den Oord et al., 2017; Razavi et al., 2019) in-troduced discrete tokenizers for generation. RAE (Li et al., 2024) proposes continuous representation auto-encoders that replace the pixel VAE with a representation-space tok-enizer, showing improved sample quality and compactness. SRA-2 (Wang et al., 2025) and VA-VAE (Chen et al., 2024) extend this to self-representative and video-audio settings. Our work unifies RAE with REPA: the representation tok-enizer provides both the diffusion latent and the alignment anchor. 

Self-supervised representation learning. Self-supervised methods—contrastive (Chen et al., 2020; He et al., 2020), non-contrastive (Grill et al., 2020; Zbontar et al., 2021), and masked image modeling (He et al., 2022)—learn powerful visual features without labels. Representation learning theory (Bengio et al., 2013) and guillotine regularization (Bordes et al., 2023) provide foundational analysis. DINO (Caron et al., 2021) and DINOv2 (Oquab et al., 2024) provide the representation models used as alignment targets in REPA. Vision-language models (Radford et al., 2021; Zhai et al., 2023) and hierarchical backbones (Liu et al., 2021) offer complementary representations. Topological orthogonality in visual tokenization (Yang et al., 2026a) and unified token spaces (Yang et al., 2026d;c) improve the quality and geometry of learned representations, motivating our use of a well-structured alignment target. 2UniRA: Unified Representation Alignment and Auto-Encoding 

Positioning. Whereas REPA aligns the backbone but keeps a pixel VAE, and RAE replaces the VAE but does not align the backbone, UniRA unifies both: a single represen-tation space serves as the diffusion target and the alignment anchor, jointly optimized. To our knowledge this is the first framework to co-optimize a representation tokenizer, a dif-fusion backbone, and an external representation model in a single objective.

3.4. Joint Objective and Theoretical Guarantee 

The full UniRA objective combines the three losses: 

LUniRA = Lgen + α Lalign + β Lrec , (4) with scalars α, β > 0. We now state our main result. 

Theorem 3.1 (Representation preservation and convergence acceleration) . Let the external representation model Φ have representation quality QΦ, and let the tokenizer E be initial-ized from Φ. Under the UniRA objective with α, β > 0, the representation quality of E satisfies QE ≥ QΦ − O(β−1)

(i.e., the tokenizer does not collapse below the external model by more than a controlled term), and the diffusion backbone converges to a given FID target in O(1 /α ) fewer steps than without alignment. Proof. The representation quality bound follows from the reconstruction term βLrec , which keeps E close to a faithful auto-encoder, and the alignment term αLalign , which keeps the projected backbone features close to E(x); together they prevent the collapse mode where E becomes degenerate (a constant map), since such a collapse would make both Lrec 

and Lalign unbounded. Formally, if E collapses, Lrec grows (no reconstruction) and Lalign saturates at a trivial cosine, violating the joint optimum; hence QE is bounded below. The convergence acceleration follows the REPA argument (Yu et al., 2024): alignment injects a representation-learning gradient into the backbone, steering intermediate features toward a semantic manifold. Standard SGD analysis on a 

μ-strongly-convex local surrogate shows that the effective learning rate along the representation direction scales with 

α, yielding a step reduction of O(1 /α ). Combining the two gives the stated result. Theorem 3.1 formalizes the intuition that coupling align-ment and auto-encoding is mutually reinforcing: the tok-enizer’s quality bounds the alignment target, and the align-ment accelerates the backbone that consumes the tokenizer’s output. 

3.5. Principled Multi-Objective Weighting 

The three objectives compete for capacity. We balance them via a min-norm gradient (MGDA-style) search (Sener & Koltun, 2018) over the gradients of Lgen , Lalign , and Lrec ,finding weights λ ∈ ∆2 that minimize the norm of the combined gradient. This yields a common-descent direction that does not increase any objective to first order.

3.7. Scalability 

UniRA adds only a projector Π and the MGDA solver to a REPA+RAE pipeline, so the overhead is negligible (under 

3% ). The framework is compatible with any DiT variant (Peebles & Xie, 2023; Esser et al., 2024) and any flow-matching or DDPM sampler (Lipman et al., 2023; Karras et al., 2022), and scales to higher resolutions and video (Brooks et al., 2024; Blattmann et al., 2023) without chang-ing the objective. 

# 4. Experiments 

4.1. Setup Datasets and metrics. We evaluate on ImageNet (Zhao et al., 2009) class-conditional generation at 256 ×256 and 

512 ×512 , reporting FID (Heusel et al., 2018), Inception Score, and CLIP score. We also report zero-shot classifica-tion accuracy and linear-probe accuracy to assess represen-tation quality. 

Baselines. We compare against DiT (Peebles & Xie, 2023), Stable Diffusion 3 (SD3) (Esser et al., 2024), REPA (Yu et al., 2024), REPA-E (Yu et al., 2025a), U-REPA (Yu et al., 2025b), RAE (Li et al., 2024), and SRA-2 (Wang et al., 2025). 

4.2. Architecture 

Figure 2 shows the UniRA architecture. The representation tokenizer E maps images to a semantic latent; the DiT backbone denoises this latent; and the projector Π aligns a middle-layer feature of the backbone with the tokenizer output, which serves as both the diffusion target and the alignment anchor.

Table 1 reports the main results. UniRA achieves FID 4.91

Fixing the weight instead of MGDA costs about 0.11 FID and 0.1M steps. The alignment layer matters: layer 16 (a middle layer) is optimal, consistent with REPA’s finding.

# 5. Conclusion 

We presented UniRA, a unified framework that couples representation alignment and representation auto-encoding into a single objective for efficient diffusion generation. A theoretical result shows that joint optimization preserves representation quality while accelerating convergence. Ex-periments confirm that UniRA matches or surpasses REPA, RAE, and SD3 in FID while reducing training steps by up to 5×, and that the unified latent improves representation quality. We believe co-optimizing the tokenizer, backbone, and representation model is a promising direction for effi-cient, high-quality, and semantically grounded generative models.
