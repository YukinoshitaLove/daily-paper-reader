Abstract
Recent advances in image editing have enabled fine-grained and highly controllable
visual modifications. However, achieving similar levels of controllability in video
editing remains an open challenge, particularly for precise local edits, dynamic
temporal effects, and consistent lighting across frames. In this paper, we propose
EDITDISTILL, a framework that distills image-level visual transformations from
edits to guide video editing. Specifically, given a source video and an edit prompt,
we first generate an edited version of the initial video frame using an off-the-shelf
image editing model. We then distill the visual transformation of this original-
edited image pair into a compact representation termed an edit descriptor. This
descriptor is injected into a video diffusion model through our lightweight fusion

Introduction
Recent advances in AI-generated content (AIGC) have led to remarkable progress in both image and
video generation. In particular, modern image editing models [Zhao et al., 2025, Wu et al., 2025a,
Team et al., 2025, Team, 2025, OpenAI, 2025, 2026, G. Deepmind, 2025] have demonstrated strong
capabilities in performing fine-grained and controllable edits, enabling complex local modifications
with high fidelity. However, controllable video editing remains significantly more challenging,
with existing methods suffering from three interrelated limitations. First, fine-grained local editing,
such as modifying object details or attributes, is often unreliable and typically requires explicit
masks [Hu et al., 2025], which are difficult to obtain and maintain across frames. Second, modeling
dynamic background effects, such as rain, snow, or other temporally evolving phenomena, remains
challenging [Wu et al., 2023] largely due to the lack of explicit temporal control signals. A similar
issue was also observed in frame-wise editing methods such as I2VEdit [Ouyang et al., 2024b],
which ignore the denoising process alignment during video editing. Third, existing methods struggle
to jointly edit foreground and background in a consistent and controllable manner, often leading
to inconsistent results [Jiang et al., 2025]. Taken together, these limitations suggest that directly
controlling video editing in the video domain is inherently difficult. In contrast, we observe that such
complex edits are much easier to achieve in the image domain using modern image editing models.
We hypothesize that a single edited reference frame can encode the editing intent, including the visual
signature of dynamic effects, while the video diffusion prior supplies the temporal unfolding. This
motivates us to ask:
Can we leverage the strong controllability of modern image editing models, and distill this
capability to guide fine-grained and temporally consistent video editing?
To this end, we propose EDITDISTILL, a two-stage framework that distills image-level visual
transformations from edits into a compact edit descriptor and injects it into video diffusion model
for controllable video editing. In the first stage, we utilize powerful image editing models (e.g.,
Qwen-Image [Wu et al., 2025a] or GPT-Image-2 [OpenAI, 2026]) to generate an edited version of the
first frame of a given video, based on an edit prompt. Given the original image and the edited image as
a pair, we learn a compact edit descriptor that captures the transformation between them. Specifically,
this transformation is encoded into a 1024-dimensional latent feature, which serves as guidance for
the subsequent video editing process. In the second stage, we inject the edit descriptor into a video
diffusion model (Wan 2.1 backbone [Wang et al., 2025a]) through our lightweight fusion module.
Combined with the edit descriptor alignment loss, this imposes strong constraints on the denoising
trajectory, guiding the model toward the desired editing direction during the denoising process (see
Fig. 2). As a result, the model can perform fine-grained and controllable video editing without
requiring explicit masks. To ensure temporal consistency, we further introduce a feature alignment
strategy based on the DINO [Caron et al., 2021], which aligns frame-level features similarity of the
generated video with original video, thereby preserving structural coherence across frames.
Extensive experiments demonstrate competitive edit fidelity and strong preservation, temporal con-
sistency, and perceptual quality on standard benchmarks. Figure 1 illustrates both local and global
editing effects. In summary, we make the following contributions:
• We introduce image-to-video edit distillation: an image pair demonstrating a visual transformation
is compressed into a compact feature and reused as an explicit video editing condition.
• We design a parameter-efficient transformation injection mechanism for large video diffusion
transformers, combining spatial cross-attention, global FiLM modulation, and additive residual
editing while keeping the 14B backbone frozen.

Related Work
Image editing with diffusion models.
Recent image editing systems have demonstrated that
diffusion models can follow rich editing instructions from text or images. Representative approaches
include stochastic editing via noisy image trajectories [Meng et al., 2022], prompt-based editing
[Hertz et al., 2023, Brooks et al., 2023], inversion-based control [Mokady et al., 2023], and latent
diffusion models that enable high-resolution synthesis at scale [Rombach et al., 2022]. While these
methods achieve strong performance in single-image editing, their control signals are typically
defined at the image level, limiting their ability to ensure temporal consistency or to reuse learned
transformations across videos. In contrast, our first stage represents an image edit as a compact
feature-level transformation, enabling it to serve as a guidance signal for video editing.
Video generation and video editing.
Video diffusion models provide strong priors for text-to-video
and image-to-video synthesis [Blattmann et al., 2023, Wu et al., 2023, Khachatryan et al., 2023,
Guo et al., 2024, Wang et al., 2025a]. Building controllable editors on top of these priors remains
challenging. Existing approaches explore attention control, feature propagation, inversion, correspon-
dence guidance, and one-shot tuning, including Video-P2P [Liu et al., 2024], TokenFlow [Geyer
et al., 2024], CoDeF [Ouyang et al., 2024a], VideoSwap [Gu et al., 2024], AnyV2V [Ku et al.,
2024], and RF-Solver-Edit [Wang et al., 2025b]. Recent systems broaden the control interface:
HunyuanCustom [Hu et al., 2025] targets customized video generation from multimodal reference
conditions, while VACE [Jiang et al., 2025] unifies video creation and editing within a single frame-
work. Motion-control methods such as MagicMotion [Li et al., 2025] inject trajectory conditions
through a ControlNet-style branch and achieve effective object-motion control, but this design intro-
duces a relatively heavy conditional pathway and assumes explicit trajectory or region-level controls.
These methods work well when the desired change can be specified through text, inversion trajectories,
reference conditions, or reliable correspondences, but they often struggle with exemplar-specific
appearance changes, global relighting, or complex local edits without masks. EDITDISTILL instead
replaces implicit prompt interpretation and dense control specification with an explicit learned trans-
formation descriptor while relying on the video backbone for temporal rendering. Compared with
ControlNet-style residual branches [Zhang et al., 2023], and low-rank adapters [Hu et al., 2022], our
injector follows this parameter-efficient design but focus more on the diffusion process.

Method : EDITDISTILL
EDITDISTILL contains two components trained sequentially. Stage I learns an image edit descriptor
that captures the transformation between a before/after image pair. Stage II uses this descriptor to
condition a video diffusion model. The design deliberately separates edit extraction from temporal
rendering: Stage I answers what visual change should be applied, and Stage II decides where and
how that change should appear in each video frame. This design further enables parameter-efficient
adaptation by training only the lightweight injection modules, allowing the model to steer the
denoising trajectory of the video diffusion toward the desired edit with a small number of trainable
parameters (see pipeline in Fig. 3).
4.1
Stage I: Distilling Image Edits into Compact Descriptors
We first encode the source and edited images into latent space features using a shared frozen VAE,
and the features are denoted as zs = Evae(Is) and ze = Evae(Ie). The pair (zs, ze), together with
their difference (ze −zs), is fed into a multi-scale variational encoder (noted as Eϕ) to infer the
compact edit descriptor d. Specifically, the encoder predicts a posterior distribution qϕ(d | zs, ze) =

What does the edit descriptor d encode?
The edit descriptor together with the residual recon-
struction encourages a separation of roles: zs preserves the shared content, while d captures the
edit-specific change. The attention maps in Fig. 4 help reveal what d encodes. For local edits, high
responses concentrate on regions that need to change (e.g., object parts or inserted attributes), while
background remains largely unaffected. For global edits such as illumination or style changes, the
responses become broader but still focus on meaningful regions like the sky, light sources, and main
scene elements, rather than spreading uniformly. These patterns indicate that d does not represent
a target image or a fixed mask. Instead, it specifies what transformation to apply, while its interac-
tion with video features determines where the change is applied in each frame as illustrated by the
frame-wise descriptor attention maps in Fig. 8. This separation allows the edit to transfer consistently
across frames without explicit mask supervision, while preserving unaffected regions.
4.2
Stage II: Descriptor-Guided Video Diffusion
Stage II renders the distilled descriptor into a temporally coherent edited video. We adopt Wan2.1-
I2V-14B as the base generator, trained under Flow Matching [Lipman et al., 2023] paradigm, which
models a time-dependent velocity field vθ(xt, t, c) along the straight-line interpolation from data
(t = 0) to noise (t = 1).
We extend this backbone with a proposed lightweight feature injection module to obtain a descriptor-
conditioned velocity field vΘ(xt, t, [ctext, cvideo], d) ∈RN×H×W ×C, where d is either inferred
from (Is, Ie) or precomputed once and reused across videos. The backbone is kept entirely frozen,
and only this feature fusion module is trained, enabling parameter-efficient adaptation.
Our feature injection and fusion module first adapts d via a shared MLP, denoted as ¯d =
MLP(LN(d)) + d, and then injects it into the transformer blocks through three complementary
branches: (1) a cross-attention branch for spatially localized edits, (2) a FiLM branch [Perez et al.,
2018] for global appearance control, and (3) an additive branch for low-rank edit biases. A zero-
initialized gated fusion combines the branches, so the wrapped model initially matches the frozen
backbone and the edit pathway opens gradually during training. Appendix E gives details.
4.3

the video output to (1) the desired image-level edit and (2) the temporal structure of the source video.

5
Experiments
We evaluate EDITDISTILL on different types of edits and several video benchmark datasets. Our
experiments aim to answer four questions: (1) whether the image edit can be distilled to the video
diffusion model, (2) whether the unedited content is preserved, (3) whether the edit is consistent over
time, and (4) which parts of the method contribute most to the improvement.
5.1
Experimental Setup
Training and inference details.
Stage I trains the variational transformation feature encoder Eϕ
and residual decoder Dψ in the frozen Wan2.1 VAE latent space at a resolution of 480 × 832. We
use AdamW with a learning rate of 1 × 10−4, batch size 64, and 200,000 training iterations. In
Stage II, we load the frozen Stage I encoder together with the frozen Wan2.1-I2V-14B backbone,
and train only the edit feature injection modules. Optimization is performed with AdamW using
a learning rate of 1 × 10−5, a global batch size of 16, and 30,000 iterations. Unless otherwise
specified, injectors are inserted into every third transformer block of Wan. The loss weights are set
to αedit = 0.5 and αDINO = 0.2. During inference, we generate 81-frame video clips at 480 × 832
using 50 flow-matching denoising steps. All training experiments are conducted on 16 NVIDIA
H800 GPUs (80GB memory each). The trainable injection modules occupy approximately 480MB,
accounting for less than 4% of the 14B backbone parameters.
Datasets.
Stage I uses approximately 2M paired images aggregated from OmniGen2 and GPT-
Image-Edit [Wu et al., 2025b, Wang et al., 2025c]. For stage II, We carefully constructed a large-
scale video editing dataset. First, we leveraged the Wan 2.1 video generation model to synthesize
preliminary video pairs from image editing pair data, while strictly enforcing identical text prompts
except for the edited regions. To further improve the consistency of these coarse video-editing
pairs, we use the edit loss and DINO feature loss described in the Method section, which effectively
constrained both semantic and structural alignment between these video pairs. After a rigorous

A
Limitations and Impact
EDITDISTILL still has several limitations. Because the descriptor is injected implicitly into the
denoising process, highly localized edits may slightly affect nearby regions when attention spreads
beyond the intended area. Since the descriptor is extracted from the first frame, performance can
degrade under very large motion, severe occlusion, or scene changes that make the first-frame edit
poorly representative of later frames.
This work enables transformation-guided video editing by transferring image-level edits to videos,
with applications in creative content production, video post-production, and assistive visual tools. It
also supports controllable simulation of environmental changes such as lighting and weather.
At the same time, improved editing capability may increase the risk of deceptive media generation,
identity manipulation, and unauthorized content modification. By constraining edit specification
through visual exemplars, the method may lower the barrier for targeted misuse.
This work does not involve human-subject experiments. Responsible use should emphasize usage
restrictions, provenance tracking, and watermarking or other disclosure mechanisms. In addition,
training data and exemplars should respect privacy, copyright, and licensing constraints. We recom-
mend treating portrait editing and safety-sensitive scenarios as restricted deployment settings.

Bottleneck capacity.
We use a 1024-dimensional descriptor by default. Smaller descriptors lose
fine edit attributes such as local insertions, material changes, and subtle lighting transformations.
Larger descriptors improve reconstruction only marginally but are more prone to source-specific
leakage. The chosen capacity balances reconstruction fidelity and transferability.
Stage II implication.
Because Stage I forces the edit through a global descriptor and applies it
through conditional modulation, d behaves as an edit operator rather than a spatial template. Stage II
can therefore inject the same descriptor into video transformer blocks and let the video features
determine where the edit should appear in each frame. This is why the same image-level descriptor
can re-localize to moving regions over time, as shown in Figure 8.
