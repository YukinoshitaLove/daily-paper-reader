We propose Nemotron-Labs-Diffusion-Uni (NLD-Uni), a unified discrete diffusion large language model (DLLM)
for visual understanding and generation tasks. Compared with prior unified diffusion models, NLD-Uni in-
troduces two major innovations. First, we propose a unified cross-paradigm on-policy distillation (OPD)
framework that successfully transfers the visual understanding ability of autoregressive VLMs and visual
generation ability of continuous flow models, to a unified DLLM. Second, NLD-Uni incorporates a novel
progressive self-refining diffusion process that gradually corrupts each token across multiple steps during
training, as opposed to adopting the conventional masked diffusion formulation that directly corrupts a
clean image token to an uninformative mask token. At inference, this design allows NLD-Uni to make
intermediate commit to a token, enabling progressive self-refinement for better visual quality. Through
extensive experiments, we show that NLD-Uni achieves state-of-the-art performance among unified DLLMs
on diverse tasks including visual question-answering, multi-modal reasoning, object grounding, text-to-image
generation, and image editing. Model and Code are publicly available.

1. Introduction
Unified Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) [1, 2, 3, 4] have achieved strong performance on
diverse multimodal understanding and generation tasks. Compared with specialist models, they learn to
perform diverse tasks via a unified sequence modeling interface, allowing synergetic learning that benefits
each individual tasks [5]. They generally fall into two categories. The first design represents text and image
as discrete tokens and employs a unified autoregressive (AR) modeling paradigm [6, 4, 7]. The second design
combines autoregressive modeling for text generation and continuous diffusion for image generation (AR+Diff)
[5, 8, 9, 10]. Several recent works such as LaViDa-O [11] and MMaDA [12] explored a third alternative that

Unlike AR models or continuous flow models, MDMs randomly mask discrete tokens in a sequence and learn
to predict the masked tokens during training. At inference, MDMs initialize a fully masked sequence and
gradually unmask tokens across multiple diffusion steps. Compared with AR models, MDMs offer faster
inference speed because they can generate multiple tokens in parallel [13, 14]. Compared with AR+Diff setup,
which needs to carefully balance conflicting AR and Diffusion objectives [5], unified DLLMs offer a single
objective for both text and image, resulting in simplicity and, more importantly, scalability.
Despite these advantages, a substantial performance gap remains between existing unified DLLMs and
domain-specialized VLMs and continuous flow models [12, 11]. In this work, we seek to narrow these gaps by
introducing NLD-Uni, a unified DLLM featuring a new training paradigm and new architecture.
First, although on-policy distillation (OPD) has proven highly effective in improving the visual understand-
ing capabilities of VLMs [15] and the visual generation capabilities of continuous flow models [16], adapting it
to unified MDMs is challenging because of a lack of strong-performing MDM teachers. Distilling from strong
AR VLMs and continuous flow models is not feasible because they follow fundamentally different paradigms.
To overcome this challenge, we propose cross-paradigm on-policy distillation (OPD), which enables
NLD-Uni to learn from teachers with different generation paradigms, including an AR VLM teacher for visual
understanding and a continuous flow teacher for visual generation.
Second, for visual generation tasks specifically, MDMs lack the self-refinement capabilities of continuous
flow models. At inference, each token jumps directly from a masked state to a clean state and stays unchanged
afterwards. Since MDMs sample tokens independently from per-token logit prediction, all tokens sampled
at the same diffusion step are assumed to be conditionally independent. This assumption rarely holds in
practice since tokens from the same image are highly correlated. To address this limitation and enable iterative
refinement, we introduce a self-refining masked diffusion process that incorporates new intermediate

To evaluate the effectiveness of NLD-Uni, we conduct extensive evaluations on a diverse range of multimodal

art performance among unified DLLMs and greatly reduces the gap to specialized AR VLMs and continuous

The main contributions of this work are summarized as follows: 1) We develop a cross-paradigm OPD
framework that improves both visual understanding and generation by distilling knowledge from non-DLLM
teachers. 2) We introduce a self-refining masked diffusion process that improves visual generation quality over
the canonical masked diffusion process. 3) We present NLD-Uni, a state-of-the-art unified DLLM capable of
both understanding and generating visual content. We acknowledge that while extensive experiments show
the effectiveness of our proposed methods, the model can be further improved via better data mixture and
architecture optimization.
2. Preliminaries and Related Works
2.1. Diffusion Language Models
Discrete diffusion language models [17, 13, 18] generate multiple text tokens in parallel, making them more
efficient than AR models. However, its bidirectional attention introduces overhead compared to causal
attention in AR models, particular for long sequences. Several works [19, 20] address this limitation through
block-wise semi-auto-regressive generation. Notably, SBD [21, 22] incorporates variable-sized blocks during
training, enabling flexible inference.

2.2. Masked Discrete Image Models
Masked image models generate images by initializing a fully-masked image and gradually unmasking image
patches. Each patch is encoded to a discrete code through vector quantization (VQ) [23]. MaskGiT [24]
pioneered this paradigm. Messonic [25] scaled it to large-scale T2I. Recent works such as LaViDa-O and
MMaDa [11, 12] explored integrating masked image modeling to unified MLLMs. As discussed in Sec. 1,
these models lack the self-refinement capabilities of continuous flow models. Some work mitigate this problem
using a specialized unmasking schedule that prioritizes unmasking less-correlated tokens [26, 11]. Others
explored revising already unmasked [27, 28]. However, these works are sensitive to heuristic-based confidence
thresholds and still present a mismatch between training and inference setup.
2.3. On-Policy Distillation (OPD)
OPD [29, 30] enables capability transfers from a teacher model to a student model by providing teacher
supervision signals on student-generated sequences. M-OPD [31] extends OPD to a multi-teacher setting to
consolidate the capabilities of domain-specific experts. Several works [32, 33] explored OPD for multimodal

3. Method
3.1. Model Architecture
NLD-Uni adopts a two-tower Elastic Mixture-of-Transformer (Elastic MoT) design following Lavida-O [11],
which consists of an understanding expert tower and generation expert tower. They interact with each other
through a joint attention mechanism. The understanding tower is initialized with a diffusion language model
NLD [35], which uses the block-causal SBD formulation described in Section 2.1. The generation tower is
initialized from an identical copy. Following the common design of unified MLLMs [5, 11], we encode images
with both a semantic encoder and VQ encoder. Once images and texts are tokenized, the canonical SBD
formulation can be directly applied. Elastic-MoT architecture is illustrated in Figure 2. Figure 3 illustrates
the overall framework.

Distilling continuous flow models to DLLM is more challenging as flow models operate in continuous latent
spaces, making exact KL-style distribution matching infeasible. However, the general notion of using teacher
supervision on student-generated sequences is still feasible. In this work, we explore cross-paradigm OPD for
image editing tasks with Qwen-Image [39] teacher. Given a student output, we first re-encode it with the
teacher’s continuous VAE. We then create a teacher supervision target by first adding noise to the student
generation in teacher’s latent space and re-denoise it with the teacher model, conditioned on the input image
and instruction. We call this target teacher-refined student output. This pipeline is shown in Figure 6 (right).

4. Experiment results
4.1. Main Results
Setup. We train NLD-Uni in a three-stage manner. The first stage primarily includes text-image pairs for
basic visual alignment and image generation capabilities. The second stage introduces additional data for
instruction following, image editing, object grounding, and interleaved generation. The final stage is the
unified OPD training stage, with Nemotron-3 Nano Omni as the teacher for multimodal reasoning tasks and
Qwen-Image for image editing tasks. We also mix T2I SFT batches to preserve the text-to-image generation
performance. Notably, we do not include an OPD teacher for T2I tasks because the base model’s performance
on GenEval and HPSv3 is stronger than teacher candidates like Qwen-Image and Flux. The training is
conducted on 64 H100 GPUs over 26 days. In particular, the final OPD stage takes 5 days. We defer additional
details to Appendix B.
Generic Understanding Tasks. We report the performance of image understanding tasks in Table 1.
NLD-Uni outperforms the previous state-of-the-art unified diffusion model and diffusion VLMs like LLaDa-V
[41], NLD-VLM [35] and LaViDa-O [11] by considerable margin on a wide range of benchmarks such as
MMMU [42], ChartQA [43], InfoVQA [44] and AI2D[45]. On object grounding benchmark RefCOCO [46]
and GUI grounding benchmark ScreenSpot-Pro [47], NLD-Uni also demonstrates strong performance while
most unified models are not equipped with this ability, unlocking new potential applications for diffusion
language models. These gains are mostly attributed to scaling training data. Overall, NLD-Uni reduces the
gap between unified diffusion models and state-to-the-art AR models on visual understanding.
Multimodal Reasoning Tasks. We report the performance of multimodal reasoning tasks in Table
2 and compare NLD-Uni with pre-OPD checkpoint (NLD-Uni-Base). Our proposed cross-paradigm OPD
framework improves performance on a wide range of tasks, with most notable improvements in We-Math [48],
Geometry3k [49], and MathVerse [50]. It also achieves state-of-the-art performance among diffusion language
models. We additionally include a naive OPD baseline using OPD-LM [34] style loss which directly copies
teacher scores as the supervision target for diffusion generation, this worsens the performance of NLD-Uni-Base
because of the mismatch we identified in Section 3.3. These results highlight the effectiveness and necessity of
the proposed cross-paradigm OPD framework.
Text-to-Image. We report results on GenEval [59], DPG [60], and HPS v3 [61] for a comprehensive

5. Conclusion
Diffusion language models have attracted considerable attention in recent years for their greater efficiency
compared with autoregressive models and their unified interface for multimodal data.
However, their
performance in practice still lags behind that of state-of-the-art models, limiting their adoption. In this
paper, we propose two methods for developing more capable DLLMs: cross-paradigm on-policy distillation,
which transfers knowledge from diverse teacher models, and a self-refining diffusion process, which improves
visual generation quality. Extensive experiments demonstrate the effectiveness of both approaches. We hope
NLD-Uni will offer valuable insights for future research on DLLMs and unified multimodal systems.

• OPD-LM samples rollouts from the diffusion view while NLD-Uni samples from the AR view
• OPD-LM directly uses teacher scores to supervise the student’s diffusion view, while NLD-Uni uses the

unified training run combining visual understanding, text-to-image, and image editing objectives, while
OPD-LM focuses solely on single-objective AR2Diff post-training in the language domain.

Connection to Trajectory-refined Distillation in Language Modeling. We note that while our
objective is an SFT-style loss, the learning process is on-policy because the supervision target is derived from
the student output. This paradigm closely connects with several concurrent works in the OPD literature in the
language domain that explored student-teacher interleaved generation. Notably, Trajectory-refined Distillation
[40] proposes to use a teacher model to correct student trajectories in language tasks. NLD-Uni expands this
concept across model families and to the visual generation domain to address a lack of strong MDM teachers
in visual generation tasks.
Qualitative Assessment. In addition to Figure 6, we provide additional samples in Figure 14 to illustrate
the effectiveness of our proposed approach. We make two observations. First, compared with pure teacher
generation, the teacher-refined student outputs are visually aligned to the student output but with more
refined details and better source-image preservation, making them a more ideal training target. Second, we
find that the teacher-refined student output can actually be better than the student model’s outputs when
the pure teacher generation makes a mistake. In the example "Remove the wooden rectangular dining
table", teacher-refined student generation achieves the best results and outperforms both student and teacher
generation. This occurs because when the teacher makes a mistake, the pure teacher generation tends to drift
from the student example considerably (in this case, over-editing and removing two extra chairs in addition to
the table). The refinement process, which constrains the teacher output to a neighborhood of the student
roll-out, serves as useful regularization in these cases.

image-text pairs from (A) to preserve text-to-image ability.

referred to as NLD-Uni. The full details of the optimization are listed in Table 5.

C.2. Effect of refinement steps
While in the formulation we use 𝜖∈[0, 1] to define the progression speed of intermediate states in self-refining
diffusion, what matters in practical inference settings is the total number of inference steps NFE and the
number of refinements each sampled token goes through, denoted by 𝑁. For example, 𝑁= 1 corresponds
to vanilla mask diffusion with 𝜖→0, while 𝑁= NFE means all tokens follow the same refinement schedule
across the entire sampling process, or 𝜖= 1. 𝑁> NFE is not possible by definition.
We show the interaction effect of NFE and 𝑁in Table 6 at inference time. We note that at any total NFE,
setting 𝜖= 1 works poorly. At small NFE, 𝑁= 1 which corresponds to a single state jump works best. By
contrast, at larger NFE, a higher number of refinement steps starts to show benefits, with 𝑁= 8 working best
in our default inference setup with 64 steps. Increasing N from 8 to 16 provides no further improvement in the
tested settings and can substantially degrade performance at low NFE, where it implies an 𝜖value closer to
one. This suggests 8 refinement steps are sufficient. The failure patterns at low NFE and around NFE = 𝑁
suggest we should enforce some form of sequential order between the unmasking/refining processes of different
tokens, as opposed to unmasking/refining a large number of tokens simultaneously, which can be challenging.

C.3.2. Sampling Temperature
We vary the temperature used to generate student AR rollouts during OPD and evaluate the resulting
models in both diffusion and AR decoding modes. As shown in Table 8, greedy rollouts achieve the highest
AR accuracy (76.9%), whereas a temperature of 0.4 achieves the highest diffusion accuracy (74.8%). Thus,
requiring each rollout token to be an argmax of the student’s AR conditional distribution is not necessary for
effective transfer to the diffusion view. The improvement at moderate temperatures suggests that greater
rollout diversity can benefit diffusion training. Across all tested temperatures, AR-OPD+Consistency achieves
higher diffusion accuracy than the alternative designs evaluated in our ablations, such as AR-OPD+SFT.
Together, these results support using the student’s own AR predictions to supervise its diffusion view, while
indicating that strict per-token argmax alignment is unnecessary.
We clarify that all evaluations are performed with a temperature of 0 for consistency and reproducibility.
Empirically, even for models with non-zero rollout temperature, using a zero temperature still gives better
accuracy.

D.1. Unified Architecture.
Unified MLLMs are capable of both understanding and generation tasks. Integrating different capabilities into a
single model presents unique architectural challenges. Generally, unified MLLMs have two common architecture
choices. The first type, such as Janus [4], MMaDa [12], Show-O[64], uses a single dense transformer to output
both image and text tokens. The second type, such as BAGEL [5], MetaQueries [8] and LaViDa-O [11]
employs separate parameter sets for handling image and text modalities. In particular, mixture-of-transformers
(MoT) architecture [93] incorporates two separate sets of attention and FFN parameters to process different
modalities, while still allowing cross-modal interaction via a joint attention mechanism. Under this paradigm,
several works such as X-Fusion and LM-Fusion [9, 94] further investigated architecture designs and training
recipes of MoT. In particular, LaViDa-O [11] proposes Elastic-MoT design, which decouples the attention
layer in the final half of the model and only enables joint attention in early layers. Given an 8B base model,
Elastic-MoT extends it to a 16B MoT. However, when performing understanding tasks, only 8B parameters are
loaded. When performing generation tasks, only 8B generation parameters + 4B understanding parameters

F. Limitations and Future Work
In this work, we propose NLD-Uni, introducing a self-refining diffusion process and cross-paradigm on-policy
distillation. Despite considerable success, some limitations remain. First, while the cross-paradigm OPD
framework allows us to distill an AR teacher to a diffusion student, it still requires the vocabulary spaces of the
two models to match. In our setup, both models use the Ministral 3 [104] tokenizer. Many existing works have
explored cross-tokenizer distillation between AR models [105]. We leave extending these methods to support
cross-paradigm distillation to future work. Second, while NLD-Uni achieves state-of-the-art performance
among unified diffusion MLLMs, there may be considerable room for further optimization on the model
architecture side. We leave that for future work as well since it is not the focus of this work. Third, we define
the self-refining diffusion process with the non-Markov formulation with predefined per-token corruption
intervals and uniform progression of intermediate states. This design is mostly motivated by minimizing
complexity. Future work may explore defining a Markov process with a stochastic transition rate that allows
tokens to stay at different intermediate states for varying lengths of time. Finally, from a system perspective,
NLD-Uni lacks fine-grained text rendering capabilities because such data is not widely present in the training
mix. We will address this issue in future by curating additional text-rendering-focused data.
