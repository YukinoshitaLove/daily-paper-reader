Broad classes of deep generative models (DGMs) operate by learning to map a simple source distribution, such as a standard Gaussian, to a com-plex high-dimensional target distribution. Despite their success, DGMs often empirically struggle to accurately represent distributions with heavy tails or well-separated modes. In this work, we show that these difficulties reflect intrinsic properties of the underlying transport problem rather than particular neural network architectures or training procedures. We establish quantitative lower bounds on the gradients and Jacobians of pushforward mappings that transport log-concave source distributions to such targets. For heavy-tailed target distributions, the required gradient magnitude grows as the target de-velops increasingly large moments. For multimodal target distributions, the low-probability regions between well-separated modes similarly force large gradients in the transport map. We discuss how such large derivatives relate to trade-offs between expressivity and numerical stability. Our results hold under substantially weaker regularity conditions than previous analyses and remain stable under perturbations of the target distribution. Finally, we show that these obstructions can be mitigated when the goal is approximation of a given density rather than generative modeling. Specifically, we propose simple classes of bijective reparametrizations that can alleviate the difficult geometric structure and reduce the gradient burden placed on the learned generative map. Numerical experiments support our theoretical analysis. 

1. Introduction. Deep generative models (DGMs) are foundational tools in modern artificial intelligence. Despite their success in tasks such as image generation, it has been empirically observed that DGMs often struggle when the target distribution is heavy-tailed [37, 54] or multimodal [59, 67]. In particular, DGMs can grossly under-characterize tail events [66] and fail to represent distinct modes [36]. These phenomena are consequential in practice since DGMs are increasingly adopted in scientific domains where heavy-tailed or multimodal data are the norm rather than the exception [32, 46]. Heavy-tailed data arise in applications such as the climate sciences [16, 35], where inadequate representation of extreme events can lead to under-estimation of extreme climate occurrences [75], and finance [9], where underestimating tail probabilities can distort estimates of important risk measures such as Expected Shortfall [19, 50]. Similarly, multimodal distributions arise in applications ranging from single-cell analysis, where missing modes can obscure biologically distinct cell populations [48], to the modeling of heterogeneous populations, where omitting modes associated with under-represented subgroups can undermine the accuracy and fairness of downstream analyses [47].

As DGMs have become more widely used, it is critical to understand why DGMs can struggle to faithfully represent heavy-tailed and multimodal distributions. In this paper, we study the intrinsic limitations of pushforward DGMs in representing such distributions. DGMs can be viewed through the lens of learning a map T : Z → X that pushes a simple source distribution ν (most commonly a Gaussian) on some latent space Z to a target distribution μ

on the sample space X . Prior work has examined limitations arising from global Lipschitz constraints on the neural network map bT used to approximate T [34, 59, 66]. We ask a more fundamental question: what regularity constraints does the target distribution μ itself impose on any transport map T satisfying T#ν = μ? This perspective isolates the intrinsic obstruction in transporting ν to μ, independently of the architecture or estimation procedure used to approximate the map. As shown by the heatmaps of the approximate samples, normalizing flows under a Gaussian source distribution struggle significantly with these targets. For our theoretical analysis of heavy-tailed targets, we leverage Poincaré-type inequalities to show that under the predominantly used Gaussian source measures, pushforward maps to targets with large centered moments must have large gradient components and Jacobians. We further generalize these results to log-concave and strongly log-concave source distributions. For multimodal target distributions with well-separated modes, we use isoperimetric and coarea inequalities to show that large gradients are a mathematical necessity, even when the target’s moments remain bounded. It is well-known that, for invertible generative models, large transport derivatives can amplify numerical errors [4], leading to a tradeoff between expressivity and numerical stability. Our results thus unveil insights into why common DGMs can fundamentally struggle to learn transport maps for heavy-tailed and/or multimodal targets, 

irrespective of their underlying neural network parameterization. Our analysis also suggests ways to mitigate these obstructions when the goal is to use such neural networks for approximation of a given target density rather than generative modeling. The target distribution in generative modeling is typically specified by training samples [52]. When the target density is known up to a normalizing constant, as is typical in variational inference [56], we can leverage this additional information to mitigate these obstructions. The key idea is to use known bijective reparametrizations to absorb part of the required derivative growth, leaving a more regular map to be learned. Although the resulting composite map remains subject to our lower bounds, this decomposition reduces the burden on the learned component of the map. The right two panels of Figure 1 demonstrate the practical benefits of applying these bijective reparameterizations. Specifically, Figure 1(b) shows that the tail regions of the heavy-tailed Student-t density are better represented, while Figure 1(d) demonstrates that both modes of the Gaussian mixture are correctly captured. Our main contributions are summarized as follows: 1. For heavy-tailed targets, we derive lower bounds on transport gradients and Jacobians in terms of centered target moments. Our theory holds under Gaussian and log-concave source distributions without assuming a global Lipschitz bound. Importantly, these results characterize intrinsic limitations of the underlying transport problem, independent of any neural network architecture or training procedure. 2. For multimodal targets, we similarly show that low-probability valleys between modes force large transport gradients and Jacobians. 3. When the goal is to approximate a given density, we propose simple bijective reparametriza-tions for heavy-tailed and multimodal targets that reduce the derivative burden on the learned map. This allows us to alleviate the intrinsic gradient barriers in pushforward DGMs and more faithfully represent these challenging densities. The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews related work, and Section 3 introduces the notation and setup. Section 4 uses functional inequalities to derive gradient lower bounds in terms of target moments, while Section 5 establishes localized Jacobian bounds for multimodal targets. Section 6 discusses practical implications for DGM architecture design and numerical stability. Section 7 develops bijective reparametrizations for density approximation, and Section 8 presents numerical illustrations. Proofs, technical background, and additional experiments are provided in the Supplement. 4

2. Related Work. In this work, we focus on pushforward-based deep generative models, where the generated distribution is realized as the pushforward T#ν of a fixed source distribu-tion ν by a learned map T . This perspective encompasses models that represent such maps explicitly with neural networks, including generative adversarial networks (GANs; [28]), vari-ational autoencoders (VAEs; [38]), and normalizing flows [17, 56]. It also includes continuous flow models, such as neural ordinary differential equations (ODEs) and related stochastic flow models [1, 12, 44], where a learned vector field is integrated over time to produce a transport map. Diffusion models [31, 63, 64] can also be treated within this pushforward framework after time discretization; the reverse sampling procedures can be viewed as pushforwards of a Gaussian measure on an augmented space [59, 66]. Our results therefore apply to the resulting discretized sampling maps. Models that do not naturally specify a pushforward mechanism, such as energy-based models defined by unnormalized densities or large language models based on attention architectures, fall outside the scope of our analysis. Several prior works have quantified the expressive power of DGMs under light-tailed/moments assumptions on the target [45, 74]. However, there is growing empirical and methodological evidence that standard DGMs struggle to faithfully represent heavy-tailed targets [26, 34, 66, 72]. These observations have motivated work aimed at mitigating such deficiencies, such as heavy-tailed diffusions [54], Pareto GANs [33], and tail-adaptive normalizing flows [30]. For multimodal targets, [59] and [67] relate mode separation and approximation error to global Lipschitz constraints. Our multimodal analysis yields lower bounds on derivative moments under only local Lipschitz regularity. The most closely related prior works are [34] and [59], who study heavy-tailed and multi-modal targets under global Lipschitz assumptions. Related analyses of invertible networks and bi-Lipschitz flows also use global Lipschitz bounds [29, 70]. Our results instead constrain gradients and Jacobians directly, without imposing a global bound on their size and allowing transport maps with unbounded derivatives. We require only Sobolev regularity for the mo-ment bounds and local Lipschitz continuity for the multimodal bounds. These conditions are substantially weaker and cover a broader class of generative models, including normalizing flows and continuous flows whose transport maps need not be globally Lipschitz. The main tools that we use in our analysis are functional and isoperimetric inequalities. These inequalities play a central role in probability theory, functional analysis, and the study of Markov chains [2]. In the context of sampling, such inequalities are often used to analyze the convergence of sampling algorithms such as Langevin dynamics [15]. While these inequalities are typically used to derive upper bounds on a Markov chain’s distance to stationarity in the sampling literature, here we use them to lower-bound the size of transport gradients and Jacobians. The gradient bounds derived in our analysis motivate changing the coordinates in which a target density is approximated. Such coordinate transformations have a long history in the sampling literature. For example, measure transport methods [49] and normalizing flows [25, 53] have been used to construct changes of coordinates that facilitate Monte Carlo sampling from complex target distributions. Gaussianization methods [13, 27, 58] seek transformations that make a target distribution approximately Gaussian, whereas stereographic MCMC [73] uses stereographic projections to reformulate the sampling problem on a sphere. In this article, we develop simple, bijective reparametrizations specifically for heavy-tailed and multimodal target densities.

4. Gradient Bounds for Deep Generative Models via Functional Inequalities. To this end, it is natural to consider Poincaré inequalities, which relate the variance of the target to the gradient of the pushforward map. We begin with the most relevant case when the source distribution is Gaussian. Proofs of the exact-transport results in this section are delegated to Section S2 of the Supplement. 6

5. Gradient Bounds for Multimodal Targets. While moment bounds quantify the derivative growth associated with heavy-tailed targets, they do not generally capture the difficulty of transporting to well-separated modes. Large moments in the target distribution are not the only source of large Jacobians. In this section, we show that accurate representation of target distributions with well-separated modes also requires large Jacobians. Here, the relevant property is the presence of two regions with substantial probability separated by a low-probability region, which we refer to as a valley. An accurate transport map must send substantial probability mass to each of the two high probability regions while sending little mass to the valley. In what follows, we use isoperimetric inequalities to show that satisfying these requirements forces large Jacobian norms on the transport map, with quantitative bounds determined by the separation and the probability masses of these regions. This generalizes and complements analyses that rely on global Lipschitz assumptions [59, 67]. All proofs in this section are given in Section S3 of the Supplement. 5.1. A Localized Jacobian Bound. 6. Practical Implications for Deep Generative Modeling. Our results concern proper-ties of any pushforward map T that transports a source distribution ν to a target μ = T#ν. Our heavy-tail bounds in Section 4 apply to Gaussian and, more generally, log-concave sources under weak Sobolev regularity. Meanwhile, our multimodal bounds in Section 5 require only local Lipschitz continuity. We now outline practical implications of these results for deep generative models. 

Prior Lipschitz-based Analysis. Prior work studies heavy-tailed targets under global Lipschitz assumptions on the generative mapping bT [34, 66, 72]. Related analyses address disconnected or multimodal targets under global Lipschitz or bi-Lipschitz assumptions [59, 67, 70]. A global Lipschitz bound requires ∥ bT (x) − bT (y)∥2 ≤ L∥x − y∥2 for all x, y . This raises the question of whether allowing mappings without such a bound removes these difficulties. Our results show that large derivatives can remain necessary without a global Lipschitz constraint. For example, with Z ∼ N (0 , I d), large component variances of T (Z) require 

E∥∇ Tj (Z)∥22 to be large for the corresponding coordinates j. This averaged bound does not determine where large derivatives occur. For the explicit heavy-tail rearrangements in Section 8, they grow rapidly in the latent tails. For multimodal targets, our bounds identify a large Jacobian contribution over the preimage of the low-probability region separating modes, even when target moments remain bounded. The need for large gradients is therefore intrinsic to the transport problem. 

Implications for Expressivity versus Stability Trade-off. The requirements of large gradients are related to a practical trade-off between numerical stability and expressivity. It is known that large gradients can contribute to numerical instability in normalizing flow models [4]. For a differentiable mapping T , perturbing an input z by an error e yields 

T (z + e) − T (z) = JT (z)e + o(∥e∥2), e → 0,

where JT (z) is the Jacobian at z. Large Jacobian norms therefore allow small input errors in some directions to produce much larger output errors. Such errors can arise from rounding and finite-precision representations in numerical computations. Behrmann et al. [4] document related numerical failures in reconstruction and sampling, even for mathematically invertible neural networks. Large gradients are also related to numerical instability in neural ODE models [12, 23], as detailed in the following example. EXAMPLE 6.1. [70] establish related lower bounds on total variation approximation error for bi-Lipschitz normalizing flows. Section 7 considers simple, invertible coordinate transformations when the target density can be evaluated up to a normalizing constant. Their invertibility allows us to learn the transport in the transformed coordinates and then recover samples in the original coordinates by applying the known inverse transformation. By lightening the tails or bringing separated modes closer together in the transformed coordinates, these transformations can reduce the Jacobian norms required of the learned transport map. 

7. Approximating Densities via Bijective Reparametrizations. The preceding results describe limitations of learning a pushforward map in the original coordinates of the target. Several prior works have considered modifying the source distribution to be heavier-tailed to model heavy-tailed targets [34], including in diffusion models [54]. Here, we propose a different approach which retains the simple source distribution but reparametrizes the target 

geometry through a change of coordinates. By learning in the transformed coordinates, we aim to reduce the Jacobian norms required of the transport map. We consider the task of approximating a target density that can be evaluated up to a normalizing constant. We focus on simple one-dimensional transformations that can be applied coordinate-wise to multivariate targets. These transformations simplify marginal geometry, leaving the learned generative map to model the joint distribution, including dependence between coordinates. Let Ψ : Rm → Rm be a bijection such that both Ψ and Ψ−1 are C1. We choose Ψ before fitting the learned map and write τ = Ψ #μ for the transformed target. S5. Experimental Details and Additional Experiments. 

S5.1. Details of the Heavy-Tail Illustrations. These distribution families are widely used in many fields such as climatology, hydrology, and finance [9, 16, 35], making them natural and representative test cases. They are also particularly well-suited for illustrating our theory for several reasons. First, a transport map from a Gaussian source can be constructed explicitly for these targets, enabling direct inspection of their gradients and Jacobians. This allows us to numerically assess the tightness and practical relevance of the theoretical lower bounds derived in Section 4. Second, the tail behavior of these families is governed by interpretable parameters (i.e., the degrees of freedom for the Student-t family and the shape parameters for the Pareto and Fréchet families), providing explicit control over moment growth and allowing us to systematically investigate varying degrees of tail heaviness. In the multivariate case, when pushing forward a standard Gaussian in Rd to a product distribution also in Rd, the pushforward map can be applied coordinate-wise. If d = 2 , this is given by

As target distributions, we consider three canonical families of heavy-tailed laws: (a) Student-t distributions, (b) Pareto distributions, and (c) Fréchet distributions. Here, all target measures are product measures with independent coordinates. For ease of visualization, we present results in the two-dimensional setting. Since all target distributions are product measures, this entails no loss of generality, and the behavior observed in two dimensions is representative of higher-dimensional cases. For each parameter setting, we plot both sides of the trace bound Poincaré inequality given in part (iii) of Theorem 4.2. Specifically, the lower bound (left-hand side of the inequality) corresponds to the trace of the covariance matrix of the target distribution, which admits a closed-form expression for the distributions considered. The right-hand side of the inequality is given by the sum of the expected squared norm of the Jacobian diagonals of the transport map under the Gaussian source measure.
