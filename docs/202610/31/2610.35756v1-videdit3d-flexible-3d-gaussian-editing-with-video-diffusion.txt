Abstract
Recent advancements in text-guided 3D editing, particularly with 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS), have shown promise but
still lag behind the flexibility of 2D editing tools. Existing methods face a critical trade-off between preserving multi-view
consistency and allowing structural modifications. Image-based editors can also struggle to apply the intended edit to
objects viewed from unusual angles. To address these limitations, we introduce VidEdit3D, a framework that reformulates
3D scene editing as a trajectory-based video editing task. By drawing a parallel between camera movement in static
3D scenes and temporal dynamics in video, we leverage the robust spatiotemporal priors of pre-trained Video Diffusion
Models (VDMs) to encourage semantic and structural coherence across views. Specifically, we propose Trajectory-Guided
Rendering (TGR) to select informative keyframes and construct smooth video clips aligned with VDM input requirements.
To propagate edits from an anchor view to these clips, we develop Flow-Aligned Video Editing (FAVE), a training-free
framework incorporating an Editing Consistency Guidance (ECG) mechanism. ECG contrasts source and edited image
conditions at a shared latent state, using scheduled source-image amplification to strengthen adherence to the edited ref-
erence while maintaining original scene context. Finally, we introduce Region-Selective 3DGS Refinement (RS-GS) to
strictly confine geometric and appearance updates to target primitives, reducing background attribute leakage. Experi-
ments demonstrate stronger instruction alignment and better multi-view consistency than the baselines.

1. Introduction
Recent advances in text-guided image editing have demonstrated remarkable success in enabling users to modify images ac-
cording to natural language instructions [1–3]. By leveraging large-scale diffusion or transformer-based models, these systems
can generate semantically aligned and visually coherent edits, such as replacing objects, altering styles, or modifying attributes,
while preserving content fidelity. This paradigm has significantly democratized content creation, making intuitive visual editing
accessible to non-experts. Building upon this progress, researchers have recently extended text-driven editing to the 3D domain,
particularly in neural implicit representations such as Neural Radiance Fields (NeRFs) [4] and explicit point-based representations
such as 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) [5]. Particularly, compared with NeRF, 3DGS offers greater flexibility and efficiency as
an explicit 3D representation, enabling high-quality real-time rendering and fine-grained local editing. These advantages make
3DGS editing an attractive paradigm for controllable 3D content manipulation, which is the focus of our paper.
However, despite the potential of 3DGS, the capabilities of existing 3D editing frameworks still lag significantly behind their
2D counterparts. Many 3D editing methods focus on operations such as global style transfer, texture modification, or object
removal. This stands in stark contrast to state-of-the-art 2D editing tools, which support highly imaginative and complex trans-
formations with ease. A primary bottleneck restricting 3D editing flexibility is the requirement for multi-view consistency; unlike
2D editing, modifying a 3D scene requires simultaneous adjustments across multiple viewpoints while ensuring the underlying
geometry remains coherent. Early attempts that naively applied 2D editors independently across different views inherently lacked
cross-view coherence [6–8], leading to severe artifacts such as ghosting and flickering. To mitigate these inconsistencies, sub-
sequent works [9–11] introduced geometric guidance through depth or correspondence with the original images. While these
constraints stabilize the optimization process, strong alignment to the source can limit editing flexibility. This limitation becomes
particularly evident in instructions that require structural changes. For instance, a simple instruction like “make the person wear
a hat” becomes difficult to execute when the required geometric addition conflicts with alignment constraints derived from the
original shape.
Image-based editors can also struggle to apply the intended edit to objects viewed from unusual angles, especially when the
relevant parts are partly or fully hidden. For example, an instruction such as “put glasses on a bear” is readily interpreted from
a frontal view but becomes ambiguous from a rear view, where the face is occluded. 3D editing therefore needs to propagate a
clearly specified edit across views while accounting for which object parts are visible.
These limitations motivate the design of a flexible 3D editing framework capable of performing precise semantic manipulations
without the excessive rigidity of geometric locks. To achieve this, we introduce VidEdit3D (Fig. 1), which takes an edit created
in a single view with a 2D editor, propagates it across viewpoints, and reconstructs the edited 3D scene. To robustly facilitate this
propagation, we identify a powerful parallel between temporal consistency in videos and multi-view consistency in the rendered
frames of a 3D scene. A camera trajectory moving around a static 3D object yields a sequence of frames that is structurally
analogous to a video clip. This insight inspires us to leverage the robust spatiotemporal priors of pre-trained Video Diffusion
Models (VDMs) [12–16]. VDMs are designed to maintain object identity and semantic coherence across time, providing a useful
prior for relating 3D viewpoints. ViP3DE [17] also propagates a single edited view into 3D using motion-preserved noise blending
and geometry-aware denoising. VidEdit3D strengthens reference transfer through an explicit contrast between source and edited
image conditions, while spatial source regularization preserves the surrounding scene. Our inversion-free formulation allows
the edited region to change without enforcing source-geometry feature correspondences during denoising. In our comparisons,
VidEdit3D achieves stronger instruction alignment and better multi-view consistency than ViP3DE, including when both use the
same video backbone.
To transform sparse 3D renderings into a form compatible with video diffusion models, we introduce a Trajectory-Guided
Rendering (TGR) strategy. Starting from a user-selected anchor, TGR selects views of the source subject carrying the edit and
connects them into a smooth camera trajectory. We propagate along two branches from the anchor to shorten the sequence
of transfers to distant views and reduce error accumulation. Overlapping clips maintain continuity within each branch while
matching video diffusion priors.
To propagate edits from a key view to the rendered video clips, we develop Flow-Aligned Video Editing (FAVE), a training-
free framework inspired by recent inversion-free editing methods [18, 19]. Unlike prior methods that are primarily guided by text,
our goal is to leverage the strong generative and editing capabilities of 2D image editing models to transfer a user-specified edit
from one anchor view to the full 3D scene.
In standard image-to-video (I2V) generation, classifier-free guidance (CFG) [20] evaluates the denoiser twice under different
text conditions, while both evaluations typically share the same first-frame image as the temporal anchor. This design is effective
for video synthesis, since a fixed first-frame condition helps preserve object identity and temporal coherence over time. In
contrast, FAVE, as an inversion-free editing method, conceptually requires two distinct conditioning roles: one should follow the
edited reference frame and target prompt, while the other should preserve the original scene structure under the source frame
and source prompt. However, directly reusing the standard I2V conditioning scheme in our setting leads both evaluations to
2


remain anchored to the same edited first frame, rather than forming the desired source-versus-target contrast. In I2V models, this
mismatch is exacerbated by the strong first-frame anchoring effect: later frames remain strongly influenced by the first-frame
image condition, which can make the two evaluations inherit similar image-conditioned components and weaken the intended
guidance contrast. To address this issue, we introduce Editing Consistency Guidance (ECG), a training-free inference-time
guidance scheme that restores a meaningful source-versus-edit contrast in the guidance computation. Specifically, ECG amplifies
the source concatenation condition early in sampling, then replaces it with edited-frame features while retaining source text and
cross-attention conditioning. FAVE complements this guidance with source-sequence and spatial constraints that preserve camera
motion and unedited content.
During 3D optimization, existing 3D editing methods [7, 9–11, 21] can suffer from global attribute leakage, where gradients
intended for the target object erroneously update the entire scene. Explicit spatial control keeps the refinement localized to
the intended target. To address this issue, we introduce Region-Selective 3DGS Refinement (RS-GS), a method that explicitly
constrains the refinement to the target region by back-projecting 2D segmentation masks into 3D space and using depth-guided
filtering to select the corresponding Gaussians. By confining the optimization to these back-projected primitives while freezing
all others, RS-GS ensures that geometric and appearance updates are confined to the edited regions.
In summary, VidEdit3D effectively reformulates 3D scene editing as a trajectory-based video editing task. Our main contribu-
tions are:
• We propose VidEdit3D, a framework that reformulates 3D editing as video editing to propagate a reference edit consistently
across viewpoints.
• We design TGR to select informative keyframes and organize them into smooth video clips, improving their compatibility with
pretrained I2V priors.
• We develop FAVE with ECG, a training-free mechanism that combines scheduled source–edit image conditioning with a sepa-
rate source-trajectory constraint to enable precise edit propagation.
• We design RS-GS refinement to confine geometric and appearance updates to target primitives, reducing background attribute
leakage.
• Experiments demonstrate stronger instruction alignment and better multi-view consistency than the compared baselines.
2. Related Work
A significant body of work has explored editing 3D scenes (NeRFs [4] or 3DGS [5]) through optimization or distillation strategies.
Early implicit approaches relied on mesh scaffolds or interactive distillation [22, 23], while subsequent explicit methods utilize
iterative optimization to distill 2D diffusion priors into 3D representations [7, 8, 24]. Recent advancements further enhance
quality through identity-preserving noise estimation [25] or task-specific optimization for style transfer and virtual try-on [26,
27]. However, ensuring multi-view consistency remains a primary bottleneck. To address this, several methods use geometric
guidance such as depth or epipolar alignment [9–11, 21, 28]. Some methods use per-scene fine-tuning [29, 30], while others train
generalized multi-view editors on paired data [31, 32]. ViP3DE [17] propagates an edited view through motion-preserved noise
blending and geometry-aware denoising. VidEdit3D uses inversion-free source–edit conditioning to drive the requested change,
together with spatial source regularization that is weaker inside the edited region and stronger outside it. This separates edit
transfer from preservation of surrounding content, and our comparisons with ViP3DE show gains in both instruction alignment
and multi-view consistency.
For image editing, SDEdit balances realism and fidelity through noising and denoising [33]. With diffusion transformer (DiT)
backbones and rectified flows [34, 35], later methods reduce inversion drift through ODE solvers, noise alignment [36–38],
or history-guided velocity correction [39]. Other methods preserve structure through attention and feature control [2, 3, 40].
MasaCtrl supports non-rigid edits with mutual self-attention [41], while KV-Edit and FreeFlux use DiT-specific key-value reuse
for background-preserving, task-adaptive editing [42, 43]. Video editing adds temporal coherence: cross-frame attention control
and fusion [44, 45] and space-time modulation [46] reduce drift and improve fine-grained control. I2V methods propagate
first-frame edits using temporal features or motion–content alignment [47, 48], whereas FlowDirector uses inversion-free ODE
steering [49]. Complex motion and large appearance or shape changes remain challenging.
3. Method
Given a reconstructed 3D Gaussian scene, calibrated source views, an edited anchor image, and source and target prompts,
VidEdit3D produces an edited 3D scene. As illustrated in Fig. 2, our pipeline consists of three stages: (1) TGR converts selected
multi-view renderings into smooth video clips; (2) FAVE propagates edits across the clip through a trajectory-regularized flow
formulation, augmented with ECG for semantic alignment and a dynamic attention mask for background preservation; and (3)
RS-GS lifts the edited frames back into 3D via depth-constrained back-projection, confining updates to the target Gaussians to
preserve scene-wide consistency.

renderings and the smooth temporal patterns commonly observed in natural videos. When consecutive camera views are far apart

Given the reordered keyframes, we construct a dense camera trajectory via Kochanek–Bartels spline interpolation [50]. The

sides of the trajectory.
To keep propagation focused on the source subject, we evaluate the semantic relevance of the rendered source clips before
editing. Each branch follows consecutive accepted clips and stops before its first rejected clip, preserving a continuous chain of
edited boundary images. Appendix A provides the filtering and interpolation settings.
3.3. Flow-Aligned Video Editing (FAVE)
A central challenge in our setting is that the rendered multi-view sequence corresponds to a static 3D scene observed along a
camera trajectory. Therefore, during edit propagation, the desired variation should come from viewpoint change alone, while the
subject layout, pose, and spatial placement should remain stable across frames. In practice, however, off-the-shelf video editing
models [46, 48, 49, 51, 52] often introduce spurious object motion or geometric drift, which is particularly harmful for 3D editing.
To address this issue, we develop Flow-Aligned Video Editing (FAVE), a training-free propagation framework that adapts
trajectory regularization to I2V editing. Our design is inspired by FlowAlign [18], which regularizes editing trajectories toward
structurally consistent solutions in image editing. We incorporate its source-consistency principle into a coupled source/target
propagation process tailored to video-conditioned denoising, enabling a user-specified edit to propagate along a multi-view video
trajectory while suppressing undesired subject motion.

3.4. Editing Consistency Guidance
In standard I2V generation, classifier-free guidance (CFG) performs two denoiser evaluations under different text conditions
while sharing the same first-frame visual anchor. In our setting, however, this formulation is problematic when computing the
guided prediction vp for edit propagation. In FAVE, an inversion-free editing method, the two guidance evaluations are intended
to play different roles: the target-conditioned prediction should follow the edited first frame and target prompt, whereas the
source-conditioned reference should preserve the original scene structure using the source first frame and prompt. Specifically,

4. Experiments
4.1. Implementation Details
We evaluate VidEdit3D on 50 editing cases, comparing against seven baselines and diverse controls. We use Wan2.1-I2V-14B-
480P [12] with a 50-step schedule at strength 0.9, giving 45 effective denoising steps. Settings are scale g = 15, source-image
scale λ = 1.5, condition-switch index s = 30, and foreground/background regularization coefficients 0.001/0.005. The 3D stage
uses Nerfstudio Splatfacto [54] with fixed cameras and local Gaussian updates. Additional implementation details are provided
in Appendix A.
We compare GaussianEditor [7], DGE [11], VCEdit [10], GaussCtrl [9], EditSplat [21], Omni3DEdit [32], and ViP3DE [17].
All methods use the same source scenes and the same editing prompts. Reference-conditioned methods additionally receive the
same edited anchor image for each case.
CLIP-D measures alignment between image-change and text-change directions, and CLIP-S measures edited-image alignment
with the target description [55]. DINO measures pairwise DINOv2 feature similarity across edited views [56]. MEt3R evaluates
geometric feature consistency [57] on a common set of overlapping adjacent camera pairs.

5. Conclusion
We presented VidEdit3D, which reformulates reference-guided 3D scene editing as I2V propagation by bridging static multi-view
observations with the spatiotemporal priors of pretrained video diffusion models. TGR organizes selected 3D views into coherent
video-like streams for edit propagation. FAVE enables robust semantic propagation through an inversion-free flow formulation,
while ECG uses scheduled image conditioning to maintain a clearer source-to-target direction in DiT-based I2V models. RS-
GS confines updates to target regions during final 3D optimization, reducing background leakage and better preserving unedited
scene content. Together, these components enable high-fidelity, view-consistent edits across diverse viewpoints and challenging
structural modifications without network fine-tuning. VidEdit3D shows how pretrained video priors support flexible 3D editing.

A. Additional Implementation Details
The edited anchor conditions the I2V backbone, while the rendered source clip guides scene preservation. Source and edited
states share noise at each denoising step; ECG amplifies source concatenation features early in sampling. Clips contain 61 frames
to match the causal autoencoder’s temporal compression and retain their rendering camera intrinsics and field of view during
propagation.
We use N = 4 keyframes for small scenes and N = 7 for large scenes, including the user-selected anchor in both cases. The
keyframe count is fixed for each scene. Candidate views are screened using CLIP ViT-H/14 similarity to a description of the
source subject carrying the edit. A view is retained when its similarity is at least 0.15 and at least 0.74 times the source anchor
similarity. For example, adding glasses uses a description of the person’s head. We also project the reconstructed subject points
into each candidate camera and require at least 90% of these points to lie in front of the camera and inside the image. We exclude
views where the projected subject occupies too little or too much of the image, or lies too far from the image center. Duplicate
camera poses are removed.
For FPS (Eq. 3) and closed-loop ordering (Eq. 4), we use d(i, j) = ∥ci −cj∥2 + arccos(f ⊤
i fj), where ci is the camera center
in the scene coordinates used for reconstruction and fi is the unit viewing direction. The angular term is measured in radians,
and both terms have unit weight. Kochanek–Bartels interpolation uses tension 0.5, with 61 samples per segment and a shared
endpoint between consecutive segments.
Before editing, we score every unedited rendered frame with the same source-subject description. A clip is accepted when its
minimum frame similarity is at least 0.74 times that of the original anchor render; the anchor score must also reach 0.15. Both
propagation branches use this same anchor reference, and each stops at its first rejected clip. Within each accepted branch, the
edited last frame of one clip conditions the next.
The edit mask Mk,f(u) in Eq. 7 compares attention from the source-state branch and the target-conditioned edited-state branch,
restricted to the first and current frames. We average the normalized differences across the 40 DiT blocks and threshold at 0.25.
The mask is recomputed at each denoising step and upsampled to assign the foreground and background regularization weights.
The edited anchor is restored directly.

E. Limitations and Failure Cases
E.1. Failure-Mode and Scope Analysis
Fig. 10 analyzes inaccurate segmentation masks, thin structures, severe occlusion, and imperfect 2D anchor edits. Severe oc-
clusion and ambiguous prompts can make anchor selection and semantic propagation less reliable, whereas thin structures and
inaccurate masks stress precise localization. The removal example also shows that extra mask regions need not cause visible
changes outside the intended edit.
Fig. 10. Failure-mode and scope analysis: occlusion, imperfect anchors, thin structures, and segmentation.
The edited anchor determines the visual content transferred to the 3D scene. Background artifacts and incomplete object
removal in the anchor can therefore persist after propagation and 3D refinement. A clean, target-visible anchor reduces these
errors, and stronger 2D editors can be incorporated without retraining the propagation model. RS-GS preserves the attributes of
unselected Gaussians, although their rendered visibility can change as the edited target changes shape.
E.2. Large-Area Appearance Edits
Large-area texture or style edits leave little unchanged context to anchor propagation. FAVE uses source-consistency regulariza-
tion to preserve scene layout, but transferring a new appearance across substantial visibility changes becomes harder when most
visible surfaces are edited. The target appearance can then drift or fade toward the source appearance in later views. Fig. 11
illustrates this dependence on scene context.
A wider rendering field of view introduces more unedited context and can help sustain the target appearance along the tra-
jectory. This reduces the target’s image resolution, however, making fine-grained appearance transfer less accurate after 3D
refinement. Balancing contextual support with target resolution remains a direction for improving large-area editing.
E.3. Editing Time
Computational efficiency remains a limitation. Our Wan2.1-I2V-14B backbone requires repeated denoiser evaluations over dense
multi-view clips, making interactive editing costly. Sparse attention [59–61], feature caching [62, 63], model distillation [64], and
learned few-step solvers [65] offer promising ways to reduce this cost. Integrating these techniques while preserving reference
fidelity and multi-view consistency is an important direction for future work.
