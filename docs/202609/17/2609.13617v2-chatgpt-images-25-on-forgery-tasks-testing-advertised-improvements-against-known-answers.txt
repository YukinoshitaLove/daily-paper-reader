Abstract. OpenAI released ChatGPT Images 2.5 on 8 September 2026, advertising more precise local edits, better consistency across edits, more faithful reference products and sharper detail. We evaluate these claims on four forgery tasks with answers fixed in advance: receipt-field alteration, repeated editing, product placement and small-print rendering. GPT-Image-2 provides same-week baselines at a cheaper and a more expensive tier. A limited improvement appears in receipt editing. After alignment, OCR detects changes to surrounding text in 31.7% of Flare outputs, against 44.2% for the cheaper baseline. This gain is concentrated on CORD receipts and sensitive to shifts of a pixel or less; the forged value itself is no more often correct. Repeated editing and fine print show no measurable gain. Product codes become more legible mainly because Images 2.5 draws the product larger. Defence outcomes change little: localisation remains weak for both generations. A detector that flags 68.6% of controlled benchmark images flags only 35.9% of images posted online. Advertised improvements therefore transfer unevenly to the tested forgery capabilities, while substantial detection limitations remain. 

## 1 Introduction 

Image editing supports both creative work and document or marketplace fraud. Entrust’s 2025 Identity Fraud Report 

records a 244% year-over-year rise in digital document forg-eries, which now outnumber physical counterfeits [5]. Amodel release that promises better editing therefore raises two practical questions: does it make forgery easier, and do existing defences still work? Our earlier AIForge-Doc v2 study evaluated 3,066 receipt and form forgeries made with GPT-Image-2. In a side-by-side comparison, human inspectors identified the forgery 0.501 of the time, at chance level. Three automated detectors achieved AUCs of 0.532–0.599. Two of those detectors score 0.962 and 0.852 on conventional tampering [32]. These findings establish a detection problem, but leave the generator’s editing capability largely unmeasured. 

The evaluation gap. Public leaderboards rank models by pairwise human preferences [1, 3]. System cards report unsafe outputs and refusals [16]. Neither establishes whether a forged receipt field is correct and the surrounding text preserved. The Images 2.5 system card includes answer-graded biological and cyber tests, but no equivalent forgery tests. Forensic benchmarks address a complementary question: whether an output is detected. We evaluate both the requested edit and the response of a defence. Contemporaneous comparisons also matter. Our earlier study used one model at one quality tier, leaving model, tier and benchmark effects unresolved. Here we compare gen-erations, tiers and evaluation samples. These comparisons describe differences; they do not identify their causes. 

From release claims to testable tasks. On 8 September 2026, OpenAI released two Images 2.5 API models, Flare and Sunburst [17]. The release promises precise local editing and better preservation of earlier edits. It also promises greater reference fidelity and sharper detail, with new xhigh and 

max quality tiers and 4K output. Flare is advertised as faster. We map the four editing claims to receipt-field alteration, repeated editing, product placement in a fake listing and small-print rendering. Each task has a correct answer fixed in ad-vance (Figure 1). Both new models are compared with GPT-Image-2, re-run in the same week at a cheaper and a more expensive tier (§3.1).

RQ3. Defence. How do refusal, detection and localisation vary with generator and quality tier (§5.1–§5.4)?

## 2 Related Work 

Document tampering and AIForge-Doc. Forensic corpora pair tampered images with localisation masks. DocTamper [23] provides 170,000 document images and a document-specific detector. OSTF [24] extends evaluation to scene text altered by eight editing models, including unseen forgery types. AIForge-Doc v1 [33] contains 4,061 diffusion-generated financial and form forgeries with pixel-precise masks. Version 2 retains the specifications but uses GPT-Image-2; human two-alternative forced-choice accuracy falls to 0.501, at chance level [32]. Version 3 retains the catalogue and source corpora while adding the newer generator. The present evaluation mea-sures requested-edit correctness and collateral preservation alongside forensic detection. 

Preference and ground-truth evaluation. Blind-vote are-nas aggregate human preferences into Elo-style ratings [1, 3]. These ratings are prominent evidence for Images 2.5, but do not establish whether a forged value is correct. GEdit-Bench v2 [11] trains a visual-consistency judge on pairwise human preferences. ImgEdit-Bench [34] uses GPT-4o to rate ChatGPT Images 2.5 on Forgery Tasks 3

instruction adherence, editing quality and preservation on a five-point scale. Such model-based evaluations can favour a judge’s own generations [20]. Text-rendering benchmarks provide closer methodological precedents. MARIO-Eval [2] and AnyText [31] use OCR to compare generated text with specified content; AnyText also supports editing. Our contribution combines field cor-rectness, collateral preservation and defence measurements on forgery tasks. DINO and CLIP-I reference metrics [28] instead measure embedding similarity and do not verify small label text. 

Public forensic tools. TruFor [7] combines RGB evidence with a learned noise fingerprint to produce localisation and integrity scores. DocTamper is the public document localiser used here. Community Forensics [21] is a generator-agnostic detector trained on images from thousands of generators. As in our earlier study, we omit reconstruction-based AEROBLADE [26], which is unsuitable for these local edits because most of each image remains untouched. 

## 3 Evaluation Design 

We define the model comparisons, scoring instruments and four forgery tasks below. Several primary contrasts and the statistical framework were selected after results were known. Section 3.5 records that timeline. 

3.1 Models and baselines 

We compare Flare and Sunburst ( gpt-image-2.5-flare 

and gpt-image-2.5-sunburst ) with GPT-Image-2. AIForge-Doc v1 and v2 are earlier dataset releases [32, 33]; v3 is the release used here. For v3, we re-ran GPT-Image-2 in the same week as Images 2.5 (September 2026). 

Price-bracketing comparisons. The receipt, repeated-editing and reference experiments (E1–E3) run Images 2.5 at 

medium . OpenAI’s quality parameter determines price. No GPT-Image-2 tier has the same price as this setting: low 

is the nearest cheaper tier, while medium costs four times as much. We use both as baselines. The small-print experiment (E4) and paired timings instead use equal-cost comparisons. E4 pairs GPT-Image-2 medium with Images 2.5 high , and GPT-Image-2 high with Images 2.5 max (Appendix A). E1 included both baselines from the start. For E2 and E3, we added low after the main runs under an addendum fixed before generating its images. Stimuli, prompts, masks, edit orders, seeds and scoring rules were unchanged. The added images were generated about 11 hours later than their comparators (§3.5). Both baseline contrasts enter each claim’s test family. All calls used one OpenAI API account. The ledger records successes, refusals and failures; Appendix B documents routing and execution. 

3.2 What we measure: capability, review check and defence 

Each task has a correct result fixed before generation (Figure 1). The figures and tables distinguish three measure-ment layers: 

L1. Capability: whether the requested edit is correct. 

L2. Review check: whether automatic checks find changes to other text or previous edits. This is a preservation proxy, not a measurement of human acceptance. 

L3. Defence: whether the request is refused, the output de-tected, or the edited region localised by a public tool. We also measure Flare’s advertised speed advantage (§5.3). 

Scoring instruments. Capability and preservation checks compare outputs with known answers using OCR (easyocr [10]), geometric alignment, colour distance and object detec-tion. Alignment uses SIFT [12] or ORB [27] with RANSAC [6]; colour is measured by ∆E00 [29]; object checks combine OWLv2 [13] with a colour test. CLIP [25] and DINOv2 [19] embedding similarity cannot establish whether label text is correct, so they do not determine the capability verdicts. Defence tests use DocTamper [23], TruFor [7] and Com-munity Forensics [21]. No OpenAI model judges an OpenAI output [20]. In one E2 variant, gpt-5.4-nano relays in-structions to the image model but performs no scoring. 

Validation limits. An AI coding agent (Claude) spot-checked earlier scorer versions on blind sheets. These checks were not human ratings, and their agreement counts carry no inferential weight. The final registered E1 token set has no independent validation. Two calibration quantities limit interpretation. The E1 false-change rate comes from rereading unedited source replicas. It is a reference rate, not a lower bound or an estimate of measurement error on generated outputs. The OCR ceiling is the smallest glyph size read reliably on clean renders: 9 px cap height in E4, and an output-specific threshold in E3. Misses below that threshold cannot be attributed to the generator. Appendix D.5 reports a separate pixel-level measure of change outside the edit. 

3.3 Four forgery tasks E1: Change one receipt field. We apply AIForge-Doc speci-fications to CORD [22] and WildReceipt [30], using identical prompts and alpha masks across models. Crops must sat-isfy the API’s aspect-ratio constraints (Appendix D.2). The analysis uses the 199 specifications rendered by every model. Capability is exact OCR agreement with the forged value, restricted to source fields that OCR can read. The review check detects changes to other text in the crop. Before reread-ing, we align each output to its source: median displacement is 2.53 px for GPT-Image-2 medium and 0.87–1.08 px for Images 2.5. Even aligned unedited copies produce an OCR false-change rate of 12.1%. Shift-stratified results assess sen-sitivity. Defence tests use DocTamper for localisation, TruFor ChatGPT Images 2.5 on Forgery Tasks 4

for the paste-back seam control, and Community Forensics for whole-image detection (Appendix D). 

E2: Edit the same image repeatedly. The receipt exper-iment (E2a) applies 4 edits to each of 60 receipts: 30 from CORD and 30 from WildReceipt. One field is edited per turn in a seeded order, with each turn sent as a fresh request. We check whether the target is correct at its own turn and remains intact at the last turn, and whether never-edited fields remain unchanged. A joint endpoint, added post hoc as co-primary, counts all requested edits that both succeed and survive. The photo experiment (E2b) uses 23 photographs without people, forming 39 edit sequences. Each turn adds either a short code, checked by OCR, or a coloured object, checked by an object detector. Turns are sent as fresh requests or within one chat conversation where a text model relays instructions. We measure survival of successful edits at the last turn and preservation of their requested position, requiring box overlap of at least 0.3 (Appendix E). 

E3: Place a reference product in a fake listing. Each of 119 synthetic references carries a nonsense brand, price, small SKU code and two-colour logo. Programmatic ref-erences avoid giving any generator a home-field advantage. Each model places the product in a front-on hero view and an in-scene view among other items. We rectify the label and measure exact brand, SKU and price OCR, logo colour distance and logo shape overlap. Larger products can make the same label easier to read. We therefore measure label area and relative glyph size, with exploratory comparisons at matched glyph height and prod-uct size (Appendix F). The documented input_fidelity 

parameter is rejected by every tested model, so all edits use default fidelity (Appendix A). 

E4: Render small print. Each of 40 prompts requests 6 exact random codes at decreasing sizes. We test every tier of each model and 4K output. The primary outcome is the per-prompt fraction of lines read exactly above the OCR ceiling. We report excluded prompts with no eligible line in either configuration. Intention-to-treat rates and matched-glyph-height contrasts are exploratory. Latency is measured separately on prompt-paired syn-chronous runs of E1 edits and a photoreal core generated at every tier (Appendices G and J). 

3.4 Statistical analysis Tests and intervals. Every contrast is paired: Images 2.5 minus a baseline on the same units. Clusters are specifications in E1, receipts in E2a, source photos in E2b, references in E3, and prompts in E4 and latency tests. Two-sided cluster sign-flip tests use 20,000 permutations. Unadjusted percentile bootstrap 95% confidence intervals use 2,000 cluster resam-ples. Exact McNemar tests provide an E1 sensitivity check. We report minimum detectable differences where computable, and equivalence bounds for defence comparisons interpreted as showing little change (Appendix C). 

Multiple comparisons. Holm’s correction [8] is applied within each advertised claim, across layers, models and base-lines. The four families are: • Local editing: E1 target correctness and surrounding-text changes (8 tests). • Multi-turn consistency: E2a success, survival, never-edited fields and the joint endpoint by corpus, plus E2b survival and position (40 tests). • Reference fidelity: E3 in-scene SKU exactness (4 tests). • Sharper detail: E4 exact lines at the two highest matched settings (4 tests). A stricter correction across all 56 primary tests provides a sensitivity check. Other analyses are exploratory and do not determine primary verdicts. E3 programmatic and rendered references and E4 sensitivities have their own Holm correc-tions in 24 families; other exploratory results are unadjusted. 

3.5 Analysis plan and decision timeline 

The E1–E4 metrics were documented on 10 September 2026, before E1 scoring and E2–E4 generation. Primary contrasts in E1, E3 and E4 were selected after their results were available. The statistical framework was also adopted after all results were known. Both decisions are therefore post hoc. E2 rules were fixed before full-run E2a estimates existed, but after a pilot had shown Flare’s results. Chat-mode E2b had already been scored in full, so its contrasts remain ex-ploratory. Appendix C.1 records the complete sequence and source documents. 

## 4 Do the Advertised Improvements Hold?

## 5 Detection, Localisation and Refusal 

We evaluate refusal, localisation and whole-image detection, with contemporaneous GPT-Image-2 baselines (Figure 3, Ta-ble 5). These comparisons show how outcomes differ between the tested configurations; they do not identify the causes of those differences. 

5.1 Refusal remains rare in both generations 

Images 2.5 refused 7 of 2,000 masked receipt edits on first pass: 0.35% [0.17%, 0.72%]. With the same requests, prompts and alpha masks, GPT-Image-2 refused 1 of 400 (0.25%). Both rates are far below the ∼10% reported for GPT-Image-2 in our earlier study [32]. The low contemporaneous rates for both generations do not support attributing the historical decline to Images 2.5 alone. On the 200 specifications sent to every model, refusal counts are 1 for Flare, 0 for Sunburst, 1 for GPT-Image-2 low and 0 for medium . These sparse events do not distinguish the models: every p = 1. A detectable difference would require at least 6 specifications refused by only one model. Changes in request format confound the historical comparison, alongside the change in date (Appendix H). 

5.2 Localisation remains weak 

DocTamper’s mean per-image AUC is 0.588 [0.576, 0.600] for Flare and 0.599 [0.586, 0.612] for Sunburst. The GPT-Image-2 AUCs are 0.613 at low and 0.615 at medium . medium , with a paired AUC difference of −0.025 [−0.048 ,

−0.002 ]. All model-to-model differences are within ±0.044 AUC; Appendix I.3 gives the margins and tests. These results apply to a crop-edit-and-paste-back pipeline. ## 6 Discussion 

6.1 Capability gains and defence outcomes differ 

The four release claims translate unevenly to the tested forgery tasks. The clearest legibility gain, in product codes, accom-panies larger product placement; it does not establish more faithful copying. Receipt editing provides a different result: automatic checks detect fewer changes to surrounding text af-ter alignment. That preservation endpoint is directly relevant to comparison with an original, whereas public preference votes do not measure it. The receipt result remains narrow. It is concentrated on CORD, depends on registration and token selection, and lacks independent validation of the final endpoint. It therefore sup-ports an instrument-defined preservation gain, not a claim that human reviewers would accept the forgeries. Defence limitations persist across both same-week genera-tions. Refusals are rare, and DocTamper localisation is weak despite a small Flare-versus-medium difference. Community Forensics shows no clear trend across 1024-px quality tiers; 4K cells score lower for both Images 2.5 models, but equiv-alence across settings is not established. Historical refusal differences also confound request format, routing and sam-pling. Separate capability and defence measurements cannot establish whether better editing causes a change in detectabil-ity. The detector flags fewer posted images than controlled im-ages at the evaluated threshold. Content, format and prove-nance all differ between these samples, so the gap cannot be attributed to any one factor. This result concerns whole-image detection and does not establish transfer of document-localisation performance. 

6.2 Practical implications Fraud and trust-and-safety teams. Provider refusal did not reliably block the tested receipt edits. Forensic maps also re-quire care: DocTamper weakly separates edited pixels, while TruFor can respond to a pasted-crop boundary. Independently verifiable information—issuer records, line-item totals and earlier submissions— may complement image-based checks. Our outputs still contain incorrect values, misplaced edits and lost earlier edits, but we did not test the operational perfor-mance of those checks. Detection should be validated on representative traffic, and a higher generation tier should not be assumed to evade the detector. 

Model vendors. Release evaluation should include tasks with predetermined answers alongside preference ratings. Comparisons should use matched inputs and report both qual-ity settings and their costs. Refusal rates need named misuse categories and contemporaneous controls. Provenance meta-data should identify the generator: the three tested models emit the same C2PA generator string, preventing that field from distinguishing Images 2.5 from GPT-Image-2 (Appendix J). ## 7 Conclusion 

We tested four improvements advertised for ChatGPT Im-ages 2.5 on forgery tasks with predetermined answers. The evidence supports one qualified gain: after alignment, Flare and Sunburst disturb less surrounding receipt text than the GPT-Image-2 baselines. This result is concentrated on CORD and lacks independent validation of the final preservation end-point. The remaining tasks establish no general improvement. Product-code legibility increases with larger placement, with-out a replicated size-controlled fidelity gain. Flare retains fewer earlier edits on CORD receipts, while the photo task scarcely separates the models. Fine print shows no measurable gain above the OCR threshold; performance below it remains unresolved. Flare is 2.75 × faster than GPT-Image-2 medium 

for a receipt edit, but only 1.19 × faster than low .Both generations rarely refuse the tested requests, and lo-calisation remains weak. Detection equivalence across quality tiers is not established. At the evaluated threshold, Commu-nity Forensics flags 35.9% of self-reported posted images, versus a 68.6% mean across controlled cells. These findings support task-specific evaluation of release claims and valida-tion of defences on representative traffic, with independent factual checks where available.

## B Benchmark Design and Call Log 

This appendix describes how requests were issued, retried and logged. 

Refusal bookkeeping. We record a request as refused 

when three consecutive attempts return HTTP 400 with the same provider error code. This follows the round-v2 rule for separating content rejection from outages [32]. Tran-sient failures are error rows. The 10 invalid_value 

smoke rows concern unsupported GPT-Image-2 tiers; they are content-neutral, unbilled and excluded from refusal statistics. First-pass refusal uses the request’s earliest row; persistent refusal uses its latest row. We order rows by created_at ,which records row construction rather than call completion (2,204 pairs are out of order). The row_id hashes request identity, excluding prompt text and mask bytes. Re-runs skip requests already recorded as ok . Refusals remain explicit ledger rows rather than missing observations. 

Data flow. Cell builders emit jobs to a common retry, refusal and budget runner. Each call produces a ledger row, with a raw PNG for successful outputs. Exporters pass cells to the detector harness using row_id ; checkers join the results to source images and known answers. Downstream stages read only files named in the ledger.

Intervals and paired tests. Unless otherwise stated, intervals are 95% percentile bootstrap intervals from 2,000 resamples. The cluster unit is the specification for E1, receipt for E2a, source photo for E2b, reference for E3 and prompt for E4 (§3.4). Detector AUCs are clustered by specification (2000-resample bootstrap, forged side clustered by spec_id); refusal rates use Wilson 95% intervals. Primary contrasts use cluster sign-flip permutation tests with 20,000 permutations and a minimum attainable p of 

5.0 × 10 −5. The null assumes exchangeability of the two arms within paired clusters, rather than simply equal marginal rates. Paired binary E1 endpoints also use exact McNemar tests. 

Multiple comparisons. D.2 Excluding inputs with mismatched sizes 

We flag an output when its aspect ratio differs from the request by more than 2%. Resizing it directly would distort the pasted region and compromise pixel-level metrics. The flag applies to 98 (4.9%) document rows, which the detector exporter excludes. The exclusion changes the evaluated population. Flag rates are higher on WildReceipt than CORD (8.2% versus 1.6%) and on long rather than monetary fields (7.7% versus 1.7%). The most frequently flagged fields are Telephone_key 

(48) and Store_addr_key (22). Flagged rows are 84% WildReceipt and 16% monetary, compared with 48% and 48% among retained rows. E1 detector and locality estimates thus under-represent pho-tographed receipts with long, wide fields. The OCR analysis retains flagged rows and excludes them only in sensitivity analysis, leaving 192 of 199 specifications. 

D.3 OCR checks and calibration 

OCR (easyocr) reads the target field and other source-crop text lines in each output. Target read (L1) compares the recog-nised target with the forged value, ignoring case, spacing and punctuation. It is scored only when OCR reads the original field correctly: 96 of 199 specifications, including 62% CORD ChatGPT Images 2.5 on Forgery Tasks 16 

and 34% WildReceipt fields. The small eligible WildReceipt subset limits its contribution to L1. 

Any other text changed (L2) records whether a stable token changes or new text appears. We register each output to its source using the tolerant-locality transformation, then re-read at registered coordinates. The primary analysis has 738 stable tokens; the earlier unregistered analysis used 601 and re-read at identical coordinates. Absolute rates depend on the token set, although the paired contrasts are robust to this choice (Appendix D.4). Calibration uses source-crop noise replicas: a resampling round trip, a sub-pixel shift and a high-quality JPEG. A token is eligible if all but one replica reproduce the source reading. The held-out replica estimates the OCR-only false-change rate. Under registration, the rate for any other text is 12.1% (10.0% CORD; 14.1% WildReceipt), compared with 15.6% at identical coordinates. Pairing controls the source specification, not all measure-ment error. The replicas do not match each arm’s shift distri-bution, so differential OCR error can remain. 

AI-agent spot check of the scorer (not a human rater). An AI coding agent (Claude) judged blind sheets of source and output crops without seeing the scorer’s verdict. In the second of two rounds it agreed with the target-read verdict on 36 of 40 items and with the collateral verdict on 30 of 40. In the first round, after which the scorer was revised once, it agreed on 33 of 40 and 32 of 40. Both rounds predate registered re-reading, so no spot check covers the registered token set that is now primary. These counts describe scorer agreement only; no statistic is computed from them.

## E E2: Keep Editing the Same Image 

E2a procedure. Each of 60 receipts (30 CORD, 30 Wild-Receipt) is edited 4 times by each model (GPT-Image-2 at 

medium and, added post hoc, low ), statelessly, one field per turn, with the unmasked instruction “change ‘ o’ to ‘ t’; keep everything else” in a seeded field order shared by all models. Targets keep the original’s character layout and are unique on the receipt; never-edited control fields are tracked too (213 planned fields on CORD, 217 on WildReceipt). After each turn, OCR reads the receipt. An ORB and RANSAC similarity fit locates each field’s line. This task uses neither object detection nor a pixel-drift measure. Fields were pre-selected to be OCR-legible at t0, so the WildReceipt baseline (96%) is close to automatic and says nothing about OCR reliability on photographed receipts. Failure modes are reported outside the Holm family: the value written into the wrong row (CORD: 1% GPT-Image-2 

medium , 6% Flare, 0% Sunburst) and near misses at edit distance one or two (4%, 8%, 10%). No E2a request was refused. 

E2b procedure. Every model starts from the same 40 person-free authentic photographs drawn from 23 source photos (IMD2020 [15] and the Columbia set [9]). One edit per turn, in a seeded order, adds either text (a sign code, a date stamp, a sticky-note number) read by OCR, or an object (a red mug, a blue umbrella, a potted plant) found by OWLv2 and checked by a Lab colour test. Object thresholds τ were set on 276 authentic candidate crops, giving pool false-positive rates of 0.7% (mug, τ = 0.10), 1.4% (umbrella, 0.15) and 1.8% (plant, 0.43). ## J Quality Settings, Cost, Speed and Provenance 

Design. The photoreal core is 100 text-to-image prompts over six content classes (portrait, street, interior, food, product and photographs of documents), written to be photographic rather than stylised. Every prompt runs at 1024 ×1024 on all three models at every tier that model offers, and a 20-prompt sub-grid adds 3840 ×2160 at high . Scored cells hold 30–100 images at 1024 px and 20 at 4K. Tokens are a price proxy, not a compute measure (§3.1). Table 14 is the grid behind §5.4. 

Detection across quality tiers. Community Forensics’ AUC against 544 real photographs stays within 0.880–0.935 across 13 1024-px cells spanning a 36-fold token range (Table 14). Part of every AUC may be format, since the real images are JPEG and TIFF. No trend test was run, and flatness is not established: equivalence at ±0.03 fails for every model, and the smallest margin supported across all 1024-px cells is 0.080. At 4K, billed at about 1.9 × the 1024 high price, AUC is lower for Flare (0.823 against 0.888) and Sunburst (0.775 against 0.917), on unmatched prompts. 

Attribution probe (exploratory). Images 2.5 ships as two models under one brand, so we asked whether pixels separate the three generators. A multinomial logistic regression on 128-d radial power-spectrum features, with standardised inputs under 5-fold stratified cross-validation on a balanced three-class split with prompt and tier held fixed, reaches 0.342 [0.267, 0.418] at medium (n = 152 ; chance 1/3), 0.395 [0.317, 0.472] at low , 0.474 [0.394, 0.553] at high and 0.595 [0.447, 0.744] in the 4K cells ( n = 42 ). At medium , these spectral features do not reliably distinguish the generators; a probe on detector embeddings would be the stronger test. 

Content credentials do not name the generation. The in-spected raw PNGs from all three models contain C2PA content credentials [4]. The inspected manifests declare the genera-tor as gpt-image version 2.0 for GPT-Image-2, Flare and Sunburst alike; that string is neither a model name nor our round v2. ## M Limitations 

Scope. The study covers four forgery tasks, two Images 2.5 models and two GPT-Image-2 quality baselines. Localisation refers to DocTamper on masked receipt edits; whole-image detection uses Community Forensics. No test involves faces. Results apply to one account and one week of API calls, with one output per input because the API cannot be seeded. Parts of the analysis were specified after results were known. The principal limitations concern automatic measurement, crop-and-paste seams, selected or synthetic inputs, and the analysis timeline. No human rated the outputs. Claude’s scorer spot checks do not cover the registered E1 OCR metric. Photographed products remain untested, and the main E1 and E2a findings rely largely on CORD because OCR coverage on WildReceipt is limited. ChatGPT Images 2.5 on Forgery Tasks 26 

A nonsignificant result does not establish equality. This applies to refusal rates, detection across quality tiers and the fine-print comparison. E4 measures only lines above the OCR ceiling; those lines differ by arm and have high but im-perfect exact-read rates. One matched-height contrast does separate the models, so the sharper-detail claim remains unset-tled. Appendix G gives the glyph-height analysis; the detailed limitations below specify the remaining boundaries. 

Historical refusal comparison. Rounds v2 and v3 differ in generator, route, mask, prompt and corpus set. The contem-poraneous control holds the round-v3 procedure fixed, but it cannot separate changes in request format from changes in serving or moderation since round v2. Both may contribute to the lower refusal rates. A distinguishing condition, Images 2.5 with round v2’s drawn-box format, was not run. The control has 400 attempts versus 2,000 for Images 2.5, and its interval is the widest. The observed rates are of the same order; the experiment does not establish equality (Ta-ble 12). 

Reduced judge panel and preliminary image-level AUC. 

Round v2’s grid (non-expert humans, TruFor, DocTamper and the generator as self-judge) was descoped for round v3. A preliminary image-level DocTamper number exists (0.588 [0.544, 0.633] Flare, 0.610 [0.567, 0.652] Sunburst, against 200 authentic Images 2.5 document pages), but it aggregates each pixel map by the 99th percentile of its pixel scores rather than by round v2’s rule, so it does not belong beside round v2’s 0.585. Whether round-v3 forgeries are detected more or less often than round v2’s is open. 

Detailed limitations. 

• Automatic measurement. AI-agent spot checks do not cover the registered E1 token set. OCR, OWLv2 and homography checks have their own errors. OCR frequently fails below 9 px, limiting E4’s comparison of small text. E1 uses a calibrated false-change rate; E3 and E4 use size-dependent OCR ceilings. • Selection. OCR reads only 34% E1 WildReceipt source fields, and E2a pre-selects fields legible at t0. The main E1 and E2a findings therefore rely on CORD. E1’s geometry filters apply before and after generation and change the evaluated population (Appendix D.2). • References. E3 uses programmatic packshots and an ex-ploratory rendered set; photographed products and people are untested. • Analysis timing. E1, E3 and E4 primary contrasts and the statistical framework were selected after results were known. E1 images predate its design document. E2 rules preceded full-run E2a estimates but followed pilot results and com-pleted conversational E2b scoring. The later low -arm ad-dendum preceded its own data (Appendix C.1). • Post hoc additions. The GPT-Image-2 low controls for E2a, E2b and E3 followed the main results, as did the sen-sitivity analyses listed in Appendix C.1. E2b’s umbrella threshold was revised after a pilot miss; only exploratory conversational decisions depend on it (Appendix E). • Price and timing. No GPT-Image-2 tier bills 439 tokens. Its 

low tier (196) costs less and medium (1,756) four times more. Low-arm E2a, E2b and E3 runs followed the compar-ison arms by about 11 hours. • Execution route. E2–E4 mix batch and synchronous calls, unevenly by model in stateless E2b (Table 3). Latency uses synchronous calls only and does not measure concurrent throughput. E1 arms were not interleaved. The API results do not measure the ChatGPT interface; conversational E2b also uses gpt-5.4-nano , which can rewrite placement instructions. • Equivalence. DocTamper localisation is not equivalent to chance within ±0.05 for any model. Community Forensics equivalence across tiers fails at ±0.03. • TruFor coverage. Only pages up to about 2,772 px on the long edge were scored. Missing shares are 7.4%–8.3% per configuration, so results are conditional on page size. • Repeated re-editing. The laundering cell has no control arms and is scored at only four turn positions. • Format and transfer. The photoreal real pool uses JPEG and TIFF against PNG generations, potentially contributing to AUCs of 0.880–0.935. Posted images are self-attributed; their flag rate uses one calibrated threshold, and PNG/JPEG groups are not content-matched. • Fine-print interpretation. The above-ceiling band has high but imperfect OCR accuracy and different eligible lines by arm. Flare max separates from GPT-Image-2 high at matched glyph height ( +13 % [ +4 %, +23 %]). The sharper-detail claim remains unsettled (Appendix G).
