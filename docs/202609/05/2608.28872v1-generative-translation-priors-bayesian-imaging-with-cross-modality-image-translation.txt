Abstract
The ability to leverage images from co-available modalities to inform target-domain
reconstruction is highly desirable in imaging algorithms. In this work, we introduce
Generative Translation Priors (GTP)—a Bayesian framework that transforms diffusion-based
image-to-image translation models into cross-modality image priors for ill-posed imaging
inverse problems. GTP incorporates target-domain measurements through likelihood
guidance, steering the translation process toward the desired posterior distribution. The
framework is grounded in a theoretical analysis of the resulting posterior dynamics,
which reveals an intrinsic bias introduced by likelihood guidance. We further characterize
this bias and derive a ground-truth-free formulation for its estimation, enabling it to serve
as a practical metric for assessing posterior sampling quality. Building on this analysis,
we derive two discretized GTP algorithms based on gradient and proximal likelihood
guidance, respectively.
We validate GTP on computed tomography reconstruction
with magnetic resonance side information, and on positron emission tomography
reconstruction with computed tomography side information. Experiments demonstrate
that GTP effectively incorporates complementary cross-modality information and achieves
high-fidelity reconstruction even under severely undersampled measurements.

Introduction
Modern imaging procedures often involve multiple modalities that capture complementary aspects
of the same object. For example, a patient may undergo both magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)
and computed tomography (CT) scans to visualize different organs [1], while in scientific imaging,
X-ray fluorescence provides elemental composition maps complementary to X-ray ptychography [2].
Exploiting such inter-modal structure for image reconstruction is therefore a desired feature in imaging
algorithms. However, realizing its full potential demands three key ingredients: (i) an expressive model
of inter-modal dependencies capable of capturing complex relationships; (ii) sufficient generality to
apply across diverse modality pairs without customized redesign; and (iii) a principled framework
that integrates such dependencies into the reconstruction process in a theoretically rigorous manner.
Despite growing interest in this direction [3], existing cross-modality image reconstruction methods often
face a trade-off in satisfying all these criteria. The objective of this work is to address all three by
employing generative image translation models as a unified interface for inter-modal dependencies, and
formalizing them as expressive image priors within a rigorous Bayesian framework.
The aim of image reconstruction, or imaging inverse problems, is to recover an unknown image
x ∈Rn from incomplete and noisy measurements y ∈Rm. Such problems are inherently ill-posed,
meaning that the measurements cannot losslessly specify the image. The Bayesian framework addresses

As the expressivity of the image prior directly determines inference quality, reconstruction methods
have evolved from hand-crafted priors, such as those based on sparsity and total variation [4,5], to
learning-based priors parameterized by deep neural networks, such as image denoising and artifact-
removal networks [6–11]. Recent works have further extended this progression through diffusion
models [12,13], enabling generative modeling of more complex image distributions and facilitating
posterior sampling for reconstruction [14–19]. Despite these advances, existing Bayesian reconstruction
methods are formulated from a single-modality perspective, with cross-modality scenarios largely left
out of consideration.
Exploiting dependencies between co-available modality images provides an alternative paradigm
for constructing image priors. Early efforts focused on joint reconstruction for multi-modality imaging
systems [20], where shared information across modalities is exploited through structure-promoting
regularizers [21]. Despite their effectiveness, these dependency models are often hand-crafted or
tailored to specific modality pairs. Advances in image translation models have demonstrated that deep
networks can learn complex mappings between imaging modalities, providing a flexible and general
mechanism for modeling inter-modal dependencies. Prior studies have investigated supervised
end-to-end image translation [22–25], adversarial learning approaches for unpaired training [26–28],
and their applications across various imaging problems [29–32]. Of particular interest, recent works
have formulated image translation within generalized diffusion models [33–36], interpreting translation
as a probabilistic transport between image distributions and achieving state-of-the-art synthesis
quality. These advances suggest that such translation models could serve as expressive cross-modality
image priors. However, a principled Bayesian formulation for integrating such models into image
reconstruction remains lacking.
In this paper, we bridge this gap by proposing Generative Translation Priors (GTP), a Bayesian
framework that integrates diffusion-based image translation with posterior sampling for cross-modality

Specifically, the key contributions of this work are as follows:
• We characterize how the dynamics of a prior translation diffusion process, governed by a stochastic

reveals an intrinsic bias induced and controlled by the likelihood injection.
Notably, this

posterior sampling accuracy. We further note that our analysis naturally extends to the single-
modality setting, where the diffusion process evolves from a Gaussian distribution toward the
image distribution.
• We develop two GTP algorithms, termed Grad-GTP and Prox-GTP, which impose measurement
likelihood through gradient- and proximal-based updates, respectively. The two algorithms
also correspond to the forward and backward Euler discretizations of the likelihood-adapted
GTP process. We further derive the corresponding ground-truth-free bias estimators for both
methods, and numerical results demonstrate a strong correlation between the estimated bias
and posterior sampling accuracy.
• We validate the proposed GTP methods on three cross-modality reconstruction tasks: sparse-

reconstruction with CT guidance.
In the PET-CT experiment, we consider the shot noise
corresponding to the Poisson likelihood. Experimental results demonstrate that GTP algorithms
achieve high-quality reconstruction across diverse inverse problems.

single-modality setting. We then extend the discussion to image translation, cross-modality imaging,
and related methods.

Diffusion Models as Learned Image Priors
Diffusion models (DMs) [12, 13] have recently emerged as a transformative paradigm for modeling
complex image distributions. Their key idea is to learn the reverse dynamics of a diffusion process
that gradually maps a clean image x0 ∼p(x) to Gaussian noise xT ∼N(0, I). The reverse process
can be modeled by a continuous-time SDE

time-dependent diffusion coefficient, and Bt ∈Rn denotes Brownian motion. The key quantity in (5)
is the score function ∇log pt(xt), which computes the log-density gradient of the marginal distribution
associated with the intermediate state xt ∼pt(x). Since the true score is generally intractable, DMs
learn a neural network approximation Sθ(x, t) ≈∇log pt(x) using score matching techniques [46,47].
The availability of a learned score approximation has motivated incorporating diffusion models
as image priors in inverse problems. One popular approach is to adapt (5) towards the posterior
distribution by invoking Bayes’ rule to write the score of the time-dependent posterior πt as
∇log πt(xt|y) = ∇log ℓt(y|xt) + ∇log pt(xt).
This decomposition allows a pre-trained DM to provide the prior score, while the measurement
model enters through the likelihood score ∇log ℓt(y|xt). However, computing the time-dependent
likelihood score is generally intractable [14]. To address this challenge, existing approaches have
pursued two complementary directions. One line of work derives approximations to ∇log ℓt(y|xt)

2.3
Image Translation via Generalized Diffusion
Image-to-image translation aims to transform an image from one domain into a corresponding image in
another domain while preserving relevant underlying content. Existing diffusion-based approaches can
be broadly organized into two paradigms. The first adapts conventional DMs to translation tasks while
retaining the standard data-to-Gaussian diffusion framework; examples include methods that learn
cross-domain mappings through conditional generation or cycle-consistent training objectives [60–63],
and inversion-based approaches that derive a corresponding noise representation from the source
image and decode it within the target domain [64–66]. The second paradigm is represented by
generalized DMs (GDMs), such as diffusion bridges [35, 67] and stochastic interpolants [34, 36], which
formulate translation directly as stochastic transport between arbitrary source and target distributions.
By removing the Gaussian endpoint, these methods provide a more principled framework for modeling
cross-domain transport.
Formally, let x0 ∼psrc(x) and xT ∼ptgt(x) denote the source and target distributions, respectively.
GDMs describe the transport process through the SDE of the form

training paradigm or level of supervision. Additionally, as (6) includes the reverse diffusion process
in (5) as a special case, the GTP framework and its analysis naturally apply to standard DMs.

Related Cross-Modality Imaging Methods
In this section, we briefly review cross-modality imaging methods. As the field has developed from
multiple perspectives, existing approaches often emphasize different aspects of the problem.

Joint Optimization.
Classical cross-modality approaches often arise in multi-modality imaging sys-
tems, where images from different modalities are acquired for the same object and jointly reconstructed.
For two modalities, a joint optimization formulation is given by
(bx1, bx2) = arg min
x1,x2∈Rn {g1(x1) + g2(x2) + h(x1, x2)} ,
where g1 and g2 enforce measurement fidelity for the two modalities, while h(x1, x2) captures their
shared structure and inter-modal dependency. Common choices of h include patch similarity [1],
shared edge constraints [71–73], and co-sparsity [74–76]; see [3, 21] for comprehensive reviews.
Proximal-based algorithms have also been developed for solving such problems, in combination with
alternating minimization [1,71,77], primal-dual schemes [78–80], and Bregman distances [81]. These
joint optimization frameworks have been applied across a broad range of modality pairs, including
MRI-CT [1,71], PET–CT/MRI [72,82,83], and photoacoustic–optical coherence tomography (OCT) [84].
Deep Learning Models.
Another major direction treats cross-modality imaging as a supervised image-
to-image regression problem. These methods learn a direct mapping from a source modality to a target
modality using paired training data, typically by minimizing pixel-wise [85] or perceptual [86] losses
between the network prediction and the target image. Early approaches commonly used convolutional
encoder–decoder architectures, such as U-Net [87], to capture local spatial correspondences across
modalities [22,88]. More recent methods have incorporated attention mechanisms [23,89,90], vision
transformers [24,91,92], and deep unfolding architectures [25] to model long-range dependencies and
richer cross-modality representations. Such methods have been widely studied across different areas,
including low-level computer vision [93] and biomedical imaging [94].
Generative Approaches.
Existing research has primarily approached cross-modality imaging as
an image-to-image translation problem.
Early methods relied on generative adversarial networks
(GANs) [95–97], while more recent studies have explored DMs for biomedical [98,99] and scientific
imaging applications [100]. However, these methods are designed for image synthesis and typically
do not incorporate measurement information. To the best of our knowledge, using cross-modality
generative models as priors for measurement-driven reconstruction remains largely unexplored, and
GTP is developed to address this gap.

Generative Translation Priors
This section introduces the GTP framework. We begin by constructing GTP through likelihood
adaptation of a generic translation diffusion process, which reveals the presence of an intrinsic bias. We
then establish theoretical results characterizing the impact of this bias on posterior sampling accuracy.
Finally, we discretize the continuous-time dynamics to obtain the Grad-GTP and Prox-GTP algorithms
together with their corresponding bias-estimation rules.

3.2
Theoretical Analysis of the Intrinsic Bias
A careful inspection of the bias terms in Proposition 1 reveals that they depend only on quantities
available during sampling and therefore require no access to ground-truth posterior information. While
it is natural to formulate an optimization problem for finding a likelihood evolution that eliminates
the intrinsic bias, the resulting problem amounts to an optimal control problem that is difficult to
solve. Additionally, even evaluating the bias is computationally challenging in high dimensions due
to the presence of the Laplacian term ∆g(x, t). Rather than directly optimizing the bias, GTP takes
a complementary approach: it first derives a computable formulation of the intrinsic bias and then
theoretically quantifies its impact on posterior sampling accuracy. Together, these results establish the
intrinsic bias as a viable criterion for evaluating and selecting likelihood evolution schemes.

practical relevance of our bias theory.

We consider both sparse-view and limited-angle CT (SV/LA-CT) reconstruction under the linear version

We compare GTP against baselines spanning three representative classes: conventional CT recon-
struction methods, including filtered back-projection (FBP) and total variation (TV) regularization [112];

with a standard Gaussian source as unconditional image priors. These models employ the same
architecture, dataset, and training procedure as the translation model used by our GTP algorithms.
All methods are evaluated using peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR) and structural similarity index measure
(SSIM) [115]. For each reconstruction method, relevant hyperparameters are selected by grid search
to maximize the average PSNR over the validation images. We refer to Supplement C.1 and C.2 for
additional implementation and data preprocessing details.

5.2
PET Reconstruction with CT Guidance
Finally, we consider low-dose PET reconstruction with CT guidance, a realistic cross-modality setting
where the two modalities are routinely acquired together and provide complementary functional
and anatomical information. Moreover, PET measurements follow Poisson statistics, allowing us to
evaluate the applicability of GTP beyond Gaussian inverse problems.
We model the PET measurements as y ∼Pois(Ax), where A denotes the PET forward operator.
Attenuation correction is incorporated into A using the corresponding CT image, following the
standard piecewise-linear mapping from CT Hounsfield units (HU) to attenuation coefficients [116,117].
We implement the PET forward and adjoint operators using parallelproj [118] and simulate a
2D scanner with a 12-sided polygonal detector ring, roughly approximating the geometry of the
Siemens Biograph mCT scanner used to acquire the original data. We use the FDG-PET/CT Lesions
dataset [119], which contains 900 paired PET/CT volumes with manual tumor-lesion segmentations,
divided into 800/50/50 volumes for training, validation, and testing. All volumes are cropped to an
axial resolution of 256 × 256; CT images are normalized to [−1, 1], while PET images are preprocessed
following [120] to map most intensities to the same range. We use three randomly selected validation
slices for hyperparameter tuning and evaluate on 50 test slices, including 30 slices containing tumor
lesions and 20 randomly selected slices.
We consider two representative baselines for comparison: ordered subsets expectation maximization
(OSEM) [121] and PET-DDS [120]. The former represents a classical iterative PET reconstruction
method, while the latter provides a diffusion-based comparison that also incorporates anatomical
side information. Unlike GTP, which directly models the generative translation from CT to PET,
PET-DDS uses the anatomical image as a conditioning input to a noise-to-data diffusion process. For
our implementation of PET-DDS, we instantiate this conditional prior using a DDBM conditioned on
the CT image with a standard Gaussian source distribution. We refer to Supplement C.4 for additional
implementation details.

Table 3 summarizes the average PSNR and SSIM for PET reconstruction at

6
Conclusion
In this work, we introduced GTP, a principled framework for using generative translation models as
expressive image priors for cross-modal image reconstruction. GTP achieves this by incorporating a
time-varying measurement likelihood into the sampling process. We presented an analysis of how
this alteration to the particle-level dynamics affects the evolution of the resulting probability density,
revealing that injecting the likelihood in this manner leads to an intrinsic bias in the sampling procedure.
We further show that this bias can be estimated without access to ground-truth information, yielding
a reference-free criterion for assessing the accuracy of the recovered posterior. We introduced two
practical GTP algorithms based on different discretizations of the likelihood-adapted dynamics, Grad-
GTP and Prox-GTP. We validated these proposed algorithms on multiple challenging cross-modality
imaging tasks, including limited-angle and sparse-view CT reconstruction with MRI side information
and low-dose PET reconstruction with CT side information. The empirical results demonstrate the
benefits of the proposed approach over both conventional reconstruction methods and diffusion-based
inverse solvers.

B.2
Modifying the diffusion coefficient
In some cases, applying Algorithms 1 and 2 with the default schedule of the diffusion coefficient σt
can pose numerical challenges. In particular, for some choices of g(xt, t), the default σt may be too
large. This issue could be alleviated by rescaling g(xt, t) by a constant factor, but this may cause
qt(x) = e−g(x,t) pt(x) to no longer arrive at the true posterior. To handle this issue, in some cases we

modify the diffusion coefficient σt, which scales the step size on ∇g. This approach is theoretically
justified by the theory of stochastic interpolants [3], of which our DDBMs are a special case. In
stochastic interpolants, users are permitted to specify an arbitrary schedule for σt and still sample the
same marginal distributions, provided that the drift b(xt, t) is modified accordingly [3, Cor. 10, Cor.
18]. In practice, we keep the unmodified DDBM solver, although it is possible to manually modify
the drift and use other numerical schemes. For a detailed discussion on how to interpret DDBMs in
the framework of stochastic interpolants, we refer to [4].

C
Implementation details for main experiments
C.1
Training GTPs
We realize both the translation models used for our GTP algorithms and the diffusion models used
for baseline methods as denoising diffusion bridge models (DDBMs) [5]. In particular, we use the
variance preserving (VP) configuration of DDBMs. The design of VP-DDBMs is attractive for two
reasons. First, VP-DDBMs are designed to translate samples from a source distribution to samples
from a target distribution exactly between times t = 1 and t = 0. This makes the design choices of
DDBMs naturally compatible with our theoretical framework. Second, DDBMs offer state-of-the-art
performance on both generative image-to-image translation tasks and unconditional image generation
(which can be performed by setting the source distribution to a standard Gaussian).
Architecture and training.
We used the same model architecture and training settings for both the
unconditional diffusion models and the translation models. The backbone is a standard diffusion
U-Net adapted from [6]. We use a model with 64 base channels, channel multipliers of [1, 2, 3, 4, 8],
and attention at the 16 × 16 resolution. The model parameters were optimized using AdamW [7]
with a learning rate of 10−4 with other optimizer hyperparameters set to their default values. The
models were trained using four NVIDIA H200 GPUs for 250,000 steps using a per-GPU batch size of
16. During training, we maintained a per-step exponential moving average of the parameters with a
decay rate of 0.9999. In all cases, the checkpoint used for evaluation is the averaged model from the
end of training.

Data preprocessing and selection for MR→CT.
For the MR-guided CT reconstruction experiments,
we use the 2023 and 2025 SynthRad datasets [8, 9]. The 3D volumes were first preprocessed using
TotalSegmentator [10] to remove image content outside the body, such as the scanner bed. The pixel
values of the CT images in Hounsfield units (HU) were clamped to the range of [−1000, 1000] and
then linearly rescaled to [−1, 1]. The MR images were normalized by clamping their values between
0 and the 99.5th percentile voxel value of each volume, then linearly rescaling the resulting values
to the range [−1, 1]. The volumes were split into training, validation, and testing in an approximate
80/10/10 ratio.
We randomly selected five images from the validation set to use for tuning the
hyperparameters of each algorithm. For testing, we selected 30 slices from the testing dataset. The
testing slices were chosen randomly from the middle 20 slices of random volumes (for reference, the
test volumes contain about 60 slices on average). This was done to ensure that all testing slices were
relatively free of artifacts and had a substantial amount of image content.
Data preprocessing and selection for CT→PET.
Our CT-guided PET reconstruction experiments
employ the FDG-PET/CT Lesions dataset [11]. The registered CT and PET volumes were cropped
to have axial dimensions of 256 × 256. For the CT images, we followed the preprocessing routine
outlined in the previous paragraph; we clamped the voxel values to the range [−1000, 1000] HU and
rescaled the resulting values to the range [−1, 1]. For PET images, preprocessing is more difficult,
since PET images have a very large dynamic range which varies between subjects. To address this
issue, we follow the normalization technique described in [12]. For each slice, we compute a constant
c as the total sum of activity in the image divided by the number of pixels that have any appreciable
activity, that is for a PET image x, we define

We implemented DAPS [20] using our trained model by adapting the implementation from

performance of DAPS was improved by integrating to a final time of t=1e-3 as opposed to the default
of t=1e-4 in the DDBM solver, since the Langevin sampling steps became unstable for very small
values of t. For DAPS we tuned the hyperparameters using only one validation image due to its
relatively long runtime (about 3 minutes per image).

D.5
Additional visualizations
In this section, we provide visual results for additional reconstruction settings as well as for our
generative priors without measurement guidance.
In Figure 4, we provide representative results on 16-view SV-CT. In this case, we see that DDS
obtains a slightly higher PSNR than Prox-GTP. Still, Prox-GTP reconstructs a number of small details
that are omitted by DDS. Additionally, the shapes of certain features, such as the femur on the left
side of the image, are reconstructed more accurately.
In Figure 5, we show results for 60-degree LA-CT. In this setting, the proposed GTP methods
demonstrate much better performance than the diffusion baselines. Every baseline method either
fails to recover significant anatomical features or contains serious artifacts. Grad-GTP and Prox-GTP
produce reconstructions with better anatomical consistency, especially in the missing angular region.
Figure 6 displays a number of uncurated samples from our unconditional CT diffusion model,
which is implemented as a DDBM with a Gaussian source distribution. The samples were generated
using 200 sampling steps. The diffusion model generates images with diverse anatomy, including
chest, abdominal, and pelvic CT images. The generated images also contain many fine details and
small features. These visual results confirm that our diffusion model is a high-quality generative
prior.
Figure 7 shows several examples of images generated by our translation model without measure-
ment guidance. The top row shows the reference MR images, the middle row shows the translation
generated by our trained DDBM, and the bottom row shows the corresponding real CT image. Our
model shows strong translation performance, generating images with features that are consistent
with the MR images, and, in most cases, the ground truth CT image. These results demonstrate that
DDBMs are an effective practical framework for learning translation models.
